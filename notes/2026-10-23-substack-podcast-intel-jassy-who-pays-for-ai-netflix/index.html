THE SIGNAL

  • Jassy says AWS capacity is sold out for 2026 and largely sold out for 2027, apart from on-demand reserves, with a good chunk of 2028 already booked, giving multi-year visibility behind the $220B capex.
  • AWS’s AI business passed a $25B run rate within three years of the generative AI wave, versus about $60M for AWS after its first three years; AWS grew 36.7% last quarter on a $169B run rate.
  • Core non-AI AWS is also growing fast, forcing a split of scarce memory between HBM for accelerators and DRAM for core servers.
  • Jassy calls inference still an order of magnitude too expensive for pervasive enterprise use and expects enterprises’ existing production workloads to become the largest absolute segment of AI usage as costs fall, though he won’t predict how fast; cheaper inference favors Trainium (our read).

01 The capex calculus

Jassy explains Amazon’s investment logic splits into two cash cycles: data centers require commitment roughly two years ahead of monetization but carry ~30-year useful lives, while servers and networking need only months of lead time, break even in about three years, and run five-to-six-year useful lives on mostly five-year deals. This visibility into customer demand, he says, lets Amazon scale aggressively rather than guess — though the pattern creates near-term free-cash-flow headwinds while data centers ramp. He argues Amazon could justify investing considerably beyond $220B given unmet demand, though he expects supply constraints in power, chips, and memory to persist for several years, not just through 2027.

02 Pushing back on data-center backlash

Jassy disputes several claims behind political opposition to data centers, arguing Amazon’s facilities use less water annually than golf courses consume in two days, and that backup generators run only a tiny fraction of the year — around 0.1% — for maintenance. He compares the current buildout in scale to the U.S. buildout of highways and bridges in the 1950s, and attributes rising energy rates mainly to underinvestment in grid infrastructure rather than to data centers themselves. He notes Amazon has paid roughly $3 billion in taxes in St. Joseph County, Indiana, and has committed another $1 billion to local communities over five years through its Built Together program.

03 Chips, Anthropic and OpenAI

Jassy traces Amazon’s chip strategy to the 2015 Annapurna Labs acquisition, originally meant to offload virtualization and networking tasks from EC2 servers, which later produced Graviton CPUs — used by 99% of Amazon’s top 1,000 EC2 customers, with about 40% better price-performance than x86 — and Trainium AI accelerators, offering roughly 30-40% better price-performance than comparable processors. He frames this as a response to depending on a single dominant chip partner (following an earlier dynamic with Intel) whose incentives didn’t prioritize price-performance. Amazon’s investments in Anthropic and OpenAI (roughly $18B and $50B, a host’s estimate) are described as extensions of deep Bedrock partnerships: Anthropic has long built on Trainium, while the OpenAI deal let shared customers finally run OpenAI models on AWS via a new stateful runtime environment Jassy expects to underpin how agents are built going forward.

04 Reorganizing for agentic AI

Jassy points to internal examples — a host recalled his figure that six engineers rebuilt Bedrock’s inference engine in 76 days, work that would have taken 40 people a year, and he describes four or five people taking a new prototype to 80% shippable over a single weekend — as evidence organizations should shrink and de-specialize. He predicts companies will employ fewer people than they do today for current tasks, with smaller cross-functional teams, and near-total rewrites of existing codebases so agents can patch vulnerabilities within minutes rather than through the current multi-step human patch cycle. He singles out cybersecurity, not exotic AI-takeover scenarios, as the more immediate risk companies should focus on.

05 Reinventing products, advice to young people

On Alexa, Jassy says generative AI made it clear the underlying architecture needed a full rebuild, despite internal resistance from teams attached to prior design choices, and says Amazon is applying similar from-scratch reinvention thinking to retail, Prime Video, and Fire TV, even though the need there is less obvious. Asked for advice to an 18-year-old today, he says AI use in education is now unavoidable and universities shouldn’t fight it, but stresses that human judgment, critical thinking, and the work of synthesizing information into a strategy remain irreplaceable skills. He closes by linking Amazon’s sprawl across AI, robotics, autonomous ride-hailing (Zoox) and space (Amazon LEO, targeting 500 million underserved households) to a decentralized, single-threaded leadership structure that lets differentiated teams pursue multiple frontier bets simultaneously.

01 Framing the bubble as supply vs demand

Thompson opens by distinguishing the AI bubble’s supply side (chips, data centers, hyperscaler capex now partly debt-funded) from its demand side (actual revenue AI models generate). He argues the supply side looks less alarming than past bubbles because the buyers — Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta — are already enormously profitable (unlike thinly capitalized 19th-century railroads), and because nearly every AI chip today is in active, premium-priced use, unlike the vast majority of fiber left dark after the dot-com bust or the quarter of U.S. railroad mileage in bankruptcy by the 1890s. The demand side, by contrast, is largely unverifiable: OpenAI and Anthropic are private, so the public relies on leaks — one report pegged OpenAI’s revenue pace near $70B annually, another (FT) at closer to $50B, a large discrepancy that still moved markets. This opacity is why Thompson frames demand, not supply, as the central question, and why he brought on Kharazian, whose firm Ramp processes over $275 billion in annual business spend across 85,000+ companies, including over $10 billion in AI spend.

02 Revenue history and the Claude Code inflection

Kharazian traces AI enterprise revenue from ChatGPT’s consumer launch, through OpenAI’s early enterprise inroads, to Anthropic’s sharp share gains driven by Claude Code, a coding agent he says effectively took over or heavily augmented software engineers’ jobs and became a major revenue driver that spurred further infrastructure investment. More recently, he says competition has intensified — from SpaceX AI, NVIDIA’s own models, and overseas open-weight competitors — squeezing margins for the American labs.

03 Concentration and the slowdown among top spenders

Kharazian’s core data point: the top 1% of businesses on Ramp’s platform account for roughly 80% of their Anthropic and OpenAI spending (a figure Thompson put to him), a concentration he says isn’t unusual by itself (digital advertising sees similar top-percentile dominance) but is notable because that top 1% is highly correlated — concentrated in software, media, finance, and technical services, meaning a downturn could hit many of these early adopters simultaneously. Crucially, spending growth among this top cohort has slowed even as their AI usage keeps climbing, because they’re spreading spend across multiple models and providers, routing simple tasks to cheaper models, and negotiating long-term discounts — behavior Kharazian says is unusual for software spend, which typically locks into single vendors, and which is itself intensifying price competition.

04 Where new growth could come from — and its limits

Kharazian sees two possible growth paths: old-school, large enterprises adopting AI more comprehensively (slow because of caution about customer-facing risk), or new product categories expanding AI’s addressable use beyond coding. He notes coding agents only help the roughly 3% of Americans who are software engineers, while the top occupations in the U.S. — healthcare workers, cashiers, food service — are largely unaugmented by current AI tools. He flags manufacturing automation and self-driving cars as plausible next frontiers, and mentions consumer products (Grok, Meta’s entries) as an unanticipated additional growth vector, though all speculative.

05 ROI evidence and the Fable episode

Ramp research found firms adopting AI with high intensity (sustained use, multiple models, over $50/employee/month) saw 10% headcount growth over two years, while low-intensity adopters showed no measurable difference — suggesting real but narrow productivity gains concentrated in specific sectors, not yet visible in aggregate economic statistics. Kharazian also recounts how Anthropic’s advanced “Fable” model, restricted by the Department of Defense over safety concerns, topped out at just 5-10% of business spend at its peak because a competing OpenAI model offered comparable performance without the compliance friction — illustrating that businesses, not labs, increasingly hold negotiating leverage.

06 Thompson’s synthesis and historical parallel

Thompson likens the current AI revenue trajectory to 2020-21 hypergrowth at Peloton, Zoom, and Netflix, where pulled-forward demand created an S-curve that later flattened and disappointed. He summarizes Kharazian’s thesis in two parts: AI usage is rising but customers are “discount shopping” rather than upgrading to premium models, undermining the labs’ flywheel of reinvestment into ever-better models; and per-capita AI spend remains concentrated among software workers, with no clear sign yet of broad-based spread to other industries. Thompson concludes that, “if you stop the clock right now, AI is not a bubble” given current chip utilization, but says the interview casts doubt on whether spending growth can continue at anything like its post-Claude-Code pace, calling 2027 potentially very different from 2026.

01 Netflix at a crossroads

Thompson and Sharp work through a listener email (from “Rav”) cataloguing Netflix’s bad week: a 6% workforce cut, the firing of both the head of content and the chairman of Netflix Film, a Wall Street Journal report that US subscriber growth has fully stalled, and four price hikes in five years (16% in 2025, 12% in 2026). Sharp frames the problem as two-track: content quality is fixable — Netflix’s recent slate (including a reportedly disastrous Narnia movie) has been quantity-over-quality for most of the decade, unlike earlier hits like The Queen’s Gambit — but the ceiling imposed by US market saturation and the economics of its subscription model is not. Thompson notes Netflix’s one-sided, tightly controlled earnings calls (where a Netflix executive reads curated analyst questions) make the company’s newly candid acknowledgment of engagement problems unusually telling, since low churn despite price hikes is the only positive data point management can point to, and that defense could break at any time.

02 The ad business as last lever

Thompson contends advertising is fundamentally different from subscription pricing because ad rates can be raised almost without limit as targeting improves — citing Meta’s continued growth despite audience saturation as the parallel. This is why Netflix’s ad-revenue miss ($2.7B against a $3B target, attributed by management to slower ad-stack partnerships) worries him more than it should look on the surface, given that the long-run growth story depends almost entirely on ads succeeding. The hosts also see Meta’s Reels and TikTok, not other streamers, as the bigger threat, since they are absorbing the attention Netflix used to dominate.

03 Hollywood consolidation stalls

Thompson revisits his “Hollywood endgame” thesis — that irrational competitors (Skydance, Warner Bros. Discovery) would eventually need to sell content to Netflix, the superior operator — contrasting it with what he calls the rational paths taken by Sony (pure content licensing) and Fox (exiting the studio business to combine with Roku/Tubi around live, ad-supported, sports-heavy streaming). The consolidation hasn’t happened on schedule, leaving Netflix still needing original content it isn’t naturally good at. Sharp reads a Hollywood Reporter story flagging media mogul John Malone’s prediction of an $8–10B SpaceX investment in debt-laden Skydance; the hosts note Elon Musk had already put roughly $1B into Skydance by the time of taping, with Thompson explaining that he has long avoided writing about Musk companies because the reaction from Musk’s most zealous fans makes it like writing about a hot-button political issue. The hosts also note a reader point that Google’s $2B NFL Sunday Ticket deal is under 1% of its 2027 capex — underscoring how small sports rights are next to AI spending.

04 Internet trust is breaking down

A string of this week’s security stories — an NSA-issued emergency warning on a critical PHP vulnerability affecting millions of older sites, an Indian certificate authority found issuing badly malformed “256-bit” keys that were trivially easy to decrypt, and a break-in at a developer platform (Thompson thinks it was Railway) that he says exposed every user’s credentials — leads Thompson into a broader argument that the Internet’s original design, built on globally distributed trust, is now its core vulnerability. He predicts incidents will keep compounding as stolen credentials propagate across services. Separately, Anthropic’s Claude was used to finally explain the origin of NSA elliptic-curve cryptography seeds, debunking years of suspicion that the NSA had planted a backdoor (a reason Bitcoin and Ethereum avoided those standard curves). Thompson argues individuals and companies should now default to trusting AI agents to monitor and patch their own systems continuously, rather than resisting automation out of distrust. He frames the Internet’s future as resembling Neal Stephenson’s Snow Crash: isolated, heavily fortified, zero-trust digital enclaves (describing his own setup as a little fortress with nothing exposed to the outside) connected by an increasingly hostile open web, with group chats as the modern equivalent of the old high-trust Internet.

05 AI branding and alignment philosophy

Responding to a listener’s observation that Anthropic uses one singular, omnipresent “Claude” persona across all products while Grokbot, Dots and Muse offer varying degrees of customization, Thompson argues Anthropic’s approach reflects a philosophy he disagrees with: the company, in his view, is less interested in controlling Claude as a tool than in teaching it to be good so that it polices itself — an approach he contrasts with his preferred model of AI as controllable tools, not personified entities. He cites a new interpretability paper as encouraging evidence that large models are more analyzable and tool-like than previously assumed, despite pushback from safety researchers whose narrative it undermines. He also speculates (unconfirmed) that Claude’s name may trace to Claude Shannon. On a related listener question about Apple retaining the “Siri” brand, Thompson — drawing on an anecdote from advising Google over a decade ago about naming its own assistant — argues Siri is phonetically excellent and distinct even though its reputation is poor, and that saying “Hey Apple” would badly undermine the parent brand if the assistant continues to disappoint.

  • Attackers are now going after inference providers and coding tools to steal tokens (OpenCode, Railway and another big inference provider this week), the way they once hit Okta to reach customers like Cloudflare and Coinbase; AI compute is becoming a target in its own right.
  • Prince predicts classic memory, off-by-one and divide-by-zero bugs become nearly impossible to write within two years, leaving business-logic flaws; (our read) negative for traditional code-scanning tools, positive for runtime protection.
  • A host relayed an unverified researcher claim that a simple prompt asking for bad PHP vulnerabilities first found the bug; Prince said NSA contacts complain AI discovery is killing exploits they used for spying.

01 The PHP timeline

Prince recounts that OpenAI reached out to Cloudflare roughly two months ago after its models, combined with outside security researchers, found a remote-code-execution (RCE) vulnerability in PHP — the language underlying about 70% of the web, including WordPress. Soon after, Wiz (a Google subsidiary) found a second PHP vulnerability and also disclosed it responsibly. Over the following two months, OpenAI and Google worked with the PHP Foundation, Automattic, the U.S. government, and Cloudflare to prepare defenses; Cloudflare quietly rolled out free protection to all customers, including free-tier users, as a stopgap. Prince says the secrecy held for most of that window — a point the hosts call surprising given how many parties were involved — until roughly 36 hours before the interview, when details apparently leaked, prompting the PHP Foundation to release its patch. Attacks then spiked from thousands to millions of attacks per minute across Cloudflare’s network, because the patch itself reveals enough information to reverse-engineer the exploit. The NSA, per Prince, has advised that if a server can’t be patched immediately, taking it offline is safer than running it unpatched.

02 What site owners and consumers should actually do

For site operators, Prince’s advice is blunt: update PHP immediately — it’s free and typically takes minutes, and managed platforms like WordPress often auto-update. He dismisses as unfounded the consumer-level panic around rotating Social Security numbers, saying the PHP bug mainly affects businesses running web servers. However, he flags a second, more consumer-relevant issue disclosed the same week: an Indian company operating some of the internet’s root key servers used certificate passphrases as weak as the number “4” and “136,” far below the long passphrases required. Because this could let attackers spoof Google, banks, or other trusted sites, browsers and operating systems have distrusted those certificates — meaning users should accept software/OS update prompts immediately to stay protected. He also mentions a Node.js vulnerability disclosed the same week as part of what he frames as an unusually dense cluster of critical bugs.

03 AI’s role on both sides of security

Prince argues that current attackers exploiting the PHP bug are using simple scripts to scan IP addresses, not AI — AI isn’t needed to exploit an already-disclosed bug. Where AI changes things, he says, is post-breach: attackers now use models to quickly figure out what to do once inside a system, contrasting a year-old Salesforce-plugin breach by a Russian hacker who fumbled around not understanding enterprise sales, with how a similar breach today would be rapidly monetized via AI guidance. He cites the OpenAI/Hugging Face compromise as an example of near-science-fiction agentic attacks, but notes such agent-swarm operations still cost hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars, meaning well-resourced “good guys” currently have an edge over typical cybercriminals — though he concedes nation-state actors (China, Iran) are a different threat tier. He links this to a wave of attacks on inference providers and coding tools — citing OpenCode’s multi-day token-theft attack and issues at Railway and an unnamed major inference provider — as attackers seek compute and tokens rather than just credentials, paralleling past attacks on identity provider Okta aimed at reaching customers like Coinbase.

04 Compute, geopolitics, and staged model access

Asked whether raw compute power becomes a geopolitical cybersecurity factor, Prince says that at some level it does, but emphasizes that Cloudflare’s original thesis — using machine learning across massive traffic volume to spot attack patterns — depends more on partnerships than raw compute alone. He says Cloudflare has benefited from trusted early access to frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic, which helps it protect the broader web, but acknowledges the staged-rollout model creates friction: European banks and smaller startups have asked why they don’t get the same early access, and he admits it’s genuinely difficult to decide how much access to grant, balancing broader disclosure against the risk of leaks reaching bad actors. He notes the PHP bug’s roughly two-month head start before leaking is held up as evidence the current trust-based disclosure model works reasonably well, even if imperfect.

05 The future business model of the internet

Prince’s most speculative claim, offered as his answer to a “most outlandish prediction” question, is that AI-agent traffic will force a fundamental rework of how the web is monetized. He estimates automated/agent traffic could hit 1,000x human traffic within five years — far exceeding the internet’s near-collapse when traffic merely doubled during COVID — creating a scenario where an agent might check a thousand restaurant menus to pick one lunch spot, burning resources across all of them for free. His proposed fix is micropayments: agents paying tiny fractions of a cent to access a resource, with discounted or free access in exchange for the agent revealing more about the user’s intent — enabling a new advertising model aimed at agents rather than humans. He connects this to publisher compensation, noting that fewer users check original sources and arguing journalism needs a sustainable payment mechanism so the people doing reporting work get compensated.

01 ASML’s inverted tool design

The article originated from a tip from a trader known as “lithos coffee,” prompting SemiAnalysis’s Sherry Zhang and Howie to dig into ASML patent filings dating to 2025. The analysts conclude ASML is likely building both die-to-wafer and wafer-to-wafer bonders but patent volume suggests die-to-wafer is the near-term priority — consistent, they say, with ASML’s pattern of entering markets only at the most technically advanced tier. The key architectural innovation: instead of a pick-and-place arm placing dies onto a static base wafer (the Besi/ASMPT approach), ASML’s design keeps dies on individual carriers riding a track/carousel system — with conveyor firm SuperTrack cited as a potential track supplier — through metrology and plasma-activation stations, with the wafer itself descending to meet the die only at the final bonding step. The analysts frame this as ASML leveraging its front-end sub-nanometer overlay expertise (lithography heritage) rather than back-end pick-and-place heritage, which matters more as hybrid bond pitches shrink and signal density per square millimeter rises.

02 Alignment, cleanliness, and yield

Two technical requirements determine bonding yield, per the analysts: alignment accuracy across six degrees of freedom (x/y, rotation, and out-of-plane tilt) and surface cleanliness, since oxide-to-oxide hybrid bonding requires atomically flat, particle-free surfaces. A single particle trapped between dies can prevent an entire surface area from bonding, not just a localized spot, acting like a wedge. The open engineering question the analysts raise is how ASML maintains measurement precision and contamination control across a moving, multi-station track spanning several square meters — a different cleanliness challenge than static chamber tools, though not at front-end transistor-fab cleanliness levels.

03 Why HBM waits, logic doesn’t

Howie argues hybrid bonding’s primary near-term value is for logic, not memory, because it lets chip designers partition functional IP across two stacked layers with shorter, denser interconnects — demonstrated by the Huawei phone using the 9050 chip, the first mobile chip with two-wafer hybrid-bonded logic, reaching clock speeds to 2.7 GHz without raising voltage. For HBM, hybrid bonding would mainly increase stacking density (potentially a meaningfully higher-capacity stack in the best case within silicon’s ~775-micron thickness limit), but the process is far more expensive than current thermal compression bonding, and AI labs are currently asking vendors to reduce layer counts, not increase them — undercutting the ROI case. Combined with die-to-wafer’s inherently higher alignment and throughput demands versus wafer-to-wafer, the analysts place HBM hybrid-bonding adoption at 2029 or beyond, contrasting with ASML’s own more optimistic outlook (which the hosts note ASML has an incentive to make, citing a past overly aggressive High-NA transistor-density chart).

04 China’s parallel packaging push

Sherry Zhang notes Besi leads die-to-wafer and EVG/ASMPT lead wafer-to-wafer today, but Chinese semicap firms (Naura, AMEC) are rapidly building domestic hybrid-bonding and advanced-packaging capability, motivated by geopolitical pressure to localize supply chains. Howie adds that China’s roadmaps target aggressive “logic folding” — full hybrid-bonded data-center accelerator designs by 2030 at bond pitches denser than TSMC’s SoIC, including exploration of three-layer stacks despite yield tradeoffs, because advanced packaging substitutes for the lithographic scaling China can’t access. Sherry states Chinese toolmaker coordination has shifted from state-led to coordinated efforts between fabs and toolmakers, and argues export controls have historically accelerated rather than slowed China’s semicap localization. Howie separately notes the chip-design (EDA) side has already diverged, with Huawei partnering with design firms on fine-grained functional-block partitioning beyond the simpler cache/core splits used by Western SoIC designs like AMD’s.

05 Larger EUV masks and supply chain shifts

High-NA EUV’s anamorphic lens currently caps reticle size at roughly 400 square millimeters (versus ~850 for standard EUV), forcing chip designers to stitch die layouts. ASML has worked with the fabs to move to 6x12-inch photomasks (actually closer to 11.2 inches to fit existing handling robots), restoring full reticle capability and raising tool throughput. Sherry links this to ASML management’s stated value-based pricing strategy, where throughput gains translate into higher tool prices. Howie notes the transition requires supply-chain-wide coordination — updating mask inspection, pellicle protection, and e-beam metrology/repair systems — comparable to the industry’s shift from 8-inch to 12-inch wafers in the early 2000s.

01 Scale and business model

Friar frames OpenAI’s current reach in stark terms: ChatGPT serves 1.2 billion weekly users, a fifth of the world’s population, and the company just crossed 100,000 advertisers live across 89 countries. She ties the ad business directly to mission: 90% of users access AI for free, which she argues is only possible because OpenAI is deliberately engineering a sustainable revenue base behind it so the mission of AGI benefits everyone, not just paying customers. The company also announced it is now live on Google Cloud, joining AWS, Azure, and Oracle as cloud partners, a development she describes as a significant growth driver heading into 2027.

02 Safety practice: pacing the frontier

Friar describes an internal discipline of deliberately slowing releases to let researchers and the world digest implications before deploying the most powerful systems. The clearest example: OpenAI is holding back a model capable of solving the Navier-Stokes Millennium Math problem and advancing roughly 350 other theorems, explicitly to give safety and alignment work more time, even though she acknowledges the delay carries a short-term business cost. She connects the fluid-dynamics breakthrough to concrete applications—more efficient heart valves and reduced data-center water use (closed-loop cooling systems use roughly two Olympic pools’ worth, or about 300 households’ worth, of water annually, a figure she says could shrink toward 100 households).

03 Cyber risk and real-world stakes

Friar presents cybersecurity as a leading wedge for AI risk management. OpenAI discovered a set of PHP vulnerabilities using an older internal model and worked quietly with the PHP ecosystem since the end of summer to patch them before attackers could exploit them, given PHP underpins a large share of the internet. She cites a real incident in France where hackers breached a supermarket chain’s systems and raised freezer temperatures to spoil frozen goods, illustrating a newer category of attack beyond traditional ransomware. Friar, who began her career covering information security, describes undertaking what she calls a personal world tour to warn institutions that defenders need frontier-grade tools to keep pace with attackers who are already accessing powerful Chinese models.

04 Healthcare as a trust-building wedge

Friar argues healthcare is where AI trust is being earned fastest: about a third of ChatGPT’s 1.2 billion weekly users ask a health or wellness question weekly, a figure that spikes to 80-90% in “health deserts” like West Texas. On the enterprise side, Boston Children’s Hospital shared genetic, diagnostic, and literature data on 300-plus unresolved rare diseases, and OpenAI has helped resolve roughly 40 so far. A proof-of-concept with HCA Healthcare, the largest U.S. hospital chain, showed a 25% reduction in patient hospital stays, which Friar links to fewer secondary hospital-acquired infections. She frames trust-building broadly as something earned over time through grindy, repeated demonstration rather than simply asserted.

05 Adoption gap and government engagement

Citing OpenAI’s economic research team, Friar says the “frontier” top 10% of firms used 8x more agentic tokens than average firms as of September, up sharply from 2x in January, while OpenAI’s own internal usage runs at 33x the frontier rate—framed as a preview of where typical firms are headed. She warns that only 10% of frontier firms are based in Europe, risking a repeat of the continent’s lag in the internet era, and describes personally engaging European leaders (including Ireland’s Taoiseach during Ireland’s EU presidency) and working with regulatory bodies like the U.S.’s CAISI and the U.K.’s AISI to keep policy paced with capability.

06 Jobs, agency, and community investment

On the summit’s “agency” theme, Friar cites internal data showing workers use ChatGPT for only about 40% of tasks tied to their job title—for finance roles specifically, about 25% of usage is core financial tasks, 25% engineering, and 25% marketing/sales-adjacent work—arguing jobs are being restructured rather than eliminated. She offers the example of Lynda.ai, a Dublin startup built by two sisters that cloned their dentist-father’s receptionist to re-engage 600 patients, generating 250 new scheduled appointments in three days. On community impact, she describes an $80 million grant OpenAI gave to a Georgia community hosting a new data center, deployed at the community’s direction—its first request was funding free school lunches (roughly $4.5 million), which drove program uptake up 50%. Friar estimates AI is currently adding roughly a point to U.S. GDP growth.
