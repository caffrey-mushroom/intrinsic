SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion assessed a leading Chinese NAND producer’s bonded array-and-CMOS architecture, focusing on export-control exposure, scaling beyond 300 layers, manufacturing economics, and competition from global memory suppliers. The expert viewed existing production as sustainable but increasingly costly, while next-generation development faces more fundamental capability constraints.

Key Insights

The most critical bottlenecks are high-aspect-ratio etching, wafer bonding and alignment, advanced metrology, complex deposition, and immersion lithography. Current-generation production mainly faces gradual yield deterioration, downtime, and higher costs. Development beyond 300 layers carries a more severe risk of schedule delays or an uncompetitive yield ceiling. Metrology may be the primary yield-ramp constraint because weak inspection slows defect identification and process correction, particularly after irreversible wafer bonding. Multi-deck stacking can reduce etch difficulty but adds alignment, lithography, process time, and cost. Molybdenum could outperform tungsten in narrow word-line structures through lower resistance and reduced fluorine-related reliability risk. However, immature tools, costly precursors, oxidation sensitivity, and extensive qualification remain barriers. Bonded NAND is no longer a unique architectural advantage. Future positioning will depend primarily on yield, cost, tool access, and execution. Architecture alone does not determine margins. Pricing cycles, utilization, inventory, yield maturity, and product mix are substantially more important.

GP: Which categories of capital equipment, process consumables and metrology tools constitute the most sensitive bottlenecks for sustaining YMTC's current Xtacking based production under prevailing US Japan semiconductor export control frameworks?

EXPERT: I would say that the most sensitive issues or bottlenecks are the following. The first one is high aspect ratio etch because channel hole, slit, staircase, Lam and Tokyo Electron Cryo and HAR etch are the benchmark. So domestic etch from TEL and AMAT works but profile control, uniformity, and throughput at high layer counts are the gap. The second one is, of course, the wafer bonding and alignments because the core of X-stack, Xtacking array wafer bonded to CMOS wafer, the risk is bonders, surface activation, and sub-micron overlay and alignment. So yield losses from voids and misalignment show up here first. The third one that I think is the advanced metrology and inspection because KLA, Applied, and Hitachi High-Tech dominate overlay, CD-SEM, deep profile and bonded wafer void inspection. So domestic alternatives lag and without good metrology, yield ramps slow down even if the process tools exist. The fourth one out of the five is the deposition because deposition covers the on-stack deposition and tungsten or metal line fill where void-free fill at very high aspect ratio is hard.

GP: What supply chain risk differentiation exists between sustaining current generation Xtacking mass production versus developing and validating next generation Xtacking derivatives targeting 300+ layers?

EXPERT: I think that the sustaining current generation Xtacking attrition risk is the process window is already qualified. So the question is whether the fab can keep running it. That depends on spare service, consumable, and tool uptime. Substitution is incremental, so domestic tools like Naura, AMEC, Piotech can replace foreign ones step by step, and the lower spec steps can be qualified one at a time. Reports say that more than half of the tools in YMTC new third fab are domestic, including vertical stacking equipment. The remaining exposures are Japanese consumables, like resist, chemicals, gases, subcomponents, and metrology. This causes yield drift and downtime, but rarely a full stop. Of course, cost is the main impact because lower yield and throughput mean higher CapEx per bit. So developing and validating more than 300 layers derivatives is a capability gap risk. Let's say it like this: taller stacks push the hardest process steps beyond what is proven, so this includes higher aspect ratio etch like cryo within tighter profile control, stack stress and wafer warpage, void free wordline fill, and tighter bonding overlay. Metrology, for example, becomes the gating factor. Without deep profile overlay and bonded wafer inspection at the leading edge, engineers can't learn fast enough to close the process. Domestic tools, of course, have mostly been qualified on mature nodes in production, so co-development with leading-edge vendors like Lam, TLK is no longer available to YMTC, so the learning loop is slower. Another one is that the workaround is string stacking, where two decks are built instead of one Taller stack. YMTC Xtacking 4.0 reportedly has fewer layers than its 232L generation while claiming comparable performance. But multi-deck mean more litho, more etch, more overlay steps, which raises exposure to the tools hardest to replace and, of course, adds cost. So I would say that the differentiation is that sustaining carries risk of attrition, which is manageable and priced into cost. Next generation has risk of capability gaps, which is binary and shows up as scheduling slips or yield ceiling. The time horizon for sustaining risk builds slowly over years, so development risk is front-loaded and decides whether YMTC reaches more than 300 layers competitively.

GP: What fundamental process challenges emerge for Xtacking architecture when moving past 300 stacked layers, regarding HAR channel hole etch, film stack deposition, word line metallization and hybrid bond alignment overlay?

EXPERT: HAR channel hole etch, the aspect ratio pushes toward 1 to 100 or more. So ion energy and angle control get harder with depth. Typical failures are bowing, twisting, CD taper, small bottom CDs, and etch stop. Mask selectivity is the other limit because the hard mask has to survive a longer and deeper etch. The usual workaround is string stacking, like two decks, which trades etch difficulty for an extra deck-to-deck alignment step and more process cost. Another issue is the film stack deposition-hundreds of alternating oxide-nitride pairs and stress, etch stress, which actually causes wafer bow. Bow hurts lithography chucking, etch uniformity, and, of course, later, the bonding. I would say that thickness uniformity has to hold across every layer, and particles and defects accumulate. Deposition time scales with the layer count, so throughput and cost per wafer rise.

The wordlineline-let's talk about the wordline-let'sline metallization. Removing the nitride sacrificial layers through narrow slits means long lateral etches that need high selectivity and must not collapse the step. Tungsten then has to fill thin gaps far from the slit without voids, and resistance climbs as the layers get thinner. Fluorine diffusion from tungsten precursors is a reliability risk, I must say. Alternatives such as low fluorine tungsten or molybdenum need new qualification.

Another item is the hybrid bond overlay. So, Xtacking bonds the array wafer to the CMOS wafer. Higher IO density means finer pad pitch and tighter overlay. Stress-induced bow and distortion from the tall array wafer make alignment harder. Copper CMP dishing, surface roughness, and particles create voids and opens. And, of course, wafer-to-wafer bonding can't pair known good die, so array yield times CMOS yield compound. So a weak array process drags down the whole bonded wafer.

GP: What technical and economic trade offs arise from potential material transitions such as tungsten to molybdenum word line metallization for Xtacking platforms targeting 300+ layers?

EXPERT: I would say, why consider molybdenum? Well, first of all, as wordline gap shrinks at more than 300 layers, tungsten resistivity rises because of grain boundary and surface scattering, and its barrier and nucleation layers take up a growing share of the gap. Now, molybdenum has a shorter electron mean free path, so thin film resistivity is lower, and it also needs little or no barrier layer, which actually leaves more conductor in the gap. Another issue is that it can be deposited without fluorine-containing precursor, so there is no fluorine diffusion into the oxide and less reliability risk. Now, of course, there are technical trade-offs. For example, gain, fill, process integration, and qualification. Let's talk about the gain. Lower the the wordline resistance means faster read and write, lower RC delay, and lower power. Fill, void-free fill from slit to far gap still has to be proven in very high aspect ratio structures. Process: The tools are less mature ALD or CVD with the molybdenum precursor, and deposition rates and film adhesion need work. Integration: anneal behavior, stress, and interface with the blocking oxide have to be requalified.

Molybdenum is also prone to oxidation, so handling and the queue times matter. Qualification: new reliability, endurance, and retention data is needed. That is a long learning loop, especially for a fab with limited access to leading-edge vendors. Now, there's also economic trade-offs, cost savings, and what will be the net? So cost: molybdenum precursors are costlier and have thinner supply than WF6. Tools need requalification or replacement, which means upfront CapEx. Savings: Thinner stacks, higher speed, and better bit density can lower cost per bit once the process is mature. So skipping barrier steps may offset some costs. The net is the payback depends on yield and scale. So for a leader, it is a clear path to continued scaling. For a smaller player, it is a bigger bet on a process nobody has fully de-risked. So I would say that the YMTC-specific angle is that domestic tool makers would need to build the molybdenum deposition capability, and Molybdenum precursor may come from restricted or foreign suppliers, adding a material supply risk. And of course, a fab with limited access to leading vendors will probably adopt later than the leaders. So it would either stay with tungsten longer using Thinner liners and process set tuning, or wait for the domestic Molybdenum tools.

GP: To what degree do metrology and inline characterization capabilities become a gating factor for yield ramp of Xtacking enabled ultra high layer count devices, given sub micron bonding overlay and nanoscale surface topography requirements?

EXPERT: I can say that the metrology is close to the main gating factor because past about 300 layers, how fast you can find and fix a problem sets the yield ramp, I would say more than whether the process steps exist. So why does it gate the ramp? Because bonding overlay is the tightest spec. With submicron end shrinking, hybrid bond pitch, a small misalignment gives opens or shorts. Overlay has to be measured on both wafers, then fed back or forward to the bonder. Now, surface topography decides bond quality. Copper dishing, oxide roughness, and wafer bow will have to be in the nanometer range for void-free bonding. So a small excursion in CMP gives voids across a wide area. Now, actually, you can't rework a bonded wafer. Once the array and CMOS wafer are joined, a defect is permanent. So pre-bond inspection therefore matters far more than post-bond screening. Now, wafer-to-wafer bonding compounds yield, so without known good die matching and a weak array or CMOS wafer costs you the pair. Better pre-bond sorting, I would say, depends on good in-line data. So now, where measurement is actually hardest at ultra-high layer counts: First of all, in deep structures. So channel hole profile, bowing, twisting, and bottom CDs are buried in a stack that optical tools can't fully see. This needs cross- section X-ray based or other specialized deep profile methods, which are slow and sometimes Destructive. Now, stress and bow. Film stack stress has to be monitored per layer and per wafer because it feeds directly into overlay and bonding. Bonded wafer inspection, acoustic and infrared methods detect voids after bonding, but resolution and roughness are limited. Now sampling: Destructive methods limit how many wafers you can check, so fewer data points mean lower root cause analysis and more yield learning cycles. So why is it actually a bigger issue for constrained fab? Because the leading metrology comes mostly from US and Japanese vendors: KLA, Applied Materials, Hitachi High-Tech, so domestic alternatives are less mature. And another issue is that weaker metrology mean fewer, noisier data points and slower feedback loops. So even if the process tools are adequate, the team learns more slowly.

EXPERT: I would say that the core idea is that Xtacking's advantage is that the CMOS periphery and the array are built on separate wafers and bonded. So if you keep adding layers without redesigning, the array stops being the limiting side and the periphery and the interconnect become a problem. So let's take the die area penalty, periphery mismatch for example. Taller stacks let you shrink the array footprint, but the CMOS has to fit under it. EXPERT: I would say that the Xtacking is no longer a unique architecture. YMTC's edge shifts from only one with bonding to who executes bonding best at scale. So the advantage that remains is YMTC's experience and cost position on bonding, but it narrows as The leaders add deeper process maturity, tools, and scale. So what global players are doing, for example, Kioxia, Sandisk, they already use a bonded CMOS-to-array approach. They call it CBA in recent generation. Samsung. Samsung is reported to be moving to wafer bonding for its 400 layers class generation. SK Hynix and Micron have used periphery under cell design. So bonding is a likely next step as layer counts rise, though it's not certain on timing. Now, how does does positioning shift over the two to three generations? Generation one, which I call the near term, YMTC keeps its edge at the bonding as the bonding pioneer, sure, with performance comparable to the leaders. The gap is mostly yield and cost, not architecture. In generation two, competitors close the architectural gap. So they gain the same benefits and independently optimize periphery, faster IO, higher density, and better die efficiency. So YMTC's differentiation narrows to Bonding yield and IO performance. Generation three, the outcome depends on execution because leaders can combine bonding with 300 and more layers, new word line materials, advanced metrology, and leading edge tools.
