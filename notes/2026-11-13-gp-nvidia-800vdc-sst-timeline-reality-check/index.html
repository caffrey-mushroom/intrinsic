SUMMARY

Overview

The expert discussed power delivery for AI data centers, including Nvidia’s Kyber rack timeline, adoption of 800VDC and ±400V architectures, voltage conversion, sidecars, and solid-state transformers (SSTs). The main constraints are specialized materials, engineering challenges, and lengthy safety certification for systems connected to public power grids.

Key Insights

Kyber mass production is more likely in Q2–Q3 2028 than the earlier target. High-layer backplanes create yield, alignment, and warpage issues; shortages of specialized materials such as T-glass and high-speed laminates are a harder constraint than the engineering problems. Nvidia may describe ramp-up as mass production before yields stabilize. Expedited manufacturing can save about two months, while yield improvement may take several more months, contributing to differences between company roadmaps and supplier expectations. ±400V is expected to commercialize sooner and at lower cost than 800V. The expert expects both architectures to coexist for years, potentially much longer, because infrastructure choices are difficult to reverse once deployed. Two- and three-stage voltage conversion are both considered production-ready. The harder challenge is vertical power delivery beneath the processor, which could reach production around late 2027 or early 2028. Sidecars are expected to remain a long-term feature as accelerator power needs grow, even as their contents evolve. SST deployment in early 2028 is considered unlikely globally. Grid certification is the main obstacle; broad deployment is more plausible in late 2029 or 2030. Reliability and safety would matter more to buyers than purchase price, while efficiency gains are the primary benefit and space savings are secondary. The expert sees regulatory timelines as an underestimated risk and expects Asia to approve deployments ahead of the United States and Europe.

MM: Thank you for the introduction. I believe your more than 30 years of industry experience will help us gain a deeper understanding of this topic. First, based on what you have observed across the supply chain and among CSPs, when do you think Nvidia’s next-generation Kyber rack architecture could realistically enter mass production? Can Nvidia meet its original roadmap?

TC: At present, we expect Kyber to enter mass production in the second quarter of 2028. Personally, I think the third quarter would be a safer estimate because quite a few Kyber-related issues remain unresolved. From a technical perspective, several aspects have changed completely from the previous VR200 generation. For example, the backplane has not only increased from more than 40 layers to nearly 80 layers, which will reduce the yield, but it also uses an orthogonal backplane. This means that different layers are rotated by 90 degrees. Once they are rotated, they must be aligned, and the alignment tolerance must be less than three microns. Therefore, in addition to the lower yield caused by the increased layer count, there is also a new alignment challenge. This is the first major technical issue. However, I think suppliers have enough time and should be able to resolve it. The second issue is more difficult and concerns the supply of upstream materials and equipment. As speed increases, power dissipation and heat generation also rise, while output signal speeds become faster. This means low-Dk materials, glass-fiber materials, and specialized copper-clad laminates for high-speed silicon are required. The supply of these materials is highly concentrated among one or two Japanese companies. For example, Nittobo dominates the high-end T-glass market. This problem is more difficult to solve than the technical issue I just mentioned. Kyber’s technical problems can be resolved with sufficient time and experience. However, if materials are in short supply, production cannot begin without those materials.

Nvidia has provided funding to Japanese suppliers to support capacity expansion. However, building or expanding a factory requires purchasing materials and equipment, waiting for new machinery to arrive, starting operations, adjusting process parameters, and then moving into mass production. This cannot be completed overnight. That is why the original target of late 2027 has likely shifted to the second or even third quarter of 2028.

These issues could be resolved because Nvidia could spend more money and ask suppliers to run what is known as a super hot lot. A super hot lot shortens the production cycle, but it costs approximately three times as much. Paying three times the normal price is not a problem for Nvidia. Its priority is to shorten the schedule sufficiently to meet its target. A super hot lot can save approximately two months.

Another factor is how mass production is defined. From a supply chain perspective, we estimate mass production based on when a product can genuinely be produced at scale, rather than when ramp-up begins. From the beginning of ramp-up until yields stabilize, there is usually a four- to five-month period. Nvidia can treat the beginning of ramp- up as the start of mass production and claim that the product is already in mass production, even if yields are still poor. From a supplier’s perspective, production at poor yields is loss-making, so suppliers would not call it mass production. Nvidia, however, can tell the market that mass production has begun. The alternative uses an additional stage, stepping from 800V to 50V, then to 12V, and finally to 1V. In other words, the voltage will either be stepped down in two stages or three stages. A single-stage conversion is too difficult. It can technically be achieved, but it cannot be mass-produced economically, or the yield in mass production would be too low.
