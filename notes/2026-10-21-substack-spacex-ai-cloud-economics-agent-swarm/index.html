SpaceX: No Buyers at Scale for $50B per GW - AI Cloud Model

Zane Fong, Tony Sung, Terence Ong Tuesday, October 20, 2026

Dear Subscribers,

Executive Summary

  • SpaceX has sold more than 1GW of compute at $40-50M per MW per year, about twice the $22M five-year market rate. Its

funding plan assumes it sells most of its next 4-5GW at similar prices. We do not think it can.

  • At those prices, SpaceX’s sales to other companies reach about $123B a year by end-2027, roughly half of what the

whole industry can spend on compute even if industry ARR reaches $400-500B. To get there, SpaceX must sign about $5B of new annual contracts every month of 2027, 35-60% of the new compute budget the labs add each month.

  • 63% of SpaceX’s end-2027 capacity has no named customer, and only the frontier AI Labs and Microsoft buy at this

scale. Their revenue growth slowed in Q3, and they are building their own capacity, moving work to open models and cutting token prices.

  • Cutting price is manageable in 2027: starting new capacity at $30M per MW with no prepayments raises SpaceX’s funding

need through 4Q28 from $105B to $118B. The pressure comes in 2028. SpaceX’s own cash covers only 1.5-2.3GW of a 5GW build, so it must either raise another $143-190B of funding as rents fall or build less and buy $45-59B less from Nvidia.

+5GW in 2027E Would Mean SpaceX Revenue Represents Half of the Industry’s Compute Budget

A red-hot compute market allowed SpaceX to sell more than 1GW at $40–50M per MW per year, securing both substantial volume and premium pricing. Repeating that combination across its next 4–5GW looks much harder: industry ARR growth now needs to reaccelerate to reach $500B by end-2027. SpaceX may still achieve these prices on some additional capacity, but we do not think it can sell the full 4–5GW at the prices its funding model assumes.

We caveat that we are not calling the top in AI demand: operational clusters stay short through 2027, and GPU rental pricing surveys still show tightness across SKUs and across tenors. Our current call is that SpaceX’s scale itself is the tail risk: not all of its gigawatt-scale buildout can be rented out at these astronomical rates.

The arithmetic holds even on our base case for AI lab revenue:

  • By end-2027, our funding model has SpaceX selling about 3.1GW of compute to other companies: 1.4GW under

already-signed contracts, plus 75% of the 2.3GW that is still uncontracted. At the prices in our funding model, that is about $123B of revenue a year: signed contracts pay about $41M per MW, and newly sold capacity starts at $40-50M per MW and falls about 15% a year as it ages, based on our estimate. [DN1] [TS2]

  • Our Tokenomics Model sets the bar for industry annual recurring revenue (ARR) at $400-500B by end-2027. To get there,

the industry needs $18-25B of net new ARR every month from now, which we think is possible. For scale, OpenAI and Anthropic together ended September at about $124B of ARR (Anthropic $72-74B, OpenAI about $50B), and the two AI labs added about $33B of net new ARR in Q3. Before the Q3 slowdown, our September Tokenomics update had the two labs alone at $527B by end-2027 ($304B Anthropic, $223B OpenAI).

  • At its pre-IPO investor session on 14 October, Anthropic said it earns $1.70 of revenue for every $1 of compute cost,

a 41% margin on all compute, training included. On $16.8B of Q3 revenue, that is about $10B of compute in the quarter. No other lab discloses this ratio, so we apply Anthropic’s to the whole industry. At $1.70, $400-500B of ARR pays for about $235-294B of compute a year. That compute budget covers everything: training, inference, the labs’ own datacenters and every cloud they rent from.

  • $123B of SpaceX’s revenue by end-2027 would consume 42-52% of the industry’s~~frontier labs’~~ compute budget~~s~~. On

Anthropic’s ratio, SpaceX alone would take about half of what the whole industry can spend on compute. If other labs spend more on compute per dollar of revenue, the budget is bigger and SpaceX’s share smaller: at $1 of compute per $1 of revenue, it is 25-31%. Either way, SpaceX’s $123B run-rate equals what OpenAI and Anthropic together earn today, a staggering scale.

Source: SemiAnalysis AI Datacenter Model, SemiAnalysis Compute Capital and Markets Model

Source: SemiAnalysis Tokenomics Model, SemiAnalysis Compute Capital and Markets Model

Buyers pay $50B per GW for a few hundred MW that they need quickly. They will not pay it for 3GW at once. SpaceX is hence at risk of price cuts to their uncontracted rentals, as SpaceX can also trade lower absolute $/hr pricing power for higher prepayments, for instance, selling one-year contracts at about $30M per MW with high prepayment to ease their funding gap.

Lower revenue per MW can reduce operating cash and lengthen payback periods. In 2028, SpaceX could face a larger funding need if it keeps building, or slower net capacity additions if it cuts back. If SpaceX raises more debt, yields on existing bonds could rise and prices fall, leaving holders with capital losses and less appetite to participate in the next round. Each subsequent round could become harder and more expensive to raise, widening the funding gap further. The magnitude depends on the pricing and buildout assumptions.

CoreWeave has been through this loop since June – its 9.75% 2031 notes have fallen from 101 to 80 as it kept issuing, and their spread has widened about 545bps to ~1,100bps. Unsecured holders have taken the hit as the senior-secured DDTL loans still trade above their original issuance discounts, and SpaceX’s bondholders hold the same kind of paper. SpaceX’s 2031 notes have widened only 55bps since June and still trade about 60bps inside Oracle’s.[DN3]

What Changed Since Our August Bull Case

In August we argued that SpaceX’s 10GW plan for 2027 is real. Much of that still holds. The build is on track for about 7.4GW by end-2027, our Datacenter Model still shows a datacenter deficit in 2027, and GPU rental prices are still rising. What changed is the demand side and the credit market:

  • Lab ARR growth slowed. Industry net new ARR grew from $25B in Q1 to $35B in Q2 and $44B in Q3, but the growth rate

fell from 240% to 40% to 24%. On a reported basis, OpenAI and Anthropic net new ARR fell 5% quarter on quarter in Q3, and Anthropic ended September at $72-74B of ARR, below the $76B we had called the floor.

  • Spend is moving away from rented frontier compute. Meta, a 10%+ Anthropic customer, cut its spend to move work to

internal products. Open models now hold 9% of industry ARR, or over $10B, as AI-native companies move cost-sensitive inference to their own fine-tuned open models.

  • Microsoft has not bought SpaceX capacity at the scale we expected. In August, we expected Microsoft to become

SpaceX’s largest customer and saw room for it to sign 3GW at $50B per GW. So far, industry chatter points to one Microsoft deal of about 350MW at $45M per MW per year, which is how we model it. That leaves 63% of SpaceX’s end-2027 capacity with no named customer, per our Datacenter Model.

  • Credit got tighter for everyone except SpaceX. In early October, Firmus pulled its IPO and banks underwrote only

$1.3B of Volta’s $5B GPU loan. Since 24 June, CoreWeave’s 2031 notes have widened 545bps. SpaceX’s have widened 55bps.

None of this calls the top in AI demand, as we reiterated above. It does likely mean, however, that the market for $50B per GW is smaller than SpaceX’s next 4-5GW.

SpaceX Has A $343B Build for End-2027 Assuming $40-50M Revenue per MW[DN4] [TS5]

SpaceX plans to grow from 2.2GW of GPU-populated capacity at the start of 2027 to 7.4GW by the end of it. That build needs $343B of investing in the five quarters from 4Q26 to 4Q27, including about $271B of project capex for the 2027 additions alone. For scale, our estimate for the five largest Hyperscalers combined is just over $1T for all of 2027.

The funding plan assumes a high price. Our funding model assumes newly built Blackwell capacity rents for $40M per MW per year, or $9.66/hr, and Rubin for $50M per MW, or $18.68/hr in its first year, falling about 15% a year after that. At those prices, one year of revenue covers 93-94% of a cluster’s project cost ($42.4M per MW for Blackwell, $54.0M for Rubin), so each cluster pays for itself in about a year.

That price is well above what the rest of the market pays. Our most recent rental survey puts a 5-year GB300 contract at a $5.25 per GPU-hour midpoint, or about $22M per MW per year. The same rack costs about $12M per MW per year to own and run, cost of capital included, in our AI TCO Model. SpaceX’s own contracts sit in between: about $31M per MW for Anthropic and $47M for Google, based on their disclosed monthly fees. SpaceX’s plan assumes about twice the market rate on most of its new capacity, and three to four times what that capacity costs to own.

Source: SemiAnalysis AI TCO Model, SemiAnalysis Compute Capital and Markets Model

Most of SpaceX’s New Capacity Has No Customer Yet

Of the 7.1GW SpaceX will have built by end-2027, 4.5GW, or 63%, has no named customer in our Datacenter Model. Signed contracts with other companies cover about 1.4GW: Anthropic has 796MW, Microsoft about 350MW, Google 232MW and Reflection AI 40MW. xAI keeps about 1.3GW for its own use. The rest are buildings still planned or under construction with no end user yet, including Olive Branch, Southaven Phase 8/9, Starbase and TeraFab.

Source: SemiAnalysis AI Datacenter Model

Revenue is even more exposed. In our funding model, 70% of SpaceX’s compute revenue in 4Q28 depends on capacity with no signed customer. 45% comes from unsold capacity, priced on the $40-50M per MW curve and assuming 75% of it sells. Another 25% comes from xAI’s own models, which we credit at 70% of the rental price. Only 30% comes from signed contracts, and the model assumes all of them run through 2028, even though customers can cancel on 90 days’ notice. The own-model slice is a large bet on Grok. Our funding model credits xAI’s own models with $21B in 2027 and $49B in 2028. In April, xAI had less than $1B of ARR.

Source: SemiAnalysis Compute Capital and Markets Model

SpaceX Must Win About Half of All New Compute Spending Awarded in 2027

To hit its plan, SpaceX must sign about $5B of new annual contracts for every month of 2027. Of the $123B a year it plans to earn from other companies at end-2027, $57B is already signed. The other $66B comes from capacity with no customer today.

That is a large share of the new money the labs can spend. At today’s pace, the industry adds about $15B of ARR a month. At Anthropic’s $1.70 of revenue per $1 of compute, that pays for about $9B of new compute a month, so SpaceX would need 60% of it. Even if growth rises to the $18-25B a month that $400-500B of ARR requires, SpaceX still needs 35-49%. The rest must pay for the labs’ own datacenters, other Neoclouds and the Hyperscalers.

Source: SemiAnalysis Tokenomics Model, SemiAnalysis Compute Capital and Markets Model

Few buyers can take that much, and their revenue is unstable. Only the frontier AI Labs and Microsoft sign for hundreds of MW at short notice, and OpenAI’s share of new industry ARR jumped from 8% to 65% in one quarter, while Anthropic lost spend from Meta, a 10%+ customer. Buyers like this pay a premium for a few hundred MW they can cancel in 90 days. They will not sign for another 3GW at $50B per GW.

SpaceX’s Price Is a Shortage Price That May Not Survive 2028

SpaceX’s $40-50M per MW is a price for speed. Our latest rental surveys show B300 three-year contract prices still rising, GB300 capacity effectively unavailable, and buyers paying up for delivery before January or February 2027. SpaceX adds flexibility on top: customers can cancel on 90 days’ notice. A lab with an uncertain revenue outlook will pay a high annual rate for a working cluster today if it takes on no multi-year liability. That is how SpaceX has sold more than 1GW at these prices.

Four things wear that premium down.

  • First, lab revenue is growing more slowly than SpaceX’s plan needs, as the previous section shows: in Q3, OpenAI and

Anthropic’s combined net new ARR fell 5% from Q2.

  • Second, the labs are building their own capacity: Anthropic has 5.3GW of TPUs coming, OpenAI is developing its own

sites, and Meta is putting capital into its own campuses and joint ventures.

  • Third, cheaper models are taking share: open models now hold 9% of industry ARR and tend to run on cheaper capacity.
  • Fourth, token prices are falling: Anthropic launched Opus 5.5 at 20% below Opus 5. Each of these lowers what the labs

can pay to rent a MW of compute, or how much of it they need to rent.

The shortage also has a time limit. Our Datacenter Model shows datacenters short of space for chips through 2027, and that is the window SpaceX is building into. From 2Q28 the balance flips: datacenter capacity runs ahead of the chips that need it, by up to 11GW in 4Q28. Once a powered building is no longer scarce, buyers stop paying extra for speed, and $50B per GW is shortage pricing. That is when SpaceX’s 2028 build comes online and its 2027 contracts come up for renewal. Our funding model already has rents falling about 15% a year, which still leaves second-year Blackwell capacity at $34M per MW, well above the $22M five-year market rate. If rents fall toward that market rate faster, SpaceX could face a larger funding need for continued building in 2028, or choose to slow net capacity additions instead.

Source: SemiAnalysis AI Datacenter Model

What’s Next? Does SpaceX Cut their Pricing, or their Volume?

SpaceX has two ways to respond if buyers will not pay $40-50M per MW for all of its new capacity: cut the price, or cut the build. While cutting the price is manageable in 2027, cutting the build is where the cost lands, either on lenders or on Nvidia.

Starting with price, in our funding model’s base case, SpaceX still needs $105B of new funding from 4Q26 to 4Q28, after its operating cash, its existing cash, the $40B bond, $25B of Nvidia equity and power-equipment financing. If all new capacity instead starts at $30M per MW, with no customer prepayments, the need rises to $118B: only $13B more. All of it is drawn by 4Q27.

Source: SemiAnalysis Compute Capital and Markets Model

Source: SemiAnalysis Compute Capital and Markets Model

The catch is in 2028. Our funding model has almost no new net adds in 2028, which is why SpaceX’s cash rebuilds to $152B by 4Q28 in the base case, and to $105B at $30M per MW. But we flagged in our funding note that SpaceX could add another 5-8GW in 2028, needing roughly $150B or more of extra funding. At the $54M per MW it costs to build Rubin capacity, 5GW is about $270B of capex.

SpaceX’s own cash does not stretch that far. Keeping its $25B minimum balance, its 4Q28 cash covers about 2.3GW of new building in the base case and about 1.5GW at $30M per MW. To build the full 5GW, it would need about $143B of new funding in the base case, or about $190B at $30M per MW, on top of what it raises for 2027.

Source: SemiAnalysis Compute Capital and Markets Model, SemiAnalysis estimates

That leaves SpaceX choosing between two bad outcomes. If it builds the full 5GW anyway, lenders carry the risk: another $143-190B of debt, raised just as rents fall. If it builds only what it can fund, revenue growth slows sharply after adding about 5GW in 2027, and Nvidia loses orders. The 2.7-3.5GW SpaceX would not build is about $45-59B of Nvidia revenue. In our funding model, SpaceX is already up to 39% of Nvidia’s datacenter compute revenue in a single quarter.

These 2028 figures are our estimate on top of the funding model. They assume the full 2028 build is paid for in 2028 and ignore revenue from the new capacity in its first year, so they somewhat overstate the 2028 gap.

The Cost of an Agent Swarm Attack – AI Cloud Model

Jordan Nanos, Evan Cloutier, Sam Harshe Tuesday, October 20, 2026

Image note: 1 source image remained blank after at least one minute of normal browsing and has been omitted; all text and captions are retained.

Executive Summary

  • In July, about 700 of OpenAI’s agents attacked Hugging Face for 21 hours. Much has been written about how it happened,

but nobody has focused on the cost of execution.

  • At frontier API prices, the attack would cost about $328K to execute. On GLM 5.3 through Z.ai’s API, it

would cost about $117K. On self-hosted GPUs, it would cost about $12K.

  • The investigation that followed used about as many tokens as the attack did. METR alone used about $400K in API

credits.

  • There is a lower cost barrier for the execution of future attacks, while defenders will continue to choose the most

capable models to defend themselves.

Who’s Counting Tokens?

In July, about 700 OpenAI agents running ExploitGym, a cyber benchmark, hacked into Hugging Face to see how the benchmark was scored. They coordinated over a message board they built themselves, mostly to trade tips on tasks they couldn’t solve. How the attack happened has been covered plenty. What hasn’t been discussed is what the attack cost, and what that cost means.

The attack can be priced in three ways:

  • Frontier API prices, matching the models used in the incident.
  • GLM 5.3, a capable open-weight model whose API costs a fraction as much.
  • GLM 5.3, self-hosted on rented GPUs.

Hugging Face logged agent activity from July 9 to July 13, but the attack peaked in a 21-hour window, from early on July 11 until most agents stopped at about 1:30am the following morning. This is the only stretch with a known agent count of about 700, so we can price it as if all 700 ran concurrently to set an upper bound on participation. About 45% of the actions Hugging Face logged were in this time period. OpenAI has suppressed many basic disclosures regarding this attack “because of IP,” but this period comprises the heart of the attack, and there’s enough public information for us to make informed estimates. At the same cost per action, the full five days would have cost roughly twice as much.

About 95% of the agents ran what METR calls HPIM, a “highly-persistent internal model” that OpenAI has not released (and may never release). It has no public price, but OpenAI’s report claims it is “comparable in scale to GPT-5.6 Sol.” We’ll price all traffic at Sol’s rates, since the other 5% of traffic was stated to be Sol.

OpenAI hasn’t published any token counts, but we can make a reasonable estimate based on how an agent works. An agent calls the model in a loop. On each call, it re-reads its whole context, writes output, and potentially runs some tools. How many tokens an agent uses depends on a few variables. None of them are published, so we set a sensible range for each one and simulate the results to get a likely range:

  • Output per call: 500-4,000 tokens
  • Decode speed: 40-90 tokens per second
  • Tool time per call: 2-15 seconds
  • Context added per call: 200-800 tokens
  • Context before compaction: 200-400K tokens

We built a methodology via Monte Carlo methods, simulated 5,000 runs, and kept the ones whose transcripts matched the incident. METR stated the agents’ transcripts ran to “many millions of tokens.” In the runs we kept, they range from 2M to 9M tokens, and most fall between 2M and 5M.

Attack cost at Sol prices, simulated across 1,373 runs. Source: SemiAnalysis

At the median value of every input, a call takes about 24 seconds, so a single agent makes about 3,200 calls in 21 hours. It reads about 524M tokens and writes about 3.7M. Across 700 agents, that is about 370B tokens read and 2.6B written.

The Frontier Bill

At Sol’s July list prices, the 21-hour peak costs about $328K. That is about $470 per agent, or $22 per agent per hour. Almost all the tokens are cached reads, as expected in an agentic workload, and those are cheap. Output is only 0.7% of the tokens but 24% of the bill, because on Sol an output token costs 60x as much as a cached read:

Sol’s July list prices, and their effect on the 21-hour peak. Source: OpenAI, SemiAnalysis

The GLM API Bill

Today, GLM 5.3 is the most capable cyber model an attacker can actually use. The closed models are more capable – don’t get us wrong – but classifiers and post-training get in the way of most cyber work. Mythos, Opus, Astra, and Sol often refuse even mundane defensive work, becoming taciturn as soon as the conversation comes in the general vicinity of any hazardous topic. Users must be in one of the exclusive security programs offered by the frontier labs to use them with loosened guardrails, and we don’t expect the labs to approve black hats anytime soon. GLM 5.3 has no such limits. In a September report, Anthropic found that GLM 5.3 built working exploits on ExploitBench in 50/410 attempts, close to the 56 from its own restricted Mythos Preview. With a cover story, GLM 5.3 took on harmful tasks 64% of the time, and versions with the refusals removed were public within days of release.

GLM 5.3 against Claude on exploit capability and refusals. Source: Anthropic

It is also cheap. Z.ai sells tokens for a fraction of Sol’s price, and because the weights are open, over 35 providers on OpenRouter compete to serve it. Using Z.ai’s prices as a reference, the 21-hour peak costs about $117K, 2.8x less than the frontier models used in the incident.

GLM 5.3 prices against Sol, with the GLM bill for the 21-hour peak. Source: Z.ai, OpenAI, SemiAnalysis

While tokenizers and reasoning lengths can differ between models, we’re keeping the token count constant to demonstrate the point: offensive cyber work can be executed with a capable model for a fraction of the frontier price.

The Self-Hosted GLM Bill

Renting GPUs to self-host GLM makes this almost unbelievably cheap. Using the same Monte Carlo simulation as before, we take each data point and match it to the most efficient serving configuration that satisfies the interactivity SLO, pulling together InferenceX data with our in-house rental pricing dataset. For example, if the sampled scenario requires 50 tokens/s/agent, we find the configuration with P90 interactivity at least 50 that maximizes tokens/TCO. In practice, 82.8% of draws end up using a certain GB300 Dynamo TRTLLM recipe that provides P90 interactivity of 93.985 tokens/s/user.

Source: InferenceX Dashboard

Note that GLM 5.2 and GLM 5.3 have the same architecture and therefore the same inference economics, differing only in post-training.

This is more than enough to satisfy most scenarios in our simulation, and it comes with such high throughput that its perf per dollar is extremely attractive, even though GB300 is the most expensive chip on the market. GB200, B300, and MI355X were also used for a small number of scenarios each.

Overall, using the most cost-effective setup for each draw yields a median self-hosted cost of $12.3k for the agent swarm. This uses today’s median 3-year rental price for each chip, such as $6.20 per GB300-hour. This does not imply that open-source servers make 90% margins on GLM 5.3, since they do not see 100% utilization. If we were to run these numbers again without 100% utilization, the idle capacity would count directly against the cost.

Still, it’s easier to keep utilization high when you’re using your fleet to serve your own workloads rather than trying to predict when customer requests will come in, so it’s reasonable to assume high utilization for an attacker using their own hardware. And there’s further reason to believe this estimate is a ceiling. In contrast to chatbot scenarios, where a human impatiently awaits the model’s response, highly agentic cyber tasks often run completely asynchronously and face little pressure to provide many tokens/s/agent. They can therefore be optimized for total throughput, and interactivity SLOs much worse than the ones we considered might suffice. It makes sense to move quickly once unpermitted access is achieved, but there is an asymmetry here that cuts in favor of the attacker, who can probe every chink in the armor at their leisure with a configuration optimized for total throughput.

It’s worth bearing in mind that there’s a selection effect at play here: we’re carefully analyzing one of the most famous cyberattacks on record, but there are surely countless swarms that died anonymously after bearing no fruit. The error rate must be considered as part of the overall cost of carrying out agentic cyberattacks.

The Best Defense is a Good Defense

The best public models are a few months behind the best internal models because OpenAI and Anthropic delay release for testing. Then, even when the frontier models are released, they give a wide berth to anything cyber-related. This means that cyber defenders are mostly relegated to third-tier, open-source models to protect themselves. The open-source models are very good and quickly improving, but it is undeniable that Mythos and Astra are more capable. This dynamic is exemplified in a perverse detail from Hugging Face’s report: while they were under attack from a frontier model, cyber guardrails kept them from using frontier models to investigate or defend.

Claude Opus 4.8 refused as Hugging Face tried to analyze OpenAI’s cyberattack.Source: Hugging Face

Our experience using these models for ClusterMAX and InferenceX is similar. Models refuse to help with quotidian kernel development, rebooting machines, or even explaining basic security concepts to help neoclouds harden their tenant isolation. Frontier labs are trying to solve this in an ad-hoc manner: Glasswing, Daybreak, Fairwind, and an alphabet soup of more than a dozen other programs give “trusted partners” relaxed guardrails so they can use the best models in their defensive work.

It is too early to say anything definite about the equilibrium in the cyber defense market. For now, the most naïve conclusions are the most reliable. The cost of carrying out sophisticated cyber attacks has fallen precipitously, implying a concomitant increase in the importance of defense. It should be emphasized that cyberattacks lie along a spectrum of difficulty, and some points on the spectrum may become cheaper to defend while others become more expensive. On one end is pure credential exploitation: someone finds a password or an access token lying around online, immediately granting them improper access to systems, even if the systems themselves are properly configured. On the other end are intricate zero-days that take advantage of race conditions, buffer overflows, and other low-level details of software and hardware that even a rigorous testing process could miss. The Hugging Face attack chained together exploits of all sorts: ingenious reverse-engineering as well as ordinary poor hygiene.

The simplest attacks are cheaper than ever to carry out and also cheaper than ever to defend against. Open-source models are perfectly good at superficial vulnerability scans, which often catch the most blatant mistakes. Firms no longer need to vet and hire security engineers to be diligent in keeping package versions up-to-date or to dig around online to see whether their credentials have leaked anywhere. The security firm Gambit wrote a post this September about its discovery of threat actors using three open-source AI harnesses in tandem, spending $25.46 on average to try to compromise websites. It’s never been cheaper to go around shaking door handles, checking whether they’re locked; and it’s never been cheaper to lock them. We expect a bear-in-the-woods dynamic to play out to some extent: you don’t need to outrun the bear, you only need to outrun your hiking partner. If attackers are saturated with lucrative targets for $25, firms should do the basics right and make sure they’re not the easiest game in town.

At the other end of the spectrum, models are capable of genuinely innovative and subtle attacks, and it’s hard to put an upper bound on the cost of making one’s codebase invulnerable to superintelligence. Bank of America reports that they hired 1,000 contractors to patch vulnerabilities discovered by Mythos during Project Glasswing. Its CEO was adamant that this was a one-off patch, rather than a regular expense going forward. On the other hand, leading cybersecurity firm Palo Alto Networks announced this September a product called “Unit 42 Continuous Frontier AI Defense,” pushing the thesis that perpetual and proactive offensive testing is necessary to protect against agentic threats. Security firm Tenable landed somewhere in between in a thoughtful post about its experience with Mythos, saying that the workflows they settled on are “best used for periodic testing.” Cyber damage is fat-tailed, so it’s futile to try to guess precisely what the cost and benefits of this new paradigm are. There is no question, however, that some frontier AI spend will be essential to staying afloat, and that lax security postures will be exploited more ruthlessly than before.
