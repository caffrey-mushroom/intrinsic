What Drove Thursday’s Selloff

Hedge funds had been cutting exposure throughout October when the Texas election raised concerns about further delays to the data center buildout. Thursday’s selling also resembled the earlier unwinds around SALP in July and VARA in October, although no large fund liquidation has been confirmed this time.

Retail investors had kept buying through the decline, putting roughly $33B into stocks over 22 consecutive sessions of net purchases. On Thursday, they switched to selling. Vanda recorded $265M of net outflows from individual stocks, the largest since March 2020.

The whole event was then amplified by systematic funds. Volatility control strategies size their equity exposure to a volatility target, so when volatility spikes they sell no matter what the price is. Leveraged ETFs rebalance daily to keep their stated multiple, which means selling into a falling close. The further it dropped, the more both of them had to sell.

Hedge funds spent all of October cutting their leverage, and Morgan Stanley called it the largest active unwinding at stock picking funds in over a decade. By the time Thursday arrived there was simply less borrowed money left to blow up.

One bank executive told the WSJ:

“Thursday could have generated even steeper losses, had so many funds not reduced their use of leverage, or money borrowed to amplify their bets, in prior weeks.”

Which leaves us with a market carrying almost no borrowed money, short positions at their most crowded in five years according to Goldman, and buyback windows reopening on Monday.

JP Morgan thinks positioning has reset enough for a squeeze into year end. And we agree. We’re watching whether rallies actually hold into the close this time, instead of fading the way every single one did in October.

Texas Turning Blue

Frozen permits are the main fear around the democrat being elected as a governor. A data center that can’t get permitted doesn’t get built, and therefore doesn’t collect a dollar from tenants.

However, Abbott froze those permits back in August. Several operators refused to tell the state how much power and water they planned to use. That left regulators unable to work out how much of the 474 GW sitting in the connection queue was real.

So Abbott ordered an audit on August 3 and stopped new grid connections. In September he stopped new environmental permits too.

Hinojosa inherits this freeze on January 19. What the market is pricing now is her keeping it going and stacking conditions on top of it. Her conditions for lifting the freeze are:

→ Proof that households won’t pay more for power or water → Water contracts void if they threaten local supply → No NDAs between data centers and local governments → An end to subsidies → Union labor on construction → Local approval for new sites

Every one of those either makes a project cost more or take longer.

Her power on day one is limited though. She can name the PUCT chair and fill one open seat. She can’t remove the other commissioners, who sit on six year terms. A statutory ban or repealing the tax breaks needs the legislature, and Republicans kept the Senate 19 to 12.

She also can’t touch a project that’s already under construction, because those permits are issued and done.

Lumentum and Marvell

Marvell is paying for Lumentum in its own stock, 4.5 shares for every Lumentum share, with no cash, and no collar.

A collar would hand over extra shares if Marvell dropped past a set level, keeping the value of the offer steady. This deal doesn’t have one, so every dollar Marvell loses comes straight out of what Lumentum holders receive.

Marvell is down 43.9% since the deal leaked. The offer has gone from roughly $1,132 a share to $635.

We want this deal to fail. Lumentum’s September quarter grew 137% and its lasers are sold out. Management guided December around 8% above where the street was. The break fee is $2.9B, about $32 a share. Cheap enough that another buyer isn’t priced out of trying.

The Information made exactly that argument on Wednesday and named AMD, Intel and possibly Nvidia. Nobody has been reported in talks though, so treat it as a possibility and nothing more.

CLEAR’s Quarter

CLEAR crushed its own revenue guide and raised its free cash flow outlook to at least $490M. The stock jumped 24% in a day.

We put CLEAR in our human identity report on October 30, with a $45.00 working price. On Friday, the stock closed at $55.85 which was our base case.

CLEAR grew revenue 27.8% in a quarter when national TSA volume fell 2%, and per the 10-Q most of that still comes from the airports. However, we care much more about the CLEAR1 platform. Bookings there came in close to three times last year’s level, which is our thesis playing out.

The product lets companies check that whoever is on the other end is a real human. CrowdStrike now runs a live CLEAR check inside Falcon when a login looks suspicious, and it’s already in almost 10% of US healthcare systems.

The company doesn’t break out what CLEAR1 earns, and the Q4 guide leaves out any large contract it signs. Anything that does land is incremental to what they’ve already guided.

OneSpan, another name from that report, jumped 27% on its own results and has given back about 16% since.
