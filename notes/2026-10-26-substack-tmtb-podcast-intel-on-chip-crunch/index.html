01 Financing gets harder, not impossible

Rasgon frames financing as one of several constraints — alongside power, supply, and semicap tools — now moderating AI buildout pace, noting the AI stocks are still up a lot but well off their peaks, a sign the market fears the buildout’s peak may be approaching. He observes hyperscalers, once the most cash-generative businesses in history, are now burning cash and probably headed for equity raises (Meta’s new share class structure would enable one, and Google has raised); CoreWeave’s unsecured bonds, which moderator Austin Lyons cited at 15% yields, signal where new issuance would price. Rasgon argues constraints aren’t purely bad — they prevent the kind of overbuild seen in the dot-com era’s unused dark fiber — and could produce a steadier, longer buildout cycle by better matching supply to demand.

Patel adds that financing has bifurcated: any cloud could get capital a year or two ago, but now neoclouds, which he says account for nearly half of what’s being built, can’t build without high-grade offtake, and even offtake from Anthropic or OpenAI gets priced at 13% to 15% debt. He cites Volta’s cluster build for Anthropic, where a 12% coupon bond sold at 95 cents effectively yields ~15%. This locks out neoclouds serving smaller enterprises and startups unless they strike structured deals — Nvidia’s revenue-share arrangements with Sharon AI, Fermi, and Lambda, and Broadcom’s rent-to-own chip leasing for Anthropic, are examples of chip vendors backstopping financing to keep capacity growing. Patel puts Meta’s compute and data-center commitments at $1.4 trillion and calls financing part of a “holy trinity” alongside land/power/shell and chips.

02 Ranking the real bottlenecks

Patel ranks substrates as the top constraint, ahead of memory, then optics/co-packaged optics (CPO) ramp — he says even one major company couldn’t satisfy 2028 CPO volumes given laser and fiber-attach limits. He argues power is a less severe bottleneck than popularly believed, since roughly 50 gigawatts of data centers are being built globally next year (7-8 in China, ~35 in the US and the rest worldwide, with Australia, India and Malaysia among the big places), and operators can work around power limits with on-site turbines, reciprocating engines, or fuel cells faster than they can fix semiconductor supply chains.

Rasgon largely agrees but stresses memory and wafer fab equipment (WFE) as constraints. He notes the trade ratio for high-bandwidth memory requires three to four times as many wafers per gigabyte versus standard DDR5, and that a company’s WFE guidance of above $200 billion for next year is about as high as he thinks possible given full clean rooms, though investors expected more — WFE estimates have already risen from roughly $120 billion early this year to a projected ~$155 billion. He expects tightness to persist through 2028, with 2029 a demand question.

03 Pricing power discovered during COVID

Rasgon recounts his own McKinsey research from years ago, which by his recollection showed the semiconductor industry destroying about $5 billion in aggregate economic profit from roughly 1996 to 2004 ($50 billion excluding Intel), meaning nearly all value flowed to customers. COVID changed that: input cost increases were fully passed through, and industry blended ASPs rose roughly 50% from 2019 to 2023/2024 even as unit shipments stayed flat — a dynamic he says is now playing out at an even greater intensity, with 70-80% of this year’s roughly 100% industry revenue growth (toward $1.5 trillion) coming from memory ASPs.

Patel notes some players flex pricing power opportunistically (memory firms swung from negative margins a few years ago to high margins, rewarding themselves on the upside after being squeezed in the downturn) while others like TSMC climbed steadily from 45% to 60% gross margin without giving much back in downturns. He estimates building a gigawatt of compute rose from roughly $40 billion in 2024 to over $60 billion now, though stripping out chip-vendor margin (Nvidia, Broadcom, Marvell) would put raw cost near $20 billion — meaning five gigawatts could theoretically be funded by one gigawatt of inference revenue. He reads Anthropic’s recent disclosures as roughly $70-80 billion per gigawatt of inference capacity.

04 Nvidia and Broadcom’s margin defense

Rasgon frames Nvidia’s margin durability as a function of performance-per-dollar gains outpacing ASP increases: Ampere-to-Hopper ASPs roughly doubled while performance rose ~10x; Blackwell ASPs rose ~50% while performance gained 3-30x; Rubin rack costs are probably doubling and cost per gigawatt is rising (about $25 billion for Blackwell and $40 billion for Rubin, as he recalls Nvidia’s figures) against another ~10x performance gain. He cites Nvidia chief scientist Bill Dally’s 2023 Hot Chips keynote target of a millionfold GPU performance improvement over ten years, following a thousandfold gain in the prior decade, as the benchmark that must hold for margins to stay defensible.

Patel says Nvidia and Broadcom trade at their lowest multiples basically ever, and Rasgon says Nvidia had traded cheaper relative to the SOX semiconductor index on only 13 days in the 10 years before a note he wrote nine or 10 months ago, and it’s cheaper today. Patel ties the discount to fears that big customers like Meta and Google will move away from Broadcom or stop buying Nvidia and insource, and says the shift has started: Nvidia’s slice of the industry’s CoWoS wafers and HBM fell in 2026 and falls again next year as Google, Amazon and AMD take share, though its earnings still double.

05 Custom silicon and enterprise adoption

Patel describes OpenAI’s first-generation custom chip beating Blackwell when SemiAnalysis ran open-source Chinese models on it with its InferenceX benchmark in OpenAI’s lab, a chip designed with heavy AI assistance; Anthropic aims to develop its own chip in half OpenAI’s development time, while Google’s TPU, Amazon’s Trainium (now fully usable in PyTorch), Meta, and Microsoft all pursue aggressive custom-silicon roadmaps. He notes the time to get new hardware running inference software on InferenceX has compressed dramatically — about six months for Nvidia and six to nine for AMD, a few months each for Google and Amazon, and a month and a half for OpenAI — suggesting chip design itself may become increasingly commoditized like software, which would pressure Nvidia and Broadcom margins longer-term even as both maintain strong positioning for the next two years.

On enterprise adoption, Rasgon identifies AI coding as the clear killer application with vertical revenue growth, and shares an anecdote of an analyst using Claude to one-shot a ten-year financial model in an hour and 20 minutes — work that would otherwise take a week. He frames the broader debate as whether AI spending generates measurable return, concluding anecdotal evidence leans positive though adoption remains early. Patel adds that enterprises will mostly buy tokens rather than deploy their own hardware or models, arguing scale economics push even pharma companies and hedge funds toward consuming AI as a service rather than running infrastructure themselves, continuing a decades-long industry shift toward cloud consumption.

01 Why he cut AI hardware exposure

Citrini says his fund largely exited AI infrastructure and semiconductor positions starting around March, shifting toward what he calls “agentic utilities” — companies benefiting from actual AI usage rather than the capex buildout itself. He says he remains constructive on NVIDIA individually, but argues the infrastructure trade now requires underwriting aggressive assumptions, whereas usage-driven plays can be justified more simply. He notes that had his portfolio stayed as infrastructure-heavy as it was in April/May, it would likely be down ~50%; separately, Farley noted semiconductor stocks fell nine consecutive days in October, a streak he said was last seen in 2006.

02 The reflexivity thesis

Citrini’s central macro argument is that AI spending has moved from being funded by hyperscaler free cash flow — a dynamic that gave the trade a fundamental floor regardless of macro shocks — to being funded increasingly by equity and debt raised specifically against AI growth expectations. This creates reflexivity: if market sentiment sours, the incremental dollars financing buildout can simply dry up, independent of whether AI itself is getting more useful. He also cites doubts about whether token-cost deflation is spurring proportional demand growth, pointing to an Anthropic Economic Index finding that a 10% cost reduction correlated with only a 3% usage increase — meaning Jevons paradox is not holding for tokens right now, in his view. He stresses this is a short-term concern, not a reversal of his long-term AI thesis.

03 The scorpion antivenom experiment

To test AI’s real capability ceiling, Citrini’s team partnered with Anthropic (via an introduction from Anthropic’s Sholto Douglas to its life-sciences team) and used Anthropic’s internal research model (which he says was more powerful than anything public at the time) to design protein mini-binders for scorpion antivenom. Working under supervision at Anthropic’s offices, the model ran largely autonomously over 64 hours, spinning up 308 sub-agents, producing a large batch of designs, and even independently emailing the contract research organization (Twist) to resolve a technical question before continuing its work. Of the designs sent to two CROs, 17 bound to toxin targets, including seven against Cn2, a toxin specific to a Mexican scorpion; the top candidate competed with an established neutralizing antibody close to the intended binding site, though the team published before the further round of lab work that would show whether it actually neutralizes the toxin. The lab validation phase took 39 days, versus 64 hours for AI-driven design, illustrating that the time from idea to experiment is compressing dramatically, while wet-lab biology (cell growth, toxicology windows) remains an irreducible, time-bound process.

04 Investment implications: long experiments, monkeys as bottleneck

From this, Citrini concludes investors should be long experiments rather than long drug-discovery hype. He estimates AI can reduce roughly 20% of the full drug-design-to-clinical-trial pipeline, with 80% irreducible. One of his favorites is Charles River Laboratories, where he says about 60% of revenue comes from discovery and safety assessment — early-stage experiments, not necessarily monkeys — which benefit from rising preclinical order volume. He flags lab automation and consumables suppliers as additional beneficiaries. The single most supply-constrained link, he says, is monkeys for preclinical/toxicology testing — animal studies remain essential precisely because more experimental data, not less, is needed to eventually build computer simulations that could reduce animal testing long-term. He also notes early positive signs from FDA reform efforts (including an expedited IND pilot program) aimed at cutting Phase 1 timelines, after the U.S. fell behind China and Australia on speed.

05 Biopharma dispersion: commoditization risk

Citrini is bearish on “me-too” biopharma companies betting on single assets in well-understood mechanisms (drawing a GLP-1-era analogy), arguing that as AI compresses the design-to-candidate pipeline, early-stage asset value will erode and the get-acquired business model will face commoditization pressure. He’s more constructive on large acquirers like Eli Lilly, citing its TuneLab proprietary AI drug-discovery platform and historical execution on licensing/M&A, as a structural beneficiary of commoditized early-stage assets rather than a victim of it. He also revisits a prior bearish homebuilders call, now seeing potential for a rebound contingent on his relatively bullish rate view.

06 Hyperscaler positioning: long Meta, short Google

Citrini turned bullish on Meta this summer on the thesis that its first-party data (Instagram/WhatsApp/Facebook, ARPU near $200+) combined with the Muse product could drive roughly a 4x ARPU increase by 2030. He’s paired this with a short on Alphabet, citing an underappreciated DeepMind talent exodus to competitors and lingering, though largely priced-out, search-disruption risk as his own search habits have shifted away from Google. On CoreWeave, he says market pricing implying a 60% chance of default by 2032 was overblown — he expects any AI-sentiment unwind to arrive gradually, through downward ARR revisions, rather than a sudden CoreWeave-style default shock.

07 Revisiting the software-disruption thesis

Asked about his viral 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis piece on AI’s impact on software and employment — which Farley says Blackstone’s Steve Schwarzman brought up unprompted, and which Citrini says was viewed about 100 million times — Citrini says he still expects meaningful software disruption but not a straight-line collapse, comparing it to BlackBerry post-iPhone or Macy’s post-Amazon, where shorting becomes crowded and profitable without requiring permanent ownership avoidance. He argues labs like OpenAI and Anthropic will be incentivized to release products that close the gap between AI capability and actual usage, potentially displacing software spend, and that some enterprise spending growth will come from AI replacing headcount rather than pure productivity gains ; if AI has had no impact on unemployment by mid-to-late 2027, he says, he’ll accept that the piece was wrong. On moats, he says AI switching costs are low and most moats will stem from specialization (e.g., an edge in biology-specific tools) rather than defensibility.

THE SIGNAL

  • Nadella calls MAI licensed weights a choice to run a plain software business, not a network-effects intelligence business, in line with his view that the value created by a customer’s usage should stay with that customer (our read: a pitch against data-flywheel labs).
  • At Unilever, Microsoft forward-deployed engineers built the trade-claims system, then Unilever’s own IT team took it over; Nadella expects classic systems integration to become this FDE model (our read: pressure on SI economics).
  • Nadella says software development demand is proving highly elastic because IT backlogs were far bigger than anyone knew, and AI is helping chip teams tape out faster, maybe with a working first spin.

01 Zero-trust over model trust

Nadella’s starting position is that increasingly capable models cannot be assumed aligned, so the correct posture is a zero-trust architecture built around the model rather than reliance on model-provider disclosures. He distinguishes this “engineered security” layer from unresolved AI safety science, arguing alignment likely needs to be embedded in training — possibly pretraining — rather than achieved via reinforcement learning after the fact. Microsoft’s answer is two systems it has built, including Agent 365: every agent gets an identity, all tool calls, model calls, and chain-of-thought get logged in tamper-proof form, and behavior is monitored against policy. He insists chain-of-thought logs should be in English, inspectable by trusted partners, and checkable using multiple models, warning that if model companies conceal unfaithful chain-of-thought, trust deteriorates further — a dynamic he says could push more use toward open-weight models.

02 Open-source, China, and the next two years

Asked whether China’s dominance in open-source AI poses a safety risk, Nadella argues an unaligned open-weight model would be dangerous to its creators too, so no nation benefits from skipping alignment — while noting he’s encouraged by Western open-weight progress (citing Jensen and others) and previewing Microsoft’s own MAI models as “licensed weights.” On the question of open-versus-closed traffic over the next 24 months, Nadella expects most capability curves to saturate, after which open weights or cheaper closed models become sufficient — especially once companies can train on their own usage traces rather than needing the newest frontier model. He expects frontier models to retain a market (he floats science as the next frontier battleground) but predicts the bulk of token usage becomes price-competitive commodity usage, pointing to Copilot’s own mix of cheap closed and open-weight models, and new local sub-agent “swarm” techniques in GitHub.

03 Firms owning their own intelligence

Nadella’s central structural argument, invoking Hayek’s view that economies run on distributed tacit knowledge, is that no single firm or intelligence can dominate, and surplus value from AI use must stay with the firm generating it rather than leaking to model providers. He calls this the reverse information paradox (a twist on Kenneth Arrow’s information paradox): historically sellers were harmed by disclosure, but now buyers need protection because even passive usage signals (thumbs up/down) create value capture for the model company instead of the customer. His proposed mechanism is that firms encode value not just in model weights but in context, evals, and especially private benchmarks — he predicts every company will eventually maintain its own proprietary eval that outside models compete to “hill climb” against, calling this core IP. Stebbings asks whether this remains confined to sophisticated AI-forward firms (Harvey, Legora-type companies); Nadella says diffusion is inevitable, comparing it to PC adoption in the late 1980s/90s and early cloud-native labeling, predicting “AI native” will be passé within five years.

04 Implementation economy and stack structure

Nadella describes an emerging implementation economy blending technical and business consulting, distinct from classic systems integration — illustrated by a Unilever case where an agentic system for order-to-cash and trade claims started on a frontier model and was later substituted with a cheaper MAI model once the client controlled its own training traces. On whether winning requires full vertical integration from chips to models to applications, Nadella says structure depends on industry dynamics; he sees five or six competing closed models and five or six open-weight models, enabling routing/decision-engine approaches rather than one vertically integrated winner, and says competition is intensifying at every layer (silicon, systems, models, apps) rather than consolidating — though he concedes a single vertically integrated winner remains a possible, if unlikely, outcome.

05 Growth, compute limits, and spreading beyond coding

On $100 billion training runs, Nadella ties feasibility to R&D spending as a share of revenue (he cites 15-20% as typical for R&D-heavy firms) and ultimately to world GDP growth, which he says has hovered near 2% a year for a long period; he says AI could inflect that rate and hopes it does, pointing to Copilot’s deployment in the UK’s NHS as a case where AI could simultaneously improve outcomes and cut healthcare costs. He flags compute supply constraints — power and silicon shortages, long lead times for new generation and fabs — as unresolved unknowns nobody can currently forecast. Asked whether AI’s coding breakthroughs (driven by RL with verifiable rewards) will extend to sales, finance, and marketing, Nadella says it’s harder to find verifiable rewards in general knowledge work but sees evidence it’s happening in contact centers, supply chains, and order-to-cash systems, and separately highlights silicon design, where teams can tape out faster, with Rolls-Royce’s maintenance scheduling also raised as a long-standing optimization problem AI can now tackle. In a closing quickfire round, Nadella mentions his daily incline-walking exercise routine, repeats that his changed view is on alignment not emerging automatically, predicts private AI benchmarks will define what a firm is within five years, and says his biggest hope is that AI diffuses globally as a true growth-lifting general-purpose technology, referencing his father’s work as a development economist on global convergence growth.

01 The bubble framework

Noble, a former Fidelity Overseas Fund manager under Peter Lynch, argues AI repeats the pattern of historic manias (South Sea Bubble, Dutch tulip mania, dot-com) where a genuine technology is real but asset prices detach from unit economics. He argues the internet bulls of 1999 were right about traffic growth (which he says compounded roughly 43%/year) but that didn’t save Nortel, Lucent, or Global Crossing shareholders — a parallel he draws to AI today. His central claim is that AI pricing is collapsing (new cheaper models weekly, Chinese open models allegedly matching performance at ~95% less cost) while balance sheets and debt levels deteriorate simultaneously, which he views as unsustainable. He points to slowing growth signals — OpenAI’s IPO delay to 2027, uncertainty over Anthropic’s IPO timing (the episode was recorded October 21, before the S-1), and what he calls a negative market reaction to an OpenAI revenue figure he says was merely restated — as evidence an awakening phase has begun, paraphrasing Soros on recognizing when a false belief gets exposed.

02 CoreWeave, Tesla, SpaceX shorts

Noble’s biggest position is short CoreWeave, which he likens to WeWork with GPUs or Hertz with depreciating assets: the company holds a pile of GPUs funded by debt, and whether the useful life of chips is four, six, or ten years is central to solvency. Farley notes implied default odds on CoreWeave’s 2032 notes near 60%; Noble says CoreWeave may avoid bankruptcy because NVIDIA could buy it in a take-under to protect the ecosystem, which would still leave the equity worth a fraction of today’s price. He is also short Tesla, arguing the company’s $38 billion cumulative profit since inception doesn’t justify its roughly $1.5 trillion valuation, and points to 14 straight years of missed self-driving promises, arguing Tesla has earned its cost of capital in only one year. On SpaceX, he argues the roughly $2.2 trillion valuation implies the company trades near 100-120x revenue, and describes it as a cash-flow-negative AI business with a rocket company attached to Starlink, estimating the rocket business and Starlink combined are worth a few hundred billion at most. Farley pushes back, noting SpaceX’s Superintelligence unit was positive even on a GAAP basis last quarter with strong growth, and cites Musk’s claim that compute payback is roughly one year; Noble dismisses annualized “run-rate” framing as potentially misleading given current pricing may not be sustainable.

03 Why not NVIDIA or Micron

Noble says he is not short NVIDIA because better shorts exist (CoreWeave, IREN), though he suspects NVIDIA will eventually prove to be a great short. He criticizes Micron’s current 85% gross margin as abnormal and argues investors should model normalized margins rather than extrapolate current peak earnings; he compares semiconductors to the shipping industry, where capital-intensive players see that prices are temporary but depreciation and debt are permanent, which he thinks also applies to AI data centers.

04 Gold, energy, and macro backdrop

Noble is long gold and gold miners (naming SSR as a favorite) as an inflation/debasement hedge against what he calls irresponsible monetary and fiscal policy, arguing fiat currencies are in terminal decline. He is also bullish energy, citing roughly 15 years of underinvestment, a gold-to-oil ratio implying oil should be far higher, and favors Schlumberger, citing a recent interviewee who called SLB perhaps the best AI play because of the data it owns. He separately argues rising bond yields reflect term-premium and fiscal-sustainability concerns rather than inflation alone, and credits Michael Howell’s liquidity framework as broadly correct over recent years.

05 Market structure and positioning

Noble argues market breadth is poor — the average stock allegedly well down while a handful of names hold up indices — and claims government incentives to manage asset prices (oil down, stocks up) are unusually high before elections and will diminish afterward, which he expects raises volatility. He discloses his 2026 model performance as longs up roughly 26% and shorts down roughly 41% annualized on a small sample of 35-40 picks, while acknowledging 2022 was his worst year. He closes by criticizing social-media figures he nicknames “compounder bro” and “bottleneck bro” who he says lack fundamental analysis skills, predicting history will not be kind to them as the cycle turns.

01 Model progress and speed

Ari Weinstein attributes recent gains mainly to the newest model, referred to as Astra, which he says is marginally but meaningfully faster because it uses fewer thinking tokens, and feels more trustworthy at the margins — a reliability edge he describes as hard to perceive but important for felt trust. James Sun adds that Astra is OpenAI’s most aligned model yet, citing its system card showing improved resistance to prompt injection and fewer unintended actions, a property he ties directly to enterprise comfort with sensitive tasks like procurement and vendor communication. Both note creative gains, including a demo of the model drawing in Microsoft Paint and internally beating earlier models at video games, plus posts from users claiming it beat Fallout in about thirty hours.

02 Consumer vs. enterprise priorities

Sun, who leans enterprise, says most large companies already see obvious use cases in legacy software accumulated over fifteen or twenty years that lacks MCP tooling, so the open question is organizational readiness more than feasibility. Weinstein, who leans consumer, frames his side as finding genuinely new experiences, exemplified by Dots, where each user gets a dedicated cloud computer that can browse, buy items, or book appointments autonomously. Sun describes using Dots proactively at OpenAI: it flags missed calendar invites from external email, filters Slack noise by reporting-chain relevance, and can autonomously pull Sentry logs and feed them into Codex for triage.

03 Enterprise trust and guardrails

Sun describes two layers OpenAI built to earn enterprise trust: deterministic sandboxing (restricting which apps, sites, or data sources an agent may touch) and tunable human-in-the-loop checkpoints, such as requiring approval for any file upload/download or new website visit. OpenAI’s Guardian system is described as a model that reviews every computer-use action for safety and intent-alignment before execution. Weinstein adds UX-level trust mechanisms — a visible, animated cursor so users can watch the agent work, and default permission prompts before using new applications — intended to make the experience feel controllable during a user’s early trust-building period.

04 Where automation lands first

Sun splits promising use cases into two buckets: tedious system-of-record automation (ERPs, CRMs, procurement — citing a company that automated spreadsheet-to-vendor-outreach workflows — and finance reconciliation across ledgers lacking APIs) versus more ambitious creative work such as circuit design, hardware engineering, game design, and architecture, which enterprises aren’t yet reaching for. On the open web, he notes friction with ad-funded sites, since an agent’s visit does not generate the same ad revenue as a human’s, and points to the WebMCP standard, supported in OpenAI’s in-app browser, as a way for sites to let humans and agents share an interface rather than being fully disintermediated.

05 Legacy software versus AI-native future

Both guests expect legacy and AI-native software to coexist for a long time: enormous amounts of production software are decades old and won’t disappear overnight, even as computer use lowers the cost of building new software. Weinstein argues the existence of computer use pressures legacy vendors to make their software work better with agents by default. Sun pushes back against a fully agent-to-agent internet, arguing craftsmanship and personalized UI still matter, illustrating with a personal project building a tailored game-training app for his wife, and predicts a future where bespoke software can be built quickly for niche use cases.

06 Founder advice and what’s next

Sun, a former startup founder, tells founders the moat today is speed and pain — deep vertical expertise, go-to-market understanding, and servicing underserved industries, citing a restaurant-group example chaining vision, procurement software, and ERP scheduling via computer use — remain wide open even as OpenAI’s own teams feel stretched. Looking ahead, Weinstein highlights pushing toward superhuman speed and blending background automation with real-time human-agent collaboration, while Sun’s priority is making the experience more coherent across today’s fragmented surfaces (Chrome extension, in-app browser, cloud computer each with separate logins), especially as mobile becomes the primary target for giving ordinary consumers a powerful, easy-to-use computer.
