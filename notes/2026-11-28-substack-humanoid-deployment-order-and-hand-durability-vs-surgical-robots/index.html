Tesla said last month that it had started production of its humanoid robot Optimus. But it put the handover to outside companies at around the end of next year, and the part of the design it named as still needing work was the hand. I was curious where and how much a humanoid can work, so I met a medical robot engineer who has built endoscope robots and blood-draw robots and asked. I asked what a humanoid can do in the operating room, the hospital, and the home, and I checked whether the answers I heard were right by looking up filings, regulatory documents, and papers. Factories and warehouses I looked into separately through company announcements and news reports. Along the way I found one number that hadn’t come up in the dialogue, which is the use count fixed for a surgical robot’s instruments. The conclusion of the dialogue is that spreading into work that needs dexterous hands will take longer than the schedules the companies talk about, and that in the meantime robots built for a single job make money first.

1. The Hand Design That Is Still Changing

In the third-quarter earnings materials it released on October 21, Tesla wrote that Optimus production had started at the Fremont factory. The schedule came out on the earnings call the same day. According to a transcript taken down by a third party, Elon Musk expects AI, electromechanical design, reliability, and the robot’s service life to be solved by around the end of next year, and said this is when Optimus gets handed over to companies outside Tesla. Musk also said the robot can’t be allowed to wear out in a month.

So where is the design still changing? When an analyst asked this, the answer was that it’s mostly the hand, and within the hand the fingers. Musk said it’s hard to build a hand that holds up to wear and tear for a long time and is still soft enough to pick up delicate objects, and added that a human hand heals itself but a robot can’t. The engineer in charge of Optimus also answered that the hand is among the hardest parts of the hardware and is more complex than the actuators used for walking.

There was a similar remark a year earlier. On the October 2025 earnings call, Musk said the Optimus hand and forearm are harder, from an electromechanical standpoint, than all the rest of the robot.

After running robots at a BMW plant for more than 1,250 hours, Figure wrote in a post that hardware failures were few, but that among them the forearm was the most vulnerable. The explanation attached is that parts have to be packed in densely, the wrist has to move in three directions, and heat is hard to get out. It said it reworked the wrist’s electronics architecture for the next generation.

A concept diagram for readers seeing a humanoid’s body for the first time. The joints across the whole body, the actuator that moves one joint (motor, reducer, encoder), and a hand whose fingers are pulled through cables by motors in the forearm

There is progress on the body side too. Tesla started production, and Figure said it had built more than 350 third-generation robots through April of this year and demonstrated raising its production rate from one a day to one an hour. The speed of building has gone up. How long a hand holds up in delicate work is still hard to confirm with published numbers.

The explanation from companies building humanoids generally goes like this. A large AI model becomes the brain, a human-shaped body uses the tools and spaces people already use, and so the size of the market is measured by human wages more than by the price of the machine. That’s also why Elon Musk and Jensen Huang keep coming back to robots. I’m someone betting that robots come after LLMs, too. A robotics researcher I met in Austin a while ago said AI eventually converges in robots, and I was fairly persuaded.

Still, news on the brain side comes out every week, while there aren’t many pieces that properly cover the body side. In May, in [[Investment Map] 20 Companies in the Humanoid Cycle: From Rare Earth Magnets to Vision Language Action, I followed the parts that go into one humanoid from magnets to reducers and actuators and sorted out twenty listed companies. That piece looked at who supplies what, and it couldn’t cover how long those parts hold up in actual work. This time I wanted to know how much the hand’s durability and force sensing limit actual work.

2. Who I Asked

The person I met is a medical robot engineer with experience in the mechanism design and clinical development of endoscope robots and blood-draw robots. They hold a PhD and have founded a company. In their own words, they are someone who designs the hardware more than the control, meaning the mechanism. We agreed not to disclose the name or the company.

You might ask why I took humanoid questions to someone who does medical robots. A surgical robot moves its joints with cables inside a thin tube, touches the human body directly, and has been sold to hospitals that way for decades. If there’s an industry that went through the problems the humanoid hand is hitting now, I thought it would be this one.

This engineer said several times, as a premise, that they haven’t built a humanoid themselves. They also said optics and learning algorithms are outside their field. So instead of carrying the answers over as I heard them, I looked up filings, regulatory documents, and papers to check whether they were right. There were places where the dialogue’s answer and the public record differed, and in those places I followed the public record. I tidied up the wording of the key questions and answers from the dialogue so they read easily, and some of the questions were rewritten to fit the topic. The outlook on the order of the areas and on timing is what I put together from the dialogue and the public record.

Q. Some say the hardware is done and what’s left is AI. Do you agree?

A. I don’t think it’s done. The hand still seems far off to me. It’s true that AI matters. AI has to decide how to move the hand, and with so many degrees of freedom, that side is a hard area too.

3. What to Know First: Degrees of Freedom, Cables, Sensors

To understand this answer you first need to know the term degrees of freedom. Degrees of freedom is the number of directions in which the joints can move independently. To bring an object to the position you want at the angle you want, you need three for position and three for rotation, six degrees of freedom in total. Six degrees of freedom is common for factory robot arms, and there are also seven-degree-of-freedom arms with one more, like a human arm. Think of picking up a cup. A six-degree-of-freedom arm has no room to move just the elbow while the hand stays lined up with the cup. Give it one more and it can raise or lower the elbow with the hand left where it is. It gains room to get around obstacles.

The human arm is like that. The shoulder turns in three directions, the elbow bends, the forearm rotates, and the wrist bends in two directions, which makes seven degrees of freedom. The hand moves at every finger joint and is said to have twenty-some degrees of freedom. That’s close to three times the degrees of freedom of the whole arm, packed into the size of a palm. This is where the statement that the hand is the hardest comes from.

As degrees of freedom go up, the problem is where to put the motors that move them. One commonly used method is to group the motors in the forearm and pull the fingers with thin cables. The industry calls it tendon drive. The human hand has the same layout, since most of the muscles that move the fingers are in the forearm. Describing the hand of its robot NEO, 1X wrote that the motors are in the forearm, and the hand Tesla showed in 2024 was also reported to be a tendon design with the actuators in the forearm. Some companies put small motors and reducers inside the hand. Unitree’s hand product page highlights compact motors and reducers.

Surgical robots have used this cable for far longer. I asked why, and the answer was diameter. To treat kidney stones, an endoscope is pushed up along the ureter to the kidney, and some of these endoscopes have an outer diameter of 7.5 Fr, which converts to about 2.5mm. In the instrument this engineer built, they said steel wire was the only way to bend the tip within that thickness.

Q. Is that wire a line that carries a signal, or a part that does the moving itself?

A. Just think of it as a rubber band that doesn’t stretch. You push and pull from the back to lift the tip and let it down. There’s no motor inside the endoscope, and it’s a completely mechanical structure.

But this rubber band does stretch a little in practice. Say you pull the cable by 10 and the tip bends 90 degrees. Release it by 10 and it should come back to where it was, but it stops around halfway.

Q. If you release it and it doesn’t come back, doesn’t that mean it’s broken?

A. No, it’s a completely expected phenomenon. The cable stretches a little under tension, and even a little stretch changes the bending angle. So you have to pull more. It’s a very important issue in surgical robots.

A cable stretches when it takes force, and friction arises where it touches its conduit. When the pulling direction reverses, the slack part has to go taut again first, so for the same amount of pull the tip ends up in a different position going and coming back. This phenomenon is called hysteresis. Because the cable diameter is between 0.3mm and 0.5mm, a very small difference becomes a visible angle difference at the tip. It’s separate from the problem of the cable wearing out with long use, and it occurs in new instruments too.

Papers say the same thing. A 2014 paper by a team at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore opens with a sentence saying that in tendon-driven systems, cable elongation makes position control inaccurate, and it gives surgical robots as the leading example. A robot hand survey paper posted this month (not yet peer reviewed) also wrote that tendon designs have the drawbacks of friction, creep, and slack, and so need tension adjustment and calibration.

This engineer described the method of reading the tip’s motion with a sensor and correcting through control, and the method of building into the instrument a mechanism that calculates in advance how much the cable will stretch and pulls that much more. They said they chose the latter and didn’t use a sensor. They added that they like solving things without sensors.

A concept diagram comparing tendon drive in a surgical instrument and a humanoid hand. The motors at the back, the cables running through a conduit, and the joints at the tip, drawn in the same order

A cable-pulled hand has these drawbacks. So how do surgical robots, which have sold this structure for decades, deal with the parts that wear? After the dialogue I looked up Intuitive Surgical’s annual report, and it had a sentence saying a chip inside the instrument prevents it from being used beyond a set number of times.

The sentence in Intuitive Surgical’s annual report (Form 10-K) stating that a chip inside the instrument prevents use beyond a set number of times. Source: Intuitive Surgical, Form 10-K

I looked into what this use limit means and whether it applies to humanoid hands as well. And with the answers from the dialogue and the public record, I compared where a humanoid will work first among the operating room, the hospital, factories and warehouses, and the home.

4. What Surgical Robots Learned First

Intuitive Surgical, which makes the da Vinci surgical robot, wrote the following in its annual report. Each instrument contains a programmed memory chip, and this chip prevents the instrument from being used once it passes a set number of uses. The default used to be 10 uses, and in 2020 the company extended some instruments to between 12 and 18. Today the count differs by instrument. The endoscope for a model called da Vinci SP had its use count raised to 50 in last year’s clearance, and the force feedback instruments also had their counts raised in a clearance this March.

So the graspers and scissors that go on the end of a robot costing up to several million dollars have been replaced after about ten surgeries. The same report also has a sentence saying instruments and accessories have a limited life and either expire or wear out. However, the company didn’t write that cable wear is what set that count. That an instrument pulling its wrist joint with thin cables would have trouble keeping its original precision through many surgeries and cleanings is my guess based on the explanation I heard in the dialogue, and it isn’t confirmed by the public record. The practice of using a precise instrument a set number of times and then replacing it has taken hold in commercial surgical robots.

This practice is also a big business. Of last year’s $10 billion in revenue, about 60% came from instruments and accessories, and robot system revenue was a quarter. Instruments and accessories also include things like staplers, energy instruments, and drapes, so this revenue can’t all be read as replacement of cable-driven instruments. Even so, the company wrote that it earns $900 to $3,700 in instruments and accessories per procedure, and more than 3 million procedures were done with da Vinci last year.

The sensor side took time. The da Vinci received its first US FDA clearance in July 2000. The force feedback instruments, which let the surgeon feel at the controls the force of pushing and pulling on tissue, received clearance in March 2024. That’s a gap of nearly 24 whole years between the two clearances. It doesn’t mean all of this period was time spent developing the sensor, and the company said research, development, and testing for this generation took more than 10 years.

Q. Why did force feedback take this long to arrive?

A. In the end it’s because the structure is driven by wires. With a humanoid you can just put a sensor on the finger, but a surgical tool can’t be made at that thickness. It also has to go through sterilization. Surgical tools aren’t disposable, so after surgery they’re taken off and steamed at high temperature, and that ruins all the sensors.

What was said here is that designing an instrument that is sterilized and reused many times is hard. There are products with sensors that survive sterilization, so it would go too far to take it as all sensors being ruined. Instruments are cleaned, disinfected, and finally steam sterilized after every surgery. For example, the clearance document for Intuitive’s sterilization tray lists conditions of steam at 132 degrees Celsius for 4 minutes and 30 minutes of drying. If you put electronic parts at the tip, not only the sensing element but also the wiring, adhesive, and sealing have to survive this process every time. How much this constraint delayed force feedback can’t be known from the public record. This was the reason this engineer said they like solving things without sensors.

This is where the dialogue’s answer and the public record differ. This engineer guessed that since it’s hard to put a sensor at the tip, the measurement would be indirect, through the current of the motors at the back or the strain of the cable. When I looked it up, a paper supported by Intuitive wrote that a force sensor at the instrument tip measures push and pull forces in three directions, and a company patent shows a structure with strain-measuring elements attached at the distal end of the instrument shaft. Whether the product is exactly as in this patent can’t be confirmed. Still, the paper confirms that the sensor was placed toward the distal end. And the same paper states that this instrument doesn’t convey grip force or tactile sensation.

How well did it work? In the preclinical test in that paper, with 28 surgeons participating, the peak force applied to tissue fell by about 30 to 50 percent depending on the task when feedback was set to its strongest. Early studies comparing patient outcomes have started to appear too. In an esophagectomy study published this October, vocal cord dysfunction after surgery was lower on the latest-generation side that used force feedback, at about 1 in 7 patients against about 1 in 3. It was a retrospective comparison of surgeries done by one surgeon at one hospital, so the authors themselves wrote that it doesn’t establish the superiority of the platform. According to this engineer, when they ask surgeons around them, the surgeons say force feedback isn’t needed. They added that no surgeon is going to say they crush tissue every time they operate. In this year’s third quarter, more than half of newly placed da Vinci systems were the latest generation that can use these instruments, but the company hasn’t disclosed how much hospitals actually use the force feedback instruments.

Water came up too. A kidney stone procedure keeps water flowing to inflate the ureter and float the stone. The first robot they built broke down, they said, because that water got all the way into the motor. I asked whether you couldn’t just put a plastic drape over the robot, and the answer was that even with a drape, the spot that transmits the motor’s motion to the instrument has to stay open, so it isn’t fully sealed. A separate design is needed to close that gap.

A humanoid hand has fewer thickness constraints and doesn’t need sterilization, so its conditions are better. But the post Figure wrote when it launched its new robot shows it isn’t simply easy. The very post that boasted the fingertip sensor can feel forces as small as the weight of 3 grams also wrote that existing tactile sensors had limits that kept them from surviving real use, so the company made its own. I took that to mean the problem was lifespan more than sensitivity.

5. Area One: The Operating Room

Q. Could a humanoid with a good enough hand replace da Vinci?

A. On technology alone, automating da Vinci is faster. The hardware that can do surgery already exists, and it’s a situation where you only need to add judgment and motion. Da Vinci is a platform that moves the surgical tool itself, while a humanoid has to move its own body to hold the surgical tool and move it. There’s one more layer.

Q. If it gets replaced anyway, what would be the reason?

A. I think it would be price. Two humanoids come in, one holds the endoscope, and they work together on their own to do the surgery, and the price is a fraction of da Vinci’s. Then of course it gets replaced.

Besides price, the other thing they raised was setup. Da Vinci is a large machine with four arms. Before surgery, people put drapes on it, make ports in the patient’s body, and insert and position the instruments one by one by hand, and this reportedly takes more than 30 minutes. The number of people in the operating room doesn’t go down either.

When I looked, there was a similar experiment. It’s a paper a UC San Diego team published in Nature this July. With a teleoperated humanoid, they completed gallbladder removal on live pigs twice. There was no clip instrument that fit the robot, so the steps of clipping the cystic duct and tying off the artery were done by a person with conventional laparoscopy. The motivation the paper puts forward also matches the answer in the dialogue. Existing surgical robots have high purchase costs and proprietary consumable costs, so they’ve been installed only at large hospitals.

The paper also lists limitations. The robot was operated by one surgeon. The assisting work of holding the camera and retracting tissue was done mostly by a person, and a second humanoid took that job only for a short stretch of the first surgery. Sterility was maintained by putting gloves over the robot’s arms, and the authors themselves wrote that this doesn’t fully reproduce the sterile procedures needed for human surgery. There’s also a sentence saying humanoids sold today have no parts that survive high-temperature sterilization. For the surgical tools, they used manual laparoscopic instruments with a wrist joint, clamped in a custom fixture. Having the robot grip and handle tools with its own fingers wasn’t tested in this experiment.

Some are starting with the less difficult jobs inside the operating room. This September, Samsung Medical Center showed a demonstration in a simulated surgical setting where one humanoid handed over instruments and another held the laparoscope. It wasn’t performed on patients.

How far has autonomous surgery come? A Johns Hopkins team announced last year that it put a learned model on a da Vinci research platform and succeeded every time at clipping and cutting the cystic duct and vessels on eight pig gallbladders taken outside the body. The robot did those motions on its own, and people did the reloading of clips and the instrument changes. It’s one step of the surgery to remove a gallbladder, short of the whole surgery. Still, it fits the dialogue’s answer that the work of adding judgment and motion is moving ahead first on top of surgical robots.

Q. Surgical automation research has been around a long time, so why isn’t it widely used in hospitals yet?

A. The research has been going on for twenty or thirty years, and things like suturing really do work automatically. But because it’s surgery, the demand for doing it well is far greater than the demand for automation. Surgeons don’t even trust residents, so would they trust a robot? But if you keep having it do the work and it turns out better than a resident, then it can be used. With self-driving too, nobody used it at first, and then the moment it crossed a certain level people started using it.

Here I offered a slightly different thought. Having used self-driving, I find it makes far fewer mistakes than when I drive myself, and yet when the car missed an exit once I was much angrier than when I miss one myself. I said the threshold would surely be higher in surgery, where a life is at stake. This engineer answered that in the end it’s a matter of level, and raised one more problem. If only some motions are automated, the work of the whole surgery doesn’t shrink much. In their words, it’s “like me driving the whole highway and having the car do only the parking.”

This engineer said there is no surgery that absolutely requires da Vinci. As examples where the advantage of the wrist joint at the instrument tip shows well, they gave prostate cancer surgery, which goes deep into a narrow pelvis, and thyroid surgery that enters behind the ear or through the armpit to hide the scar. Those two surgeries are the examples this engineer gave, and going by the annual report, half of last year’s da Vinci procedures were general surgery. What I newly understood was how much value comes from the single ability to bend the instrument tip in a tight space.

In my judgment, a humanoid operating directly on a patient’s body will come last of the four. A body built for that work already exists, and there is the threshold of sterilization. It isn’t enough for the automated step to go well, and instrument changes, exceptions, and human supervision have to be solved along with it. Assisting work like handing over or tidying instruments should be considered separately.

6. Area Two: Repetitive Procedures in the Hospital

What about work simpler than surgery? The blood-draw robot this engineer built is a good example. Drawing blood follows a fixed sequence. The patient puts an arm in, the upper arm is cuffed, a distance sensor reads the contour of the arm, an ultrasound probe is pressed against the skin at the right pressure to locate the vein, and the needle goes in. The robot is shaped exactly along this sequence. There’s a place to put the arm, a cuffing device, and axes that move the probe and the needle. There’s no human shape anywhere.

I didn’t really need to ask why it was built this way. For a job with a fixed sequence, you can build the machine with only the devices and motions that job needs. Twenty-plus finger degrees of freedom have no use here. What it takes to put a needle in the middle of a vein is the precision of a few axes.

The clearance story mattered more. In the development process this engineer went through, they said electrical and mechanical safety testing came first. You drop a steel ball to see whether the case cracks, and apply overvoltage to see whether the inside holds up. Next comes the clinical trial. The developer tries to cut down the clinical work by showing the product is similar to one already cleared, and if the regulator judges that it’s different, the trial has to be done. With an agency hired to recruit hospitals and enroll patients, they said the cost ran into the hundreds of millions of won.

Q. Once a robot gets cleared, is everything done with that robot covered?

A. No. Building a robot doesn’t get you clearance for everything that can be done with it. You have to get it separately for each surgery, as in such-and-such surgery using this robot.

This is what this engineer experienced in Korea, and things differ by country and pathway. In the US there is a pathway of showing substantial equivalence to an already cleared device, and the FDA’s guidance for this pathway asks for bench testing, animal testing, and clinical testing as performance data where applicable. Clinical trials aren’t always required, and a single clearance sometimes covers several procedures. Still, the broad frame is the same. A medical device is reviewed against a defined intended use, and adding a new use means submitting evidence that fits it again.

This made me think again about the advantage of being general purpose. The humanoid’s advantage is that it does anything. But one body being able to do many jobs doesn’t mean all those jobs can be sold as medical uses. The wider the uses, the more the scope and cost of validation grow with them.

What is actually getting cleared and going out are robots built for a single job. A case has appeared in blood draws too. This August the FDA authorized an autonomous blood-draw robot from a company called Vitestro. The conditions are use on adult outpatients, with one trained phlebotomist supervising up to three units. On the surgery side, Medtronic expanded the indications of its Hugo surgical robot to general surgery on November 17, and Johnson & Johnson’s Ottava received US marketing authorization in July through De Novo, the pathway for novel device types.

7. Area Three: Factories and Warehouses

Factories and warehouses weren’t an area the dialogue covered separately. What this engineer gave as humanoid work was work that people can do but find tedious. When I searched the public record for where that kind of work is actually being done for pay right now, it was factories and warehouses. So this section is what I put together from company announcements and news reports, and from the dialogue I took only the answer about part costs.

Q. Do surgical robots and humanoids treat part costs differently?

A. Completely differently, I’d think. Surgical robots are fairly free on unit cost. The machine sells for so much that the mood isn’t about saving money. A humanoid takes over work that people can do but find tedious, so it has no choice but to bring unit cost down. That’s why they have to make things like motors and reducers themselves.

In a factory, the robot’s price and upkeep are compared with labor cost, hiring difficulty, and the cost of work stoppages and injuries. The pressure to bring the price down is there from the start.

Agility Robotics said last month that its humanoid Digit has been working under a paid contract at a warehouse of logistics company GXO since June 2024 and has moved more than 100,000 totes. Moving standardized totes doesn’t require five dexterous fingers like a person’s. You can start with a gripper built for that object. In the next-generation robot it unveiled in September, Agility introduced a design where grippers and tools are swapped depending on the task. It’s a design that aims to increase the jobs the same robot can take on by attaching task-specific tools. The company said it plans to supply this robot to some customers first in the first half of next year and start general availability by the end of next year.

Move to work that needs fingers and lifespan comes up. A July article in the Chinese business daily Yicai carries a remark by the head of R&D at logistics robot company Geek+. Robot hands at the leading companies last 300,000 to 500,000 cycles, and that’s for hands with low degrees of freedom. Warehouse work means 8,000 to 10,000 grasps a day, so a hand could break down after about a month of use, the executive said. It’s one person’s remark, and it doesn’t say which product was measured under what conditions. In the same article, a company that makes robot hands said it reached 1 million cycles in a test under load.

The numbers the companies put out are bigger. 1X wrote that it tested finger parts millions of times, and the spec table on its order page lists the tendon drive at 2 million cycles at nominal load. Next to it is a note about replacement in a one-day service. Unitree wrote that the five fingers can be swapped out individually. The lifespan numbers are test results and specifications the companies put out, and finger replacement is a design for maintenance. How often a hand actually fails in real work and how much it costs to fix isn’t in these materials.

A table putting on one page the cleared use counts of surgical robot parts, the published lifespan figures for humanoid hands, and the daily cycle count of warehouse work

1X’s service note and Unitree’s finger replacement design are designs meant to keep the robot in use by swapping out the failed part. The resemblance to surgical instruments goes only as far as replacing parts to maintain performance. Whether replacement will happen at a set count, and whether that replacement becomes large recurring revenue, can’t be known yet. There is also a report that Tesla ran into reliability problems with some of the hand’s touch sensors and is developing a replaceable glove-type sensor. I couldn’t check the original report.

If hands get swapped out, the hand becomes a cost that keeps running during use, on top of the one-time cost paid when buying the robot. How much that will be can’t be calculated from what’s public now. We’d need to know whether the whole hand is replaced or just a finger or sensor, what the replacement part costs, and how long it actually lasts in the field. In the materials I looked at, I didn’t find a company saying it will sell hands as consumables. This is my inference from looking at the surgical robot case.

Going by the public record, this is the place among the four where humanoids work first. The objects and motions are fixed, and there’s no burden of medical device clearance or sterilization. What to watch from here is how far the range of jobs widens. There’s the route of swapping grippers and the route of using fingers, and the further toward the latter, the more the hand’s lifespan affects upkeep cost and uptime.

8. Area Four: The Home

Last, I asked this.

Q. What would you have to see to say humanoids have really arrived?

A. It’s hard to look at the hardware in isolation, and I think if I saw a robot doing origami, I’d say it has really arrived. It has to feel tiny forces, seeing with its eyes and feeling through force too, and move the hand and arm just right, and that’s an extremely hard thing. They still can’t even fold towels well.

Grip a little too hard and the paper tears, so as a standard it’s pretty harsh. The next thing they gave was cleaning the grime in bathroom corners. You scrub while spraying water, so it has to be waterproof, and in the middle of that it has to feel how hard it’s scrubbing. Driving, they thought, isn’t hard. One point about senses other than the hand came up too.

Q. Apart from hand motion, is there a sense a robot still needs to have?

A. If a humanoid got a sense of taste on top of that, you’d have to say it’s complete beyond origami.

So has really no one done origami yet? When I looked, there had already been a demonstration. Google DeepMind showed a dual-arm robot doing origami in March of last year. But the technical report shows the task is folding paper four times to make the shape of a fox’s head. The report describes it as a very hard task for a robot, but by human standards it’s simple origami. It’s not the kind of origami I had in mind, like folding a crane, which goes through many more steps. Figure released a video in August of last year of a humanoid folding towels on its own, but it didn’t say how many attempts succeeded out of how many.

Since then a number has come out. This September, Figure published the results of having robots tidy living rooms, fold towels, and make beds in 30 homes they had never been to. A task counted as a success only if it was finished to the end and as a failure if a person stepped in for safety, and the success rate across the three kinds of tasks combined was 56%. For towel folding alone it’s about six in ten. It’s the company’s own evaluation, but success rates measured outside the lab have started to appear. 1X has written on its order page that US deliveries start this year.

Water is the same story. Not many have published water and dust ratings. Boston Dynamics states that Atlas is IP67 (a rating that fully blocks dust and allows brief immersion in water), and 1X wrote that it sealed the hand to IP68. I couldn’t find a rating in Figure’s new robot announcement or from Tesla. As we saw with surgical robots, the places that transmit motion are hard to seal, and a finger has many such places.

I see the home as the most uncertain of the four in terms of getting to revenue. In a home, the objects and situations aren’t fixed, and a lot of the work involves water. The price also has to be at a level a household would pay. I think that in the home, machines that do only one job, like cleaning robots, will stay in use for a good while.

9. The Price of One Joint

Turning to investing, the most useful number from the dialogue was the price of one joint. A robot’s rotary joint is usually driven by a set that bundles a motor, a reducer (a gear that turns the motor’s fast rotation into slow, strong rotation), and an encoder (a sensor that reads the rotation angle). By this engineer’s rough estimate, this set costs about 2 million won per axis even when bought cheaply. With dozens of axes it comes to tens of millions of won. As I wrote in June in NVIDIA Did Not Come to Korea to Sell GPUs: Where the June 5 Seoul Visit Pointed in the Physical AI Supply Chain, a humanoid has twenty-five to thirty major joints alone, and several sets of motors, reducers, and sensors go into moving these joints. I covered the Korean companies that make these parts separately in that piece. It’s a number that will vary a lot with specs and purchase volume, so please take it only as a rough figure. Even so, with talk going around of selling a humanoid for 30 million won, it gives a sense of how large a share the joints take.

That’s why, they said, the big companies thinking about mass production try to make this set themselves. A motor is a combination of magnets and coils, so it’s worth trying, was the point. The one thing they singled out as hard to make in-house was the harmonic reducer. It’s a precision gear that gets a large reduction ratio by elastically deforming a thin metal cup, and because it’s small, light, and has almost no backlash, it’s widely used in robot joints. It goes into surgical robots too, and they said they’ve used it a lot themselves. The May investment map also treated the precision parts that include reducers as the bottleneck in the humanoid supply chain.

The principle of this gear is one for which American inventor C. Walton Musser filed the basic patent in 1955, and the company that led its commercialization in Japan is Harmonic Drive Systems. Its half-year results released on November 11 show first-half orders up about 70% from a year earlier. The reason the company gave is semiconductor equipment, and it wrote that in North America orders for surgical robots and semiconductor equipment increased. Humanoids don’t appear in this document.

The numbers by application are in the November 19 briefing materials. First-half orders for the medical application, which includes surgical robots, were 7.9 billion yen, close to three times a year earlier. The application combining AI and humanoids was 1.5 billion yen. Medical is five times that. The AI and humanoid side swings from quarter to quarter and came in at only the 200-million-yen range in the most recent quarter. The company assumes full-scale mass production by its high-volume customers between 2028 and 2030, and it marked actuators for the hand as still at the concept stage. At the briefing, asked which parts of a humanoid its products go into, the president answered that in the flow from prototyping to mass production, the hand seems to be running late. The president also said many startups making hands are springing up. So even a company that sells reducers sees the hand falling behind in its customers’ move to mass production. However, this remark alone doesn’t let us narrow the reason for the delay down to hand durability.

In China there is Leaderdrive. According to a September article from 36Kr, humanoid-related orders make up more than 40% of the orders this company has on hand, and it plans to expand production capacity to 1.5 times the current level by year-end. The counting basis differs from Harmonic Drive Systems’ half-year new orders, so the two ratios can’t be compared directly. Over the past year, Leaderdrive’s share price rose nearly 60% and Harmonic Drive Systems’ nearly 30% (TradingView, as retrieved 2026-11-26).

In the surgical robot development this engineer went through, they said getting the required performance came before cutting part costs. In humanoids, the pressure to lower mass-production cost is strong. Bank of America estimated that the parts cost of a Chinese-made humanoid will fall from $35,000 in 2025 to under $17,000 in 2030, and in the same 2030 projection actuators (drive units that bundle a motor and a transmission) are about half the cost. This includes actuators that move in a straight line, and there are several reduction methods, so you can’t assume harmonic reducers sell in step with robot unit counts. For a reducer company, growing humanoid orders mean growing volume, and they may also mean more customers asking for lower prices.

I haven’t yet been able to confirm whether this difference actually shows up in the reducer companies’ results. So I look at orders by application before total orders, and at whether selling prices and margins rise together. On current numbers, the growth in Harmonic Drive Systems’ orders is thanks to semiconductor equipment and surgical robots, and humanoids are still small.

Q. To compete on price with Chinese companies, what kind of technology or product do you need to have?

A. ROBOTIS was actually an actuator company. It came into that area recently, as physical AI and humanoids took off. But suppose China jumps in on price. Unless you have deep technology of your own like Harmonic Drive Systems, or a machine like Intuitive’s da Vinci that’s technically excellent, expensive, and takes so long to learn that surgeons don’t want to learn a new one, without something like that you can be caught up. Especially on the humanoid side.

This engineer gave Harmonic Drive Systems as an example of a company that can withstand price competition. However, the May piece listed price competition from Chinese companies as a risk for this company too, so how long the technology gap in reducers lasts needs to be looked at separately.

ROBOTIS is a company I put on the May investment map as an actuator company. I looked up its history. According to CEO Kim Byoung-soo’s July interview with Chosun Biz, the company was founded in 1999 to build humanoids. When the market didn’t open up, it stopped humanoid development in 2015 and focused on its DYNAMIXEL business, the robot actuators it had been selling to research institutes and robot companies. It restarted humanoid development in 2024. So what this engineer said is right for the period in between. Last year it released a humanoid that moves on wheels. Its two-legged walking humanoid had its official launch at a robot trade show this month, and a company official said product deliveries begin next year. In the July interview, what the CEO named as the biggest threat was also the Chinese companies.

I think humanoids, too, will in the end have no choice but to yield to Chinese prices, like solar cells and batteries. Korean humanoid companies like ROBOTIS may also find it hard to survive in the end unless they carry a strong color of their own and imprint it on the market.

10. The Order, Area by Area

Area map. A matrix with the operating room, repetitive procedures in the hospital, factories and warehouses, and the home as rows, and as columns what is demanded of the hand (cable life, force sensing at the tip, sealing), the purpose-built robots that already exist, the judgment heard in the dialogue, and the public evidence

Going by the public record, the place where humanoids work first is factories and warehouses, and there they are already moving totes for pay. The home has passed the demo stage and success rates have started to be published, but the path to revenue is the most uncertain. Hospital procedures stay the territory of single-purpose robots for a while, and operating directly on a patient’s body comes last. Robots are going in first where the objects and motions are fixed and a gripper built for the job can be used. The speed of spreading to other areas depends on the hand’s performance and also on the damage when it fails, on clearance and sterilization, and on the price gap with the machines already there.

I think spreading into work that needs dexterous hands will take more time. The end of next year that Tesla talked about is when it starts handing robots to outside companies, and assigning finger work across many sites comes after that. Surgical robots still count uses and replace wearing instruments today, the instrument that feels force at the tip came out nearly 24 years after the first clearance, and grip force still isn’t conveyed. This experience doesn’t directly foretell the humanoid’s schedule. The conditions differ in both thickness and sterilization. Still, it lets you gauge how hard the work is.

There are also grounds for the opposite view. To make money, you don’t need to complete every function of the human hand first. If task-specific grippers and swappable parts are used while learning performance improves and maintenance practices get fixed along the way, it could spread faster than surgical instruments did. Figure’s home evaluation and 1X’s cycle test numbers are evidence on that side.

As the dialogue was wrapping up, this engineer said this about surgical robots. They’ve been around since the 1990s already, and it’s just that the versions kept going up. I think humanoids are likely to go the same way. I expect the jobs they can take on to grow little by little as hand lifespan and task success rates improve.

Of course, I could be seeing this wrong. If Tesla starts handing robots to outside companies earlier than the end of next year and publishes hand lifespan as numbers measured in the field, I’d have to drop the view that the hand problem lasts a long time. The same goes if any company releases failure statistics from running finger work in a factory for several months and the numbers are good. On the home side numbers have already started to come out, so if data appears showing that over several months of use the number of human interventions falls and the upkeep cost is bearable, I’ll change my view on the home.
