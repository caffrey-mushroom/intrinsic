TL;DR

  • Of the roughly $2 billion in letters of intent (LOIs) the US Commerce Department signed with nine quantum companies in May, 68% went to quantum manufacturing technology development at two foundries, IBM and GlobalFoundries. But industry analysis puts the annual wafer volume for quantum chips at tens to hundreds of wafers, so this support can’t be explained by wafer revenue.

The three layers are the materials that fab won’t accept, the processes it doesn’t use, and the bump stage where chips are bonded and stacked, and this is mostly a superconducting story. But the superconducting merchant lines open today (commercial lines that take in and fabricate other people’s chips) are held by companies that sell their own qubits or processors, like IBM, IonQ and QuantWare.
  • Cross-checking the documents turned up a case where a superconducting company that hands its core wafer processing to an outside foundry wrote the very fab a competitor had bought straight into a government agreement after the acquisition. My read is that the value of this business is decided less by wafer count than by who holds the segregated line.

1. 68% and the Wafer Count

On May 21 this year, the US Commerce Department signed letters of intent worth $2.013 billion with nine quantum companies. Of that, IBM gets $1 billion and GlobalFoundries (GF from here on) gets $375 million, and both are money for developing manufacturing technology at foundries that fabricate quantum wafers, rather than for developing qubits (the computing unit of a quantum computer). Together the two come to 68% of the total.

The number of wafers going into those fabs is much smaller than you’d think. Quantum Computing Report (QCR from here on), an outlet that analyzes the quantum industry, figured that for quantum chips annual wafer volume could run tens to hundreds of wafers. I first saw this framing, judging quantum fab economics by wafer volume, in that analysis. For comparison, last year GF shipped a whopping 2.34 million-plus wafers, in 300mm equivalents. A wafer is the round silicon disc that semiconductor chips are fabricated on, and 300mm is its diameter.

The stock prices raise the same question. As of the October 23 close, GF is up about 30% over one year, while IonQ, which went as far as buying a fab outright, has fallen by nearly half. That said, it’s fair to say what pulled GF’s stock up was expectations around silicon photonics (the technology of patterning circuits that carry light on a silicon chip) and US-based manufacturing more than quantum, and quantum’s effect on GF’s stock price is still barely visible.

So with revenue this small, why did the government put money into fabs, and why did IonQ go as far as buying one? On the silicon photonics side, I laid out who can actually fabricate optical chips in The Silicon Photonics Foundry Layer: Who Can Actually Make a PIC, and this time I put the same question to quantum chips. After writing the silicon photonics foundry piece and the September quantum investment map, what was left blank between them was the fabs that make quantum chips.

The nine-company list in NIST’s May 21, 2026 release, with the IBM ($1 billion) and GlobalFoundries ($375 million) lines highlighted.

2. The Places Called “Quantum Foundries”

A foundry is a fab that fabricates chips other people designed, on their behalf. A quantum foundry means the target is quantum chips, and that covers not just qubit chips but trap chips that levitate and hold ions, photonic chips that make qubits out of light, and even chips that control and read out qubits at cryogenic temperatures.

The problem is that the same name gets attached to facilities that are pretty different in character. First there’s the commercial contract line that takes money to fabricate other people’s wafers, which the industry calls a merchant line. IBM’s subsidiary Anderon, GF’s Quantum Technology Solutions and SkyWater’s Quantum Solutions fall into this group, while the Netherlands’ QuantWare, which says it already offers a contract service fabricating superconducting chips from customer designs, is scaling up with a new fab, and Denmark’s Quantum Foundry Copenhagen is under construction. There’s also the national lab shared service. MIT Lincoln Laboratory’s SQUILL gathers just over a hundred user designs per wafer, in several sets, and fabricates them. This approach of gathering multiple designs onto one wafer is called MPW (multi-project wafer).

There are also open research fabs where researchers run the tools themselves, and Korea’s KAIST National Quantum Fab falls into this group. Some places are foundries in name only, and UC Santa Barbara’s NSF Quantum Foundry is a research center that develops materials and devices for quantum information technology. Separately, there are in-house fabs, like those of Google, Rigetti and IQM, where companies make their own chips in their own fabs.

When this piece says quantum foundry, it means the merchant line.

3. The Segregated Line

A segregated line is a zone or line kept apart from the ordinary chip fab line, where the materials and processes that fab won’t accept are handled separately. An ordinary chip fab, which the industry calls a CMOS fab (the standard process line that makes most of today’s logic and memory chips), is strict about which materials come in. The prime example is gold. When gold atoms get into silicon, they create defect levels near the middle of the bandgap (trap sites that catch electrons and wipe them out), adding more places where electrons disappear. That’s why imec said it switched to a gold-free process when bringing gallium nitride power devices into a CMOS line.

Superconducting qubits are made from metals like aluminum, niobium and indium. Germany’s Fraunhofer EMFT splits its 200mm pilot line into a CMOS zone and a separate zone for non-CMOS materials like niobium and indium, and its paper says the wafers leave the CMOS line at the indium step. So the qubits themselves are made on the CMOS line, and the separation happens at the step after that.

Two sentences from Fraunhofer EMFT’s arXiv 2505.04337, “a separate part, where the handling of non-CMOS materials, like Niobium or Indium, is enabled” and “the wafers exit the CMOS line”, highlighted. Source: Fraunhofer EMFT, arXiv 2505.04337, 2025

Process methods are a sticking point too. The standard lab processes are angle evaporation, which deposits at a tilt, and lift-off, which puts metal on top of a photoresist layer and then peels it off along with the resist, and imec wrote that these methods leave resist residue and lose reproducibility on wafers larger than small lab wafers, so they’re largely incompatible with volume manufacturing. Korean press coverage dealt with the same point. Money Today wrote that because lift-off isn’t a CMOS-compatible process, it’s hard for Samsung Electronics or SK hynix to produce superconducting quantum processors right away.

The stage where chips are bonded and stacked is a sticking point too. Flipping the qubit chip and the wiring chip to face each other and joining them with small metal protrusions (bumps) is called flip-chip, and Google’s Sycamore also joined its two chips with indium bumps. IBM putting superconducting wiring, through-silicon vias (TSVs) and bumps side by side on Anderon’s process menu is about the same stage. As qubit counts grow, you can’t lay all the wiring on one plane, so stacking becomes unavoidable, and then this stage keeps getting bigger.

But these three layers shift a bit from fab to fab and from payer to payer. For the niobium family, the call differs by fab. GF’s 300mm high-volume line already accepts the niobium nitride detector layer on PsiQuantum’s chips. In return, the two companies said in 2021 that they had installed proprietary production tools at two GF 300mm fabs (the New York fab for photonic chips, the Dresden fab for control chips). You can see it as a case of bringing separate customer tools into the fab to widen the range of materials it accepts. The opposite case is imec. imec used only aluminum and switched from lift-off to subtractive etch (a process that cuts away the parts you don’t need), and got 393 out of 400 qubits working on a 300mm CMOS pilot line. Rather than adding materials, imec in effect moved the process toward the fab’s rules.

So what’s the highest wall in getting past the three layers? It’s tempting to name a specific component like the Josephson junction (JJ), but my answer is short. It’s not something specific like the JJ, it’s ultra-precision processing. A JJ is a component with an insulating film a few atomic layers thick sandwiched between two layers of superconducting metal, and it sets the frequency of a superconducting qubit. Going by imec’s data, this junction’s resistance spreads by about 4% within a single chip and the frequency spreads by about half that, which for a 5GHz qubit is around 100MHz (an approximate calculation for those junction conditions). In an architecture where neighboring qubits can’t overlap in frequency, this spread directly determines yield, so IBM tunes the junctions by heating them with a laser on chips that have left the fab, and Rigetti trims the resistance spread with electrical pulses down to around 0.5%. At the junction you have to control a barrier a few atomic layers thick and its interfaces, and at the bumps and stacking you have to control contamination, alignment and contact uniformity. And ultra-precision processing applies just the same to the other approaches. Photonic chips and semiconductor spin chips alike only get the right loss and uniformity when lithography (the process of patterning circuits with light) defines the patterns as intended. Whether a segregated line is needed differs by approach, but I think this precision sits at the bottom of what a fab sells, whatever the approach.

4. Where Each Qubit Type Gets Made

Each approach asks different things of the fab, and so each one goes to a different fab.

Silicon spin qubits use the spin of a single electron trapped inside a semiconductor, so their process is the closest to that of ordinary chips. Intel made Tunnel Falls on its 300mm D1 line with a flow similar to a standard CMOS logic process and reported a 95% wafer yield. UK-based Quantum Motion fabricated a chip that put more than 1,000 quantum dots on 22FDX, GF’s low-power process (a quantum dot is a tiny cell that traps a single electron). What’s special here is more the materials than the process. They use silicon-28 with the isotopes that disturb the spin filtered out, and the chips have to be tested at cryogenic temperatures at the wafer level.

The photonic approach adds modules on top of a silicon photonics line. PsiQuantum makes its chips in GF’s high-volume fab and added a layer of superconducting detectors (SNSPDs) that catch single photons. Xanadu was already mixing several platforms at the time of Aurora. Going by the Aurora paper, Xanadu split the fabrication: the source chips in Ligentec’s silicon nitride (SiN) on an X-FAB line, the chips that pick and combine the source outputs in HyperLight’s thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN), and the computing chips on the AIM Photonics platform. This February it expanded its collaboration with Tower, and in October it announced a multi-year partnership with GF’s 300mm Malta line.

Trapped ion is the approach that levitates and holds ions in a vacuum with electric fields, and the trap chip is the chip with those electrodes patterned on it. As the field moves toward controlling qubits with on-chip electronics instead of lasers, the trap has turned into a question of electrode and wiring wafers. Infineon and Innsbruck researchers wrote in a paper that a single 200mm fused silica (quartz glass) wafer made at the Villach fab holds close to 500 trap chips, and Quantinuum named GF as one of several fabs that will make its next-generation ion traps on 300mm. That said, electrodes that withstand RF voltages of hundreds of volts and waveguides (paths for light on the chip) that carry light from ultraviolet to visible are still outside standard process design kits (PDKs).

Superconducting is the approach that most needs the segregated line from section 3. Google made Willow at its own fab in Santa Barbara, and Rigetti makes its chips on 150mm wafers at Fab-1 in Fremont, and it wrote in its 10-K that it expects this fab to meet all its own chip demand for at least the next three years. Finland’s IQM announced that it is expanding its domestic production fab. The main examples of superconducting merchant lines are Anderon and SkyWater in the US and QuantWare in Europe (GF has disclosed only as far as superconducting wiring, and Tower has no public material on the superconducting side), and OQC and Germany’s Fraunhofer EMFT announced that EMFT’s research pilot line will serve as UK-based OQC’s standard manufacturing site for quantum processors.

I made the point in Superconducting or Trapped Ion, They All Buy the Same Light: The Hidden Layer That Wires Quantum Computers Rack to Rack that whatever the approach, the part that links racks with light ends up buying the same components, but on the side of the fabs that make the chips, each approach goes a fairly different way. My outlook is that even for superconducting, the layers that form the qubits will move to commercial 300mm, as at imec, while the indium bumps, 3D stacking and packaging stages stay on the segregated line.

5. Why the Government Paid First

Each company gives a different answer for why these fabs are needed. Mihir Bhaskar, a former IonQ R&D executive who now runs SkyWater’s quantum business, wrote on the company blog that even IonQ’s CMOS-compatible trapped-ion hardware took more than six months to get a single round of wafers in 2025, and that the existing foundries’ terms were “no volume? No deal.” Rigetti’s CEO reportedly told IEEE Spectrum that what draws Rigetti to Anderon is process capability more than production capacity. Both are fair points, but I think the biggest reason these fabs are appearing right now is government money and supply chain security.

The evidence is the order the money came in. 68% of the letter-of-intent money went to two foundries, and the Commerce Department takes equity along with this money. Under its September agreement, GF agreed to issue 9.9 million new shares to the Commerce Department at $37.85 per share, which, divided by shares outstanding, comes to about 1.8% (calculated). D-Wave also signed its final agreement on the condition of a minority, non-controlling equity stake, and IBM also disclosed in its latest quarterly report (10-Q) that Anderon issued a minority stake to the Commerce Department. It didn’t disclose the percentage, and IBM keeps the controlling stake.

The regulators’ documents point the same way. In his July 31 statement on IonQ’s acquisition of SkyWater, the FTC Chairman called SkyWater a US-based quantum foundry and wrote that being an American company is an attraction for quantum computer developers. The same statement assumed that if competitors had to move foundries in a hurry, development could be delayed by several months, and it pointed to two new foundries funded by the Commerce Department as long-term alternatives. The names of those two, GF and IBM, appear in Commissioner Meador’s statement.

Page 4 of FTC Chairman Ferguson’s statement (”could thus add many months of delay”) and page 8 (”two new foundries”), highlighted. Source: FTC, Statement of Chairman Andrew N. Ferguson, July 31, 2026

In the end, the government didn’t wait for any particular qubit approach to win, and in effect started by buying fabs inside the US that can take any approach.

6. Three Deals of 2026: Anderon (IBM), IonQ and SkyWater, GlobalFoundries

Anderon (IBM)

Anderon is the quantum wafer foundry subsidiary that IBM announced plans to set up in May. In September the Commerce Department announced it had signed a final R&D award of up to $1 billion, and according to IBM’s latest quarterly report, about two-thirds of that became available with the agreement and the rest is released only when contractual milestones are met. The Commerce Department’s right to terminate for convenience and some clawback provisions are also attached. Separately, IBM said in May that it would put in $1 billion in cash plus IP, assets and people, and the same report says it has already put in that cash.

The fab itself is an existing line. It’s based on the 300mm line at Albany NanoTech in New York that IBM’s quantum team has been using for several years, and the complex is owned and operated by NY CREATES. Going by an IBM blog post, while the Yorktown research fab ran two shifts on weekdays, Albany runs wafers around the clock, every day. In September IBM said the first quantum wafers are running through the fab. In the launch announcement, IBM said this process is backed by a dedicated process design kit and by existing production capabilities such as inline wafer test, which measures wafers inside the line partway through the process. The first version of that design kit came out in October. Anderon said on its company blog that its first superconducting quantum PDK supports qubits, I/O components for signal wiring, amplifiers and filters, and can be tried early under a license agreement, but the public post gives no design rule numbers or materials. Headcount hasn’t been disclosed, but from what I’ve heard in the industry, it’s running for now at fewer than 100 people, carved out from some of IBM’s existing foundry engineers. It’s a number that hasn’t appeared in any official material yet.

There’s no named external customer yet. According to IEEE Spectrum’s reporting, Anderon CEO Mukesh Khare said it can deliver to external customers by year-end, and the same article has a passage saying Rigetti is in talks with Anderon. Anderon said Rigetti CEO Subodh Kulkarni shared the stage with Khare at Quantum World Congress in late September, but the two companies haven’t announced a contract yet. The modifier also changed several times over four months. In May it was America’s first pure-play quantum foundry, in IBM’s July investor letter it was the world’s first pure-play quantum wafer foundry, and in September it was the world’s most advanced pure-play quantum foundry, while the body of the quarterly report from that same July just said pure-play quantum wafer foundry, with no modifier. When you line these things up and compare them, there’s always one like this that turns up.

IonQ and SkyWater

IonQ announced its acquisition of SkyWater in January and closed the deal at the end of July. SkyWater shareholders received both cash and IonQ stock, and as of the announcement the equity value was around $1.8 billion. SkyWater was originally a specialty foundry with a 200mm fab in Minnesota, and it also holds Trusted Foundry accreditation certified by the US Department of Defense’s DMEA. It now also has facilities in Florida and Texas. 2025 revenue was in the $440 million range, and in the same release the company said it had eight development projects with quantum computing companies.

What does it tell us that SkyWater, a quantum foundry, got acquired by IonQ? That’s how much it matters. In a word, my take is that if you hold the fab, you buy time. IonQ said that co-developing with SkyWater cut its design cycle from nine months to two, and that over six months it ran 12 times as many wafer lots (batches of wafers that run together at once) as at its previous foundry. That’s the company’s claim, and the previous foundry isn’t named.

On the surface, SkyWater keeps running its quantum business separately from IonQ. In fact, in September SkyWater launched SkyWater Quantum Solutions for external customers and rolled out SC250, which puts a Josephson junction layer on top of multilayer niobium wiring, and SP90, a silicon nitride photonics platform. SkyWater said the first SP90 wafers have already shipped (the full platform is slated for 2027), and Qolab, whose CTO is John Martinis, who used to lead Google’s quantum hardware, signed a multi-year agreement as SC250’s lead customer, for its QSiP, which bundles cryogenic wiring, filters and amplifiers into one package. Structurally, though, SkyWater is a wholly owned subsidiary of IonQ, and that subsidiary is a merchant line that also takes other people’s wafers.

For reference, SkyWater said in 2022 that it would build a large fab on the Purdue campus (it wasn’t going to be quantum-only) and then dropped the plan in 2024, IBJ reported, and that same SkyWater is now an IonQ subsidiary.

GlobalFoundries and Tower

GlobalFoundries is well known, of course. Honestly, it’s been that way for a long time. PsiQuantum has been developing photonic chips with GF since 2019, and on the spin side, Quantum Motion, Equal1 and Diraq have also teamed up with GF. But it was with the launch of Quantum Technology Solutions this May that GF made this official as a “new quantum business.” On September 8, the Commerce Department’s CHIPS award of up to $375 million was finalized, and the same day Quantinuum listed GF as one of several fabs that will make its next-generation ion traps. On October 6, Xanadu announced a multi-year partnership that puts its detectors and silicon nitride on GF’s Malta line.

Superconducting has to be looked at separately. GF said it is “developing” manufacturing platforms for quantum processors across several approaches, including superconducting, and the superconducting items currently listed on its technology page are cryogenic readout and control chips and superconducting interconnect. No contract manufacturing track record with external superconducting qubit customers has been disclosed yet.

Tower has a silicon photonics line just as GF does, but its quantum exposure is much thinner than GF’s. As far as I’ve checked, the only case of a quantum company appearing as a partner in Tower’s disclosures since 2021 is the one Xanadu deal, and the body of its 2025 annual report doesn’t contain the word quantum. I see Tower as an AI silicon photonics foundry with a quantum option attached.

7. Korea’s Quantum Fabs

In academia, a KAIST quantum fab is taking shape. The KAIST National Quantum Fab will take more than KRW 45 billion through 2031, and the targets are completion in 2027 and full operation in 2028. Its operating model is fully open, with researchers running the processes themselves, and the supported platforms in the official wording are photons, point defects and neutral atoms. Director Yong-Hoon Cho reportedly said, in effect, that they prepared the photonics side first because a superconducting quantum fab already exists in Korea.

The interview doesn’t name that fab, but the place that checks out as having made superconducting qubit chips in Korea is Sungkyunkwan University’s QFab in Suwon. QFab describes itself as a quantum foundry that makes superconducting qubit devices for researchers, and it runs chips every quarter on 3-6 inch wafers. It was also reported that, for the 20-qubit superconducting system KRISS (Korea Research Institute of Standards and Science) unveiled in 2024, qubit design and fabrication were handled by Sungkyunkwan University and UNIST. On the photonic side, KOSDAQ-listed Sawnics is taking part in a KRW 10 billion quantum photonic integrated circuit foundry project led by KAIST. UNIST also opened a KRW 30 billion quantum nano fab this spring.

In other words, Korea has split the roles, with superconducting at Sungkyunkwan University and photonics at KAIST, and the spot for a merchant line like Anderon or GF is still empty. In September the Ministry of Science and ICT designated Seoul, Jeonnam-Gwangju and Busan-Ulsan-Gyeongnam as quantum clusters, and Daejeon, home of the National Quantum Fab, was reportedly left out this time.

That’s it for Korea. But one thing caught my eye while cross-checking US filings. The agreement D-Wave signed with the Commerce Department on September 4 has a list of project sites, and SkyWater’s Minnesota fab is on that list. That date is a little over a month after IonQ closed its acquisition of SkyWater.

The “Project Site means” definition in D-Wave’s CHIPS Other Transaction Agreement (8-K Ex-10.1), with “the Bloomington, MN facility of SkyWater Technology” highlighted. Source: D-Wave Quantum, SEC 8-K, September 8, 2026

8. Scale Math and Where the Value Accrues

So the first question was how big a foundry business focused on “quantum” would be, and answered in orders of magnitude alone, it’s really small. I started by counting backward how many wafers one large quantum computer takes.

Starling, which IBM says it will deliver in 2029, is a 200 logical qubit machine. A logical qubit is a unit that bundles several physical qubits together to catch errors, and IBM hasn’t disclosed the total physical qubit count. Plugging in the module size from IBM researchers’ error correction architecture paper gives anywhere from around 10,000 up into the 30,000s, depending on the configuration (estimate). This isn’t Starling’s confirmed spec. If you assume, like imec’s test chips, that one 300mm wafer yields dozens of chips and each chip carries hundreds of qubits, then even stacking a qubit chip and a wiring chip and taking yield at half, one Starling is a single-digit number of wafers (estimate). Even going to the one million physical qubit class of quantum computer that Google has named as its goal, with a generous 1mm qubit pitch and allowing for yield loss and stacking, it’s around 80-200 wafers (estimate under stated assumptions). This is an area calculation that leaves out development runs and minimum lots. Spin is far denser on a test chip basis, to the point that Intel gets more than 20,000 quantum dot test chips out of one wafer. The wafer demand of a large-scale processor including wiring and control has to be worked out separately.

This calculation only shows the scale of chip area that goes into one quantum computer. Annual unit shipments and development runs aren’t disclosed, so industry-wide demand can’t be counted, and for comparison I assumed 1,000-2,000 wafers a year. Even so, that’s less than 0.1% of GF’s shipments in a year (calculated). In money terms, GF’s 2025 wafer revenue divided by its 300mm-equivalent shipments comes to about $2,600 per wafer. It’s an average across many processes, so it’s only a proxy for comparison, but even assuming quantum wafers get ten to twenty times that, annual wafer revenue is tens of millions of dollars, around $100 million at most (estimate; the multiple is an assumption, not an actual contract price). By contrast, the money the Commerce Department promised the two foundries is up to $1.375 billion, the cash IBM put in separately is $1 billion, and the equity value when IonQ announced the SkyWater acquisition was around $1.8 billion. That said, these amounts pay for years of technology development and even non-quantum businesses, so you can’t put a multiple on them against annual wafer revenue directly, and they should be seen as the price of buying manufacturing technology, development speed and supply chain access together.

Where money actually gets booked looks to be on the development services side. In SkyWater’s last standalone quarterly report (10-Q), development services revenue was in the $60 million range for the quarter, but how much of that is quantum isn’t broken out. The company only said its quantum-related development revenue grew more than 30% in 2025. If you apply the 2025 company outlook that EE Times reported, namely that the Advanced Compute business, which is mostly quantum, would make up around 15% of existing-business revenue, SkyWater’s quantum revenue comes to around $40 million. That’s a different number from actual pure quantum revenue, and it’s an estimate on the high side.

GF describes the same shape. On the Q2 earnings call, the CEO called quantum a call option and said the next 1-3 years would be technology services revenue, with manufacturing revenue coming in in the late 2020s (Q2 call transcript). What this says is that a quantum foundry’s first revenue comes from development services like process development fees, design kits and prototype runs, more than from wafer count.

The ones who paid this money each bought something different. As we saw in section 5, the government bought fabs inside the US that can be used whichever approach wins, plus equity in those fabs, IonQ bought the time saved by shortening design cycles plus Trusted Foundry accreditation, and IBM split with the government the cost of a 300mm fab its own roadmap needs anyway. CEO Arvind Krishna also cited the finalized award, on the October earnings call, as one of the grounds for saying IBM is ready to scale after the Starling demonstration.

My judgment on where the value accrues is that in the short term it’s the owners holding the segregated lines, and in the long term it’s the neutral lines. A neutral line here means a merchant line that no qubit company owns, so it takes even competitors’ wafers without hesitation. The basis for the short-term side is the FTC Chairman’s statement itself. The Chairman called SkyWater’s position a critically important but short-term position, and saw that customers rushing to move fabs could face months of delay. Right now the superconducting merchant lines publicly named in the US are IBM-owned Anderon and IonQ-owned SkyWater, and things aren’t much different in Europe. The Copenhagen fab, aiming to open in 2027, said it would make wafers on commercial terms for quantum companies around the world, but the same announcement said it works closely with the quantum computer program that its owner, the Novo Nordisk Foundation, funds itself. QuantWare, which took new investment in May and is building KiloFab, already runs a contract service but is also a company that sells its own superconducting processors. In Europe, research pilot lines like Fraunhofer EMFT’s are making OQC chips, but in the US there’s effectively no neutral superconducting line yet that you can pick at commercial scale. The FTC Chairman proposed attaching eight conditions such as nondiscriminatory access, information firewalls and customer transition support, but the two commissioners’ votes split 1-1 and the conditions weren’t adopted. SkyWater said it runs information separation between programs (”program firewalling”), and Commissioner Meador took the view that customers can protect themselves through ordinary contracts, but how far the contractual protections for each customer go is hard to confirm from public materials. The basis for the long-term side is Commissioner Meador’s statement. Meador’s view was that a merchant foundry that takes every approach is worth a lot, so IonQ has an incentive to keep SkyWater a neutral, multi-customer foundry even now. I think that incentive actually starts to carry weight only after customers have an alternative. This part is my interpretation.

If you look at the owner tags on the map above, the dedicated quantum merchant group is mostly held by companies that sell qubits or processors, and in the commercial foundry group only the superconducting qubit column is empty.

That’s why the D-Wave agreement from earlier matters. I lean toward the view that the wafers of superconducting companies that outsource their core wafer processing will stay at SkyWater over the next two years, at least for existing US customers. D-Wave does have a small in-house manufacturing facility in Palo Alto, but it’s a company that wrote in its 10-K that it can work with existing outside foundries without spending on a new fab. Per the fiscal 2025 10-K, the SkyWater agreement allows non-renewal on six months’ notice or termination on one year’s notice, but a separate amendment contains annual minimum spending commitments through 2027 (the amounts are redacted). On top of that, it also put the SkyWater fab in its government agreement after the acquisition closed. And on October 13 the two companies announced they had signed a multi-year agreement to continue process development and manufacturing at the Minnesota fab. According to the company announcement, it includes terms protecting D-Wave’s intellectual property, but the announcement doesn’t give the contract term or amount. Qolab’s multi-year agreement also points to the relationship continuing, but since it covers wiring and control parts for QSiP, it doesn’t tell us where the qubit chips get made. The alternatives, Anderon and GF, haven’t yet named any outside superconducting qubit customers. The D-Wave agreement lists other facilities too, but the new Boca Raton facility is, per the company announcement, a headquarters, R&D and testing facility, so there’s no basis yet for saying it’s building its own wafer fab.

9. What the Quantum Business Is Worth, Seen From the Fab

If you widen the fab numbers out to the quantum business as a whole, quantum demand is still at the stage where it runs on balance sheets and government money more than on customer revenue. Last fiscal year’s revenue on TradingView puts IonQ in the $100 million range, D-Wave and Quantinuum in the tens of millions of dollars, and Rigetti under $10 million. Even if you add all four companies’ revenue together, it comes to less than $200 million, yet all four have market caps more than a hundred times their revenue (as of the October 23 close).

For that gap to close, quantum system revenue and profitability have to grow. Whether the foundries’ quantum customers and manufacturing services grow can be seen as more or less a secondary indicator for checking that progress. That said, development runs and commercial volume have to be told apart, and we don’t yet know whether the foundries will even break out quantum revenue. Going by company roadmaps, the point where wafer demand starts to show up as actual numbers is around 2028-2029. IonQ said its CMOS integration path would reach a manufacturable commercial stage in 2028, for IBM Starling it’s 2029, and GF put manufacturing services revenue in the late 2020s.

From the public-market investor’s side, I think GFS is the cleanest quantum foundry exposure. It doesn’t pick a qubit type, its interests don’t clash with the qubit companies, and the government ends up holding a stake of about 1.8% from the quantum award alone. On October 14, GF announced it would operate two new fabs in New York under a US-Japan investment initiative, and said that under a separate agreement the Commerce Department will receive about 4% of GF’s equity. The press release gives no share count, no price and nothing on what the stake is in exchange for, and it doesn’t mention quantum either. The government is in effect sitting in the same boat too, as an investor, though that doesn’t make the government stake a basis for guaranteed investment returns. Of course, at a company with a market cap in the $24 billion range, quantum is a call option, as the CEO put it, and the weak spot remains that it hasn’t yet been confirmed whether GF takes superconducting qubits.

The other names come with mixed exposure. The existing revenue of SkyWater, which IonQ has now taken in, has a large non-quantum share, enough that Texas fab wafer services alone come close to 40% (calculated), and the breakdown on an IonQ consolidated basis will first show up in the Q3 earnings release. The stock has fallen by nearly half over the past year. IBM’s Anderon is a big business once you add the government money and IBM’s own money together, but inside a company with a market cap of around $240 billion it’s still a small share. Tower has more than doubled over the past year, but as I see it most of that is AI silicon photonics expectations, so there’s hardly any part of it you can explain with quantum.

On the Korean listed side, it’s thinner than the US names above. There’s been no announcement yet of Samsung Electronics or SK hynix contract-manufacturing qubit chips, and about the only one that stands out is Sawnics, which joined a KAIST-led quantum photonic integrated circuit project. Sawnics’ share of the government grant is a little over 4% of its equity, and its core business is a foundry for telecom surface acoustic wave (SAW) filters (market cap around KRW 85 billion). Its participation in the government project is confirmed, but quantum contract revenue and its share of orders don’t show up separately in public materials. According to an early October DIGITIMES report, Samsung Electronics set up a Quantum Design Lab in Boston and started hiring, but the research scientist posting on the company’s job board puts the research scope on the theory side, like error correction, architecture, benchmarking and algorithms, with nothing about hardware or fabrication. The hardware research that the Samsung Advanced Institute of Technology (SAIT) lists on its official page is also about pairing superconducting multi-qubit chips with cryo-CMOS control chips, so it’s still some distance from a foundry business that fabs other people’s qubit chips. For the time being, moves on the national infrastructure side will probably show up before anything from listed companies.

I put the chance that Anderon names an external customer by the end of 2027 at roughly even odds (qualitative estimate). There’s a report that Rigetti is in talks, and there’s also a report that Anderon makes wafers for unnamed external customers, but the reason they aren’t disclosed hasn’t been confirmed. In an October company blog post, Anderon said that because it doesn’t design or sell its own chips, it doesn’t compete with customers, but the fact remains that its parent IBM is those customers’ competitor. Naming a customer, winning a customer and shipping to an external customer are each separate events, so a late disclosure doesn’t need to be read straight away as a failure to deliver.

10. Looking Back at the September Investment Map, and the Dates to Check

The call to put GF at number one in September’s [[Investment Map] 23 Companies in the Quantum Cycle: From Materials to Logical Qubits was, you could say, right to a degree. After that piece, Anderon announced its final agreement and first wafer runs in September, and in October Xanadu teamed up with GF. Right before publishing, that piece did fold in the September 8 definitive agreements for D-Wave, Rigetti and Quantinuum, but the final confirmation of GF’s award, which came out the same day, was left out. There are also things to fix. In that piece I wrote that Xanadu fabs at Tower, but as we saw above, Xanadu already mixed several platforms at the time of Aurora, and now GF has been added. There’s nothing showing the Tower collaboration has ended, so the right fix is to amend it to multi-sourcing. Likewise, where I wrote that IBM’s quantum foundry would start up in 2027-2028, the first wafers actually started running in September, faster than I expected, and the roughly 1% government stake in GF was in fact about 1.8% of shares outstanding from the quantum award alone, and on October 14 came the announcement that the government would receive about 4% under a separate agreement.

In the end, I see the quantum foundry less as a business that grows by selling lots of wafers and more as a business where who holds the segregated line sets the value. And that call gets checked on the dates below.

  • 2026-11-04 GF Q3 earnings: Along with the number of quantum customers, the first thing to watch is whether GF says it takes superconducting qubit wafers. If GF publicly names a superconducting qubit customer, the call that the long term goes to the neutral line would be arriving sooner than I thought.
  • 2026-11-05 IonQ Q3 earnings: The company announced it will report results after the market close that day. The point to check against is how it discloses consolidated results and revenue mix after the SkyWater acquisition. If IonQ’s volume turns out to be most of SkyWater’s quantum wafers, it should be read as captive (effectively IonQ’s own dedicated fab) rather than merchant, and if intercompany transactions and quantum volume aren’t broken out separately, I’ll hold off on this call.
  • 2026-12-10 IBM Quantum Day: On its Q3 earnings call, IBM said it would talk about quantum progress in more detail on that day. Whether an Anderon external customer name or talk of a first external wafer comes up here is the thing to watch ahead of year-end.
  • End of 2026: Named external customers and deliveries for Anderon. If no name comes out past year-end and all the way to the end of 2027, my estimate that put it at roughly even odds has to come down.
  • Around February-March 2027, D-Wave’s fiscal 2026 10-K: The renewal question got an early answer on October 13, when the two companies announced a multi-year agreement extension. The remaining point to check against is how the contract term, minimum spending commitments and termination clauses, none of which were in the announcement, are written up in the 10-K. If a filing says D-Wave is leaving SkyWater for Anderon or its own fab, I’ll rewrite the call that the wafers stay for two years.
  • 2027: IonQ Superion 256 customer deliveries, SkyWater SP90 official launch, KRISS 50-qubit system (March target), KAIST National Quantum Fab completion, Copenhagen fab opening.
