1. SpaceX: Hosting economics, orbital compute, financing and direct-to-cell

Speaker: Bret Johnsen, CFO, SpaceX.

Summary: Johnsen said SpaceX is deploying terrestrial compute at costs in the low-to-mid $30s per watt versus his estimate of $50–60 elsewhere, with hosting paybacks below one year. He detailed plans to fund expansion through operating cash flow, customer prepayments, vendor payment terms and debt, including a $40B GPU financing facility. On orbital compute, SpaceX plans initial satellites in 2027, a constellation ramp in 2028 and meaningful gigawatt-scale capacity in 2029.

1. Terrestrial compute costs and vertical integration

Johnsen said SpaceX’s first couple of gigawatts of terrestrial compute average costs in the low-to-mid $30s per watt, compared with approximately $50–60 per watt for the broader industry. He expects SpaceX’s cost curve to decline further with volume, manufacturing improvements and technology advances, while power, labor and construction constraints increase costs elsewhere.

He attributed SpaceX’s advantage to vertical integration across engineering, general contracting, manufacturing and power generation. The company has spent more than a year developing its power-generation capabilities, allowing it to bring up infrastructure in months and energize installed racks within days.

As an example, an external turbine supplier indicated that blades and vanes would arrive 18–24 months after the turbines themselves. SpaceX is establishing its own investment-casting facility to manufacture those components internally, drawing on expertise developed for Raptor engines and other manufacturing operations.

Johnsen said that if SpaceX meets its previously communicated plans, it could become one of the largest AI infrastructure providers in 2027.

2. Sub-one-year paybacks and infrastructure financing

Johnsen said SpaceX’s current terrestrial hosting deals generate paybacks of less than one year, sometimes substantially less.

The company plans to fund growth through operating cash flow and improved working-capital management. That includes collecting customer deposits earlier and extending payment terms with suppliers. Because SpaceX has only been providing third-party compute hosting for roughly two quarters, Johnsen said these opportunities have not yet been fully utilized.

He described financing from three sources: operating cash flow, working-capital optimization and debt. On debt, he referenced investment-grade bonds issued in June and a $40B GPU financing facility arranged with Apollo and Goldman Sachs, with public syndication beginning November 17.

Gavin Baker suggested that customer prepayments could cause operating cash flow to exceed EBITDA materially, and Johnsen agreed. With hosting paybacks inside a year, Johnsen characterized the financing requirement as bridging months rather than years.

3. Short-term contracts, compute demand and Anthropic insourcing

Johnsen said demand for compute continues increasing across the industry. Because SpaceX has a profitable satellite and space business, it did not need to secure large long-term hosting contracts in advance to finance its buildout.

Instead, SpaceX has retained flexibility to offer shorter-term and spot-market capacity, which Johnsen said generates higher returns while preserving the ability to update hardware and redirect capacity.

Asked about Anthropic’s intention to bring more compute infrastructure in-house, Johnsen said its projected growth would require substantially more capacity than simply replacing existing rented compute. He also emphasized demand from other customers seeking available power and compute.

The short-duration hosting strategy gives SpaceX flexibility to allocate capacity toward its own software products as they grow.

4. Orbital compute: 2027 satellites, 2028 ramp and gigawatts in 2029

Johnsen described the successful recovery of Starship’s second stage after deploying 26 satellites as a major milestone toward orbital compute.

He expects rapid second-stage reusability to produce another 10x reduction in launch costs per kilogram. Establishing a regular reflight cadence over the next one to two years is central to SpaceX’s plan to make orbital compute more economical than terrestrial alternatives.

The timetable is:

  • 2027: First orbital-compute satellites launch.
  • 2028: Orbital-compute constellation begins ramping.
  • 2029: Meaningful gigawatt-scale orbital-compute capacity reaches the market.

SpaceX intends to use an architecture based on the Starlink V3 satellite platform, replacing portions of the communications equipment with GPUs, additional solar generation and deployable radiators. Satellites would operate in sun-synchronous orbits to maintain favorable solar exposure and reduce battery requirements.

Johnsen highlighted SpaceX’s existing experience with radiation shielding, radiative cooling, laser inter-satellite links and RF communications across approximately 11,000 Starlink satellites flown.

He described orbital operations as eliminating terrestrial electricity, cooling and land costs, making launch and satellite deployment the central economic considerations.

5. Grokbot, Cursor and first-party software monetization

Johnsen said SpaceX’s software portfolio provides another way to monetize its compute investments, including Grok, Grokbot and Cursor.

He described Grokbot’s growth as strong and said the company is working on cost optimization as usage increases. Cursor adds product development, sales and distribution expertise that SpaceX did not previously have to the same extent.

Baker estimated that SpaceX could be approaching $10B in first-party software revenue, explicitly identifying the figure as his own estimate. Johnsen did not provide a revenue figure but expressed enthusiasm about the software business.

On Grok 4.8 and 4.9, he indicated that releases were coming soon without specifying dates.

6. Starlink Direct-to-Cell: 5G service targeted for 1H28

Johnsen said Starlink broadband now serves more than 13 million people, while SpaceX’s longer-term ambition is to connect billions more through ordinary mobile handsets.

The initial direct-to-cell constellation of approximately 600 satellites was designed as a proof of concept and supports text messaging, including emergency communications.

SpaceX plans to begin launching its next-generation direct-to-cell satellites in 2027, targeting true 5G-quality service directly to unmodified mobile phones in the first half of 2028.

The company has acquired mid-band spectrum from EchoStar and additional low-band spectrum. Johnsen said SpaceX is working with international regulators and telecommunications providers toward global service.

Unlike conventional cellular networks, the proposed system can backhaul traffic through satellites, allowing smaller phased-array terrestrial installations rather than relying primarily on large macro towers.

2. AMD: Easier software migration, the $220B CPU opportunity and Helios deployments

Speaker: Lisa Su, Chair & CEO, AMD.

Summary: Su said AMD’s data-center business will more than double in 2026 and exceed 60% of revenue, while discussing growth above 80% and an even larger data-center mix for the years ahead. She outlined a $220B CPU market opportunity driven by agents, confirmed Q3 shipments of MI455 GPUs and Helios racks, and highlighted Anthropic’s ability to bring its model onto AMD hardware using Claude over a weekend. Su characterized AI demand as generational rather than a traditional semiconductor cycle.

1. Data-center growth and AMD’s changing revenue mix

Su described a $2T compute TAM over the next five years, with substantial growth concentrated in data centers.

Data center represented less than 50% of AMD’s revenue in 2025 and is expected to finish 2026 above 60%, with the business more than doubling this year.

Discussing 2027 and beyond, she cited company-wide year-over-year growth above 80% and data center eventually exceeding 80% of AMD’s business.

She attributed AMD’s positioning to long-term investments in high-performance computing, chiplets, packaging and customer collaboration. AMD has focused on designing hardware around the evolving requirements of hyperscalers and frontier AI labs.

2. ROCm and AI-assisted software migration

Su discussed how improvements in frontier models are reducing the work required to support different hardware architectures.

She cited Anthropic bringing its model onto AMD hardware using one engineer and Claude over a weekend. The moderator described the process as taking approximately three days, compared with nine to twelve months historically.

Su said frontier models have become exceptionally capable at software engineering and kernel optimization over the preceding six to nine months.

AMD has acquired approximately ten software companies and substantially expanded its software engineering organization to improve ROCm and the broader developer ecosystem.

She emphasized continued collaboration with frontier labs and the growing importance of allowing customers to choose among CPUs, GPUs, FPGAs and custom accelerators.

3. Agentic computing expands the CPU opportunity to $220B

Su said the CPU market historically represented approximately $20–30B and grew at single-digit rates. AMD now expects the addressable market to reach approximately $220B over the next four to five years.

She attributed that expansion to agentic workloads, where CPUs orchestrate tasks, coordinate software execution and manage tool calls alongside accelerator-based inference.

Su highlighted AMD’s EPYC portfolio, including Genoa, Turin and the recently launched Venice generation, emphasizing performance, performance per watt and performance per dollar.

She expects agentic workloads to require a mix of CPU and accelerator computing as applications become more capable and execute increasingly complex tasks.

4. MI455 and Helios deployments

Su said AMD began shipping its MI455 GPUs and Helios rack-scale systems in Q3 2026.

She identified OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle as customers bringing up these systems in their data centers.

She said AMD is accelerating its product cadence to improve performance per dollar as models and workloads evolve.

On portfolio breadth, Su emphasized that AMD can combine CPUs, GPUs, FPGAs, networking and custom silicon technologies. She also highlighted Xilinx’s more than 6,000 customers across aerospace, defense, industrial and test-and-measurement markets.

5. AI investment cycle, supply and export controls

Asked whether the current cycle could follow the historical semiconductor pattern of supply shortages followed by overcapacity, Su said AMD is not seeing the typical digestion phase.

She described AI as a generational shift, supported by productivity improvements across businesses, with customers making investments over three-to-five-year horizons.

Su emphasized balancing investment against expected returns while avoiding both underinvestment and excessive capacity expansion.

On the new U.S. export restrictions, she said AMD anticipated many of the changes and had received approvals for several export packages. Even with the restrictions, she expects demand to remain substantially above supply.

Regarding NVIDIA’s leadership situation, Su praised NVIDIA and its management team and emphasized AMD’s focus on execution, customer partnerships and delivering against commitments.

3. Fireworks: $1.5B revenue run rate, specialized inference and enterprise AI economics

Speaker: Lin Qiao, Co-founder & CEO, Fireworks AI.

Summary: Qiao said Fireworks reached a $1.5B revenue run rate at the end of Q3, with 13x growth over nine months excluding its largest customer and 12x enterprise token growth over six months. More than 80% of platform traffic now comes from customized models or tailored deployments. Fireworks combines training, inference and model optimization, with Qiao citing 5–10x lower costs for specialized models versus off-the-shelf alternatives.

1. Revenue growth, enterprise adoption and financial position

Qiao said Fireworks reached a $1.5B revenue run rate at the end of Q3 2026.

Excluding its largest customer, the company grew 13x over nine months. The moderator described that growth as approximately $100M to $1.3B.

Enterprise token consumption increased 12x over the preceding six months, making enterprise workloads the fastest-growing part of the platform.

Qiao said Fireworks currently holds more cash than the total amount it has raised from investors. The company is increasing spending on compute procurement and talent while continuing to focus on gross-margin improvement through differentiated products.

2. Specialized intelligence and proprietary enterprise data

Qiao distinguished generalized foundation models trained on broad datasets from specialized models trained and optimized around individual businesses.

She argued that coding agents make application development easier and reduce the differentiation of products built on the same generic model APIs.

Businesses, however, possess private datasets reflecting their products, customers, processes and industry-specific knowledge. Fireworks aims to turn those datasets into specialized intelligence that can improve continuously with product usage.

She described two priorities for customers: maintaining control of proprietary intellectual property and controlling the variable cost of running AI applications.

Qiao said some customers are finding that usage growth creates substantial inference expenses, making workload-specific model optimization increasingly important.

3. Integrated training and inference platform

Fireworks provides training, inference and model assembly through one platform.

Qiao emphasized the importance of consistency between training and deployment, especially for long-running agents executing hundreds or thousands of steps. She said small discrepancies across many steps can accumulate into significant errors.

Fireworks integrates both sides of the process to preserve model behavior and automatically optimize trained models for inference.

She cited its Ultra Fast offering delivering more than 2,000 tokens per second and said specialized models can reduce inference costs by 5–10x relative to off-the-shelf foundation models.

The company also automates work associated with evaluation frameworks and compute-capacity planning, which Qiao said can otherwise take internal machine-learning teams three to six months.

4. More than 80% of traffic is tailored to specific workloads

Qiao said more than 80% of Fireworks traffic is generated by customized models or customized deployments optimized for particular workloads.

That portion of traffic increased 5x over the preceding six months.

She expects businesses to develop increasingly specialized models for different functions, with continuous cycles of training, evaluation, deployment and improvement.

Fireworks intends to support open and closed foundation models together, including routing workloads to the most suitable model and allowing customers to customize open models using their own data.

5. Compute strategy and demand outlook

Fireworks operates across three infrastructure arrangements: capacity it procures for customers, customer-owned capacity and partner-provided capacity.

The company can initially host customers that have not established their long-term capacity requirements and later move its platform onto infrastructure procured directly by those customers.

Qiao said owning more physical compute is ultimately a question of timing. As Fireworks grows, supply availability becomes an increasingly important consideration.

On the broader market, she forecast a 100x reduction in AI cost per task over the next two years alongside more than 1,000x growth in token consumption.

She expects agent-to-agent communication and autonomous workflows operating at machine speed to drive the next wave of demand.

4. Druckenmiller: Capital preservation, AI applications and the next investment opportunity

Speaker: Stanley Druckenmiller, Founder, Duquesne Family Office.

Summary: Druckenmiller said his equity book is approximately flat on a net basis, he is short copper and his focus is capital preservation. The economic and market signals that supported his bullish position in January have largely reversed. He remains interested in AI but is looking increasingly toward applications rather than infrastructure, including a potential shift by leading labs from selling tokens to selling completed tasks.

1. Positioning and macroeconomic outlook

Druckenmiller described his January positioning as long equities, short Treasuries as a hedge and long copper, with a favorable view of the economy and AI infrastructure spending.

His outlook was supported by strong nominal growth, accommodative monetary policy, rising commodities, broad equity-market participation and tight credit spreads.

He said nearly all those indicators have reversed. The Fed is now tightening, commodities have weakened, credit spreads have widened and market breadth has deteriorated.

He noted that the advance/decline line peaked on August 14 while major equity averages peaked in October.

His equity portfolio is now approximately flat on a net basis, with offsetting long and short positions, and he is short copper. He described his current approach as capital preservation, citing exceptionally low visibility into the next twelve months.

Druckenmiller also expressed concern about possible economic disappointment in the second half of 2027, pointing to weakening commodities and defensive stocks outperforming cyclicals.

2. AI use cases rather than infrastructure

Druckenmiller said he is looking for the next AI investment opportunities in applications rather than infrastructure.

He acknowledged that infrastructure stocks may offer tactical opportunities after their sharp declines but does not expect the group to repeat the same multi-year performance that it delivered over the previous four years.

He suggested that one of the most interesting application opportunities could be the frontier labs themselves.

His view is that leading labs could increasingly sell completed tasks rather than tokens or model access, building businesses around their intelligence.

He highlighted cybersecurity and healthcare as potential areas where frontier models could create substantial value.

3. AI risks: financing and politics

Asked which AI bear case he takes most seriously, Druckenmiller identified financing and politics.

He said the market remains difficult to short because of the pace of innovation and the magnitude of the equity selloff, despite his concerns about the broader economic backdrop.

He also expressed longer-term concern about fiscal deficits, predicting that significant U.S. fiscal reform could eventually emerge following a financial-market crisis.

Other issues he highlighted included deteriorating K–12 education and the importance of immigration in sustaining American technological leadership.

4. Risk management and investment process

Druckenmiller described his portfolio’s profit and loss as a primary risk-management indicator. When positions stop behaving as expected, he reduces exposure.

He said his strongest returns have historically come during concentrated periods of opportunity separated by quieter stretches.

His approach is to establish an initial position once he has sufficient conviction, then conduct more extensive research before deciding whether to add or exit. He described this as investing first and investigating afterward.

He emphasized intellectual humility, flexibility and discipline, citing his decision to repurchase Nasdaq stocks near the 2000 peak after previously selling them as an example of allowing emotion to override his process.

Looking ahead ten years, he also assigned at least a 70% probability to data centers operating in space and expressed optimism about fusion energy and AI-assisted progress against cancer.

5. Altimeter: Anthropic’s compute economics, IPO positioning and model-layer concentration

Speaker: Brad Gerstner, Founder & CEO, Altimeter.

Summary: Gerstner argued that Anthropic’s inference economics are significantly more attractive than the market appreciates. He cited a revenue run rate approaching $100B with approximately 5,000 employees, while only 25–26% of its 2GW compute base was used to serve customers during the first nine months of 2026. He expects economic value to concentrate among a small number of leading model providers and described strong institutional demand for Anthropic’s IPO.

1. Anthropic’s compute economics and profitability

Gerstner said Anthropic is approaching a $100B annualized revenue run rate with approximately 5,000 employees.

He contrasted that with Google’s approximately 105,000 employees when it reached a similar revenue level.

He argued that substantially lower labor intensity allows foundation-model companies to devote more spending to compute while maintaining attractive economics.

He also cited compute unit costs falling roughly 90% annually as model efficiency improves.

The central data point was Anthropic’s compute allocation during the first nine months of 2026. Gerstner said approximately 25–26% of its 2GW compute base, or roughly 500MW, supported customer-serving inference.

Most of the remaining capacity was devoted to training.

He argued that significant revenue growth can therefore be achieved with substantially less incremental serving compute than total infrastructure spending might suggest, while frontier-model development continues requiring large training investments.

2. IPO demand and public-market positioning

Gerstner described Anthropic’s IPO book as approximately 10x oversubscribed and expected it to represent the largest order in his firm’s history.

He said the transaction was scheduled to price and allocate on November 18, with trading beginning November 19.

He highlighted the difficult market backdrop, including substantial declines in semiconductor equities and concern around U.S. data-center construction.

Gerstner expects Anthropic’s stock to experience considerable volatility after listing, pointing to large peak-to-trough declines experienced by other major IPOs.

He distinguished his approach across investment vehicles. In his hedge fund, he would consider hedging exposure if Anthropic’s valuation overshoots, while private and long-only holdings could be managed with longer investment horizons.

3. Economic value concentrates in the model layer

Gerstner described himself as a model maximalist, expecting most economic value in foundational intelligence to accrue to the top one, two or three model providers.

He compared the potential concentration to earlier technology cycles in search, mobile, social media and cloud computing.

His investment framework focuses on businesses that benefit as general intelligence improves.

He specifically raised the possibility that more capable foundation models could absorb functionality currently provided by specialized application software or agent harnesses.

Gerstner said his team evaluates opportunities based on the potential size of the market and whether a company’s economics improve as model capabilities advance.

4. NVIDIA governance and the board letter

Gerstner discussed the letter he and Gavin Baker sent to NVIDIA’s board concerning its leadership situation.

He said the board acknowledged the correspondence.

His central request was that directors gather the relevant facts and make a considered decision without rushing in response to political pressure.

He emphasized his preference for founder-led technology companies while supporting accountability and the board’s responsibility to shareholders.

5. Invest America and broadening capital ownership

Gerstner also discussed Invest America, the initiative to provide children with investment accounts and encourage additional contributions from families, employers and philanthropists.

He highlighted Michael and Susan Dell’s contribution and Elon Musk’s pledge to contribute SpaceX shares.

Gerstner views the program as a way to broaden stock-market participation and help Americans share in the economic gains created by technology.

6. Apollo: AI infrastructure financing, private credit and the capital spending cycle

Speaker: Marc Rowan, Co-founder & CEO, Apollo Global Management.

Summary: Rowan described AI and data-center spending as part of a global industrial investment cycle requiring increasingly large amounts of long-duration financing. Apollo is focused on secured, investment-grade opportunities backed by infrastructure, energy and computing assets. Rowan expects technology companies to account for six of the ten largest corporate bond issuers within three to four years.

1. AI financing and the global industrial investment cycle

Rowan said the scale of the AI buildout is creating substantial demand for long-duration financing.

He described the investment cycle as extending beyond the U.S., with Canada and the Gulf region each potentially deploying approximately $1T across major infrastructure projects.

He separated financing sources by need: commercial banks for shorter-term borrowing, public bonds for straightforward financing and private capital for complex projects requiring customized structures.

Apollo focuses on financing long-duration assets and infrastructure, including data centers, energy generation and computing equipment.

Rowan said the current technology investment cycle is unusually large relative to historical infrastructure expansions and expects technology companies to become increasingly significant corporate debt issuers.

2. Secured credit and GPU financing

Rowan said elevated valuations and uncertainty favor more conservative credit structures.

His preference is to move from unsecured to secured exposure and from lower-quality borrowers toward investment-grade counterparties.

He highlighted financing secured by current-generation chips with repayment schedules amortizing over approximately five years. He characterized this as an investment with an average duration of roughly two and a half years.

He also emphasized energy infrastructure, noting that the U.S. has underinvested in power generation and distribution for many years.

Rowan distinguished the returns required by equity investors from the repayment objectives of creditors, whose earnings are primarily interest and principal.

3. Large technology companies can absorb uneven AI returns

Rowan argued that many of the largest companies financing AI infrastructure have substantial existing businesses outside their AI investments.

He said some individual projects may produce weak returns or stranded capital spending without necessarily undermining the entire corporate borrower.

He contrasted those companies with more concentrated neocloud businesses, citing examples of enterprises with $50–60B equity valuations while their bonds yielded approximately 15%.

He also said technology borrowers can offer wider spreads than similarly rated industrial companies because of the unusually large amount of financing they require.

4. Apollo’s insurance platform and long-term capital

Rowan described Apollo as managing slightly more than $1T, split approximately evenly between capital associated with its insurance operations and third-party investment funds.

He said the retirement-services business has expanded from essentially zero in 2008 to more than $500B.

The insurance platform provides long-duration capital that can be invested alongside outside investors in infrastructure and credit transactions.

Rowan also discussed expanding technology infrastructure for private markets, including estimated daily valuations, trading, custody and settlement capabilities.

5. Productivity, inflation and workforce changes

Rowan said infrastructure spending is increasing demand for steel, concrete, glass, copper, energy and skilled labor.

He expects continued strength in physical trades such as electricians, plumbers and construction specialists, while AI changes employment requirements in consulting, law, accounting and enterprise software.

Within Apollo, he cited an 800-person Mumbai operation targeting 50% more capacity with unchanged headcount through automation and process redesign.

He also described Austin as Apollo’s second headquarters, with future headcount expansion concentrated there while employment in New York, California and London remains flat.

7. Dell: 6,500 AI-factory customers, enterprise adoption and infrastructure demand

Speaker: Michael Dell, Chairman & CEO, Dell Technologies.

Summary: Dell said the company had 6,500 AI-factory customers at the end of its last reported quarter, including 3,300 added over the preceding three quarters. He described enterprise AI adoption as being in its early stages, with the largest opportunity coming from redesigning business processes around autonomous agents. Dell also highlighted triple-digit server and networking growth, rapid cluster deployment and AI-driven internal operating efficiencies.

1. AI-factory customer growth

Dell said approximately 3,200 customers built AI factories with the company over the first two years, followed by another 3,300 during the last three reported quarters.

The total reached approximately 6,500 customers by the end of the most recent reported quarter.

He said the server and networking business had grown more than 100% across the recent reported-quarter period.

Dell emphasized that customers are continuing to expand their deployments rather than treating their initial AI-factory purchases as one-time investments.

The customer base spans hyperscalers, frontier labs, neoclouds, sovereign customers, enterprises, smaller businesses and public-sector organizations.

2. Enterprise AI is moving toward process redesign

Dell said most organizations are still using AI primarily as individual productivity tools.

He sees a larger opportunity when enterprises redesign their processes around the technology, combining organizational data, specialized models and autonomous agents.

He described a progression from mapping existing processes and preparing data to selecting models and ultimately deploying AI systems across the business.

This creates demand for GPUs, CPUs, storage and networking.

Dell said CPUs become important for executing agents, while storage and networking support the data and memory required by more complex applications.

3. AI economics extend beyond foundation-model revenue

Dell argued that AI’s economic value is generated throughout the broader economy, not only through revenue earned by the major model providers.

He highlighted use cases in healthcare, manufacturing, financial services, transportation, logistics and retail.

He emphasized generating intelligence close to the underlying data, including within enterprise facilities and smaller data centers.

Dell expects open models, specialized models and different compute architectures to coexist as organizations optimize for their own workloads and requirements.

4. Deployment speed, supply chain and reliability

Dell attributed his company’s position to its existing server and storage franchise, manufacturing scale and supply-chain expertise.

He said the company can support customers deploying 100,000 GPUs in approximately four to six weeks by assembling and testing racks in its factories before shipment.

Racks can become operational the day they arrive at a prepared facility.

Dell emphasized time to first token, networking and system reliability as important parts of delivering large AI clusters.

5. Internal AI deployment and operating leverage

Dell said he and COO Jeff Clarke are building a fleet of internal agents to help manage the company.

These systems increasingly identify anomalies in daily reporting and support faster decisions.

He also pointed to improved efficiency across the company and additional software-development capacity created by AI-assisted engineering.

Dell expects that capacity to support expansion into adjacent markets and accelerate internal product development.

On his broader outlook, he said AI continues becoming more capable, driving new opportunities across medicine, biology, energy and materials science.

8. Meta: Data-center community agreements, smart glasses and AI safety

Speaker: Dina Powell McCormick, President & Vice Chairman, Meta.

Summary: Powell McCormick focused on Meta’s efforts to build data centers with stronger local community support, expand smart-glasses applications and strengthen AI safety oversight. She highlighted more than $2B in projected electricity-cost benefits around Meta’s Richland Parish development, a $1B community initiative, industrial glasses pilots with NextEra Energy and an eight-week delay to the Muse launch for additional safety work.

1. Community agreements and data-center development

Powell McCormick said Meta intends to develop infrastructure in communities that welcome the investment and experience meaningful economic benefits.

She used Richland Parish, Louisiana, as an example, where Meta is working with its power partner to reduce local electricity costs by more than $2B over the lifetime of the project.

She also highlighted $50,000 bonuses paid to teachers through local tax-surplus arrangements, compared with starting salaries of approximately $37,000.

Powell McCormick said large data-center developments can employ approximately 7,000 construction workers and emphasized Meta’s partnerships with local workers, unions and state governments.

She described domestic computing infrastructure as important to U.S. economic and national security.

2. Community investment and the Future Is for Everyone initiative

Meta has established a $1B initiative over four years to support community benefits associated with AI and infrastructure development.

Powell McCormick said the program will focus on priorities including educators, first responders, electricity affordability and child protection.

She also discussed industry projections of approximately $10T in capital investment, 250GW of power and two million construction and trade jobs over the coming years.

Meta is emphasizing the employment, tax and infrastructure benefits associated with its data-center projects as it engages with local communities.

3. Muse, safety governance and product development

Powell McCormick said Zuckerberg has moved his desk into Meta’s AI lab and works closely with the team developing new AI products.

She highlighted Muse as an agentic AI product designed around simplicity and ease of use.

Meta delayed its launch by eight weeks for additional safety work.

She said more than 220 employees are responsible for safety across the lab and described new governance measures, including independent board-level escalation for researchers and outside reviews of safety procedures.

4. Smart glasses and enterprise applications

Powell McCormick discussed expanding adoption of Meta’s AI glasses beyond conventional consumer applications.

She said Oakley glasses are certified as safety glasses and highlighted pilots with NextEra Energy and other industrial partners.

Workers can use the glasses to photograph inventory, communicate with supervisors and receive repair instructions without separately using tablets or radios.

She also described an agricultural business using glasses alongside Muse to inspect crops and receive instructions informed by weather conditions.

Powell McCormick discussed Meta-branded glasses priced around $240 and Ray-Ban versions around $350 or more, alongside design partnerships with Ray-Ban, Oakley and Kylie Jenner.

Powell McCormick also said small businesses using AI are recovering substantial time from routine operations, while new agents are making it easier for individual entrepreneurs to launch and manage businesses.

9. TomorrowX Founders: The AI capex cycle and the changing influence of retail investors

Speakers: Gavin Baker, Managing Partner & CIO, Atreides Management; Antonio Gracias, Founder, CEO & CIO, Valor Equity Partners; Ron Biscardi, Co-founder & CEO, iConnections.

Summary: Baker argued that a slower, supply-constrained AI infrastructure buildout could support a longer investment cycle by reducing the risk of overcapacity. He contrasted the possibility of a telecom-style boom and bust with a multidecade infrastructure expansion. The founders also discussed TomorrowX’s objective of connecting management teams and private companies with retail investors and independent financial-media communities.

1. The AI investment cycle: telecom versus railroads

Baker contrasted two potential outcomes for AI infrastructure spending.

The first resembles telecommunications infrastructure, where a rapid investment surge produced substantial excess capacity and a prolonged period of weak investment.

The second resembles the railroad buildout, which extended across several decades as demand for the infrastructure continued expanding.

He said the deciding factors are whether AI becomes increasingly economically useful and whether lower token costs continue generating additional demand.

Baker also noted that spending can shift among land, power, computing, memory, networking and storage as different parts of the infrastructure develop.

2. Why a slower buildout could be favorable

Baker said higher interest rates, wider credit spreads and shortages of power and semiconductor manufacturing capacity are slowing the pace of AI expansion.

He argued that these constraints can reduce the risk of a sudden capacity surge followed by a sharp downturn.

His preferred trajectory is a slower, smoother period of expansion rather than a rapid spike and subsequent contraction.

He described AI-equity valuations as already reflecting substantial concern about peak infrastructure spending.

3. The role of retail investors and independent research

Baker described a semiconductor-equipment stock rising 7% despite a major investment-bank downgrade after appearing in a research basket published by Citrini.

He used the example to illustrate the growing influence of independent research, Substack, X and retail-investor communities.

TomorrowX was designed partly to connect these audiences with corporate management teams and private companies, providing access similar to that traditionally available through institutional investment conferences.

4. TomorrowX and broader AI adoption

The organizers said more than 100 private companies were represented at the conference, many by their CEOs.

They also described more than 1,000 scholarships for active contributors on X, Substack and Reddit.

Gracias emphasized the importance of demonstrating AI’s benefits to ordinary Americans, including improvements in productivity, healthcare, education and creativity.

Biscardi discussed iConnections’ role in bringing its institutional event and networking infrastructure to a broader audience.

10. Goldman Sachs: Application-layer opportunities, AI-driven productivity and engineering jobs

Speaker: David Solomon, Chairman & CEO, Goldman Sachs.

Summary: Solomon described the recent AI-market correction as a recalibration of the growth curve rather than the end of the technology cycle. He expects the largest long-term value creation to emerge in the application layer. At Goldman, AI is changing engineering requirements and enabling the redesign of operating processes, with efficiencies intended to support business expansion.

1. AI investment cycle and the application layer

Solomon said AI investment remains in the early stages of a long-term technology transition.

He described the current market environment as one in which investors are reassessing growth expectations as more information becomes available.

He expects substantial additional investment in AI infrastructure but believes the largest economic opportunities over the next ten to twenty years will emerge in applications.

Solomon compared the opportunity with the development of mobile and internet platforms that eventually supported businesses and products that were not envisioned during the initial infrastructure buildout.

He emphasized that technological progress can occur much faster than enterprises are able to adapt their operations.

2. Goldman’s engineering workforce and AI adoption

Solomon said Goldman employs approximately 13,000 engineers within its roughly 45,000-person workforce.

He expects the firm could have fewer engineers in five years as AI automates more routine development work, while increasing the importance of highly skilled senior engineers.

He also noted that Goldman’s newest partner class included its highest-ever proportion of engineering and technology professionals.

The company spends more than $6B annually on technology.

3. Process automation and operating efficiency

Goldman initially selected six major business processes for redesign through AI, including client onboarding, know-your-customer requirements and anti-money-laundering operations.

The firm subsequently added two more processes.

Solomon said AI is making it possible to automate workflows that historically required substantial manual work.

He sees the resulting efficiency as a way to expand Goldman’s capacity, client coverage and business footprint.

Rather than directing every efficiency improvement toward margin expansion, Goldman intends to reinvest resources into growth.

4. Wealth management and human relationships

Solomon described Goldman’s wealth-management business as focused on clients with approximately $25M or more in investable assets.

The platform serves roughly 45,000 clients globally and provides investment management, planning and access to private-market opportunities.

He expects technology to enhance the business but emphasized that high-end wealth management will remain relationship-driven.

Solomon also discussed Goldman’s $500M commitment to an AI institute associated with its 10,000 Small Businesses initiative, supporting AI education, research and access to capital.

5. Macro outlook and NVIDIA

Solomon expressed optimism about American technological innovation over the next decade while acknowledging near-term geopolitical and market uncertainty.

He said the economy can remain healthy with higher interest rates than those prevailing during much of the post-financial-crisis period.

On NVIDIA, he emphasized the need for a careful process as leadership questions are addressed and expressed confidence in the company’s broader management team, including CFO Colette Kress.

11. Sound Ventures: Anthropic, founder selection and private-market investing

Speaker: Guy Oseary, Co-founder, Sound Ventures.

Summary: Oseary discussed Sound Ventures’ early investment in Anthropic, his emphasis on founder quality and the role of storytelling in technology-company differentiation. He also introduced Get Access, a curated investment platform designed to connect private companies with smaller investors who can provide strategic value beyond capital.

1. Anthropic and investing in founders

Oseary said he recognized AI as a major investment opportunity immediately after ChatGPT launched in November 2022.

He contacted Salesforce Ventures to learn about emerging companies and was introduced to Anthropic.

He subsequently met Dario Amodei and invested based on his conviction in the founder’s technical vision, commitment and long-term objectives.

The moderator cited Sound Ventures’ initial $35M Anthropic investment in 2023 and additional investments in subsequent financing rounds.

Oseary emphasized evaluating the people building a company, particularly their ability to articulate and execute a long-term vision.

2. Sound Ventures and the importance of storytelling

Oseary said Sound Ventures manages approximately $2B and has invested across technology companies including Airbnb, Uber, Spotify, OpenAI and Anthropic.

He described the firm’s experience in entertainment, marketing and storytelling as a way to help portfolio companies differentiate themselves.

His approach involves working with founders on explaining their products clearly, communicating their mission and reaching audiences beyond the technical community.

He said companies increasingly face many well-funded competitors pursuing similar ideas, making positioning, product communication and distribution important.

3. Get Access and private-market participation

Oseary described Get Access as a platform connecting private investment opportunities with a curated community of investors.

He said fewer than 7% of applicants are admitted and that more than 100 investors participate in the network.

The platform is designed for individuals who can contribute expertise, relationships or other strategic value to companies.

Investment opportunities can accommodate commitments as small as $2,500 or $5,000, compared with the much larger commitments typically associated with institutional private-market transactions.

Oseary described approximately weekly deal opportunities and emphasized education, allowing members to learn about emerging companies even when they choose not to invest.

His longer-term objective is for Get Access to become an important source of private-company investment opportunities for strategically valuable individual investors.
