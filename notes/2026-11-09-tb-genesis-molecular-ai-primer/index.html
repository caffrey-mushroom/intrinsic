Introduction

Questions

Can you describe what Genesis Molecular AI does and the products and services it provides?

Expert: It is an AI-enabled drug discovery company. The products and services it provides are, it has pharma partnerships where it deploys its AI computational platform to enable pharma partners' drug pipelines and it is also developing its own drug pipeline.

Could you describe the potential rationale behind Genesis developing its products? Which key unmet medical needs or gaps does the company aim to resolve in the market?

Expert: For the medical needs, as of the time that I was there and this is public information too, the pipeline was focused on oncology and immunology. For the platform component, the hypothesis is that having state-of-the-art computational methods using a combination of AI-based methods as well as physics- and simulation-based methods was a gap in the market and so that is the unique angle that the company's platform addresses.

What is the full universe of therapeutic indications and patient populations that Genesis's platform can credibly target, independent of the company's current stage, based on incidence, prevalence, clinical segmentation, geographical concentration of diagnosed and treated patients and reimbursement accessibility?

Expert: At the time, the emphasis of the company was on largely small molecules. I suppose that's just the starting point. Again, things could have changed. I think that's also public information. The initial focus was oncology and immunology. There is no reason that the company could not go into other areas, so I think it could very easily target a broad segment of additional therapeutic indications. There is nothing fundamentally about the platform that prohibits the company from going there. I think it's just about focus and using resources wisely, but the full scope can cover a variety of different diseases. It can cover the clinical segmentation spectrum. It is nothing specific to any particular geography or treated patients. It can span rare diseases to more common diseases. There is nothing fundamentally limiting about the choice for oncology and immunology.

Could you share any publicly disclosed entities involved in trials, partnerships or licensing deals that have been announced?

Expert: The publicly disclosed partners are Incyte and Gilead. Those are, I believe, currently the ones that are publicly disclosed. There is nothing that's public that I'm aware of for clinical trials right now.

Based on clinical trial registrations, regulatory filings and partnership geography that's publicly available, which regions appear to be Genesis's primary commercial and development focus?

Expert: There are no clinical trial registrations as far as I'm aware of. For regulatory filings, also none as far as I'm aware of. The partners that the company has worked with are primarily US-based, so I would imagine, but again, it's not public information. If you were to infer from the location of the partners in the US, it's certainly a very likely potential market.

Analyst: Are there any factors that drive prioritisation?

Expert: I think this is similar factors to how different companies think about which geographies to go for. There is nothing particular about the geography that determines the targets that make it better for a particular geography or not, but I'm sure the standard things are: you think about the ease of running clinical trials, you think about the potential reimbursement, you think about the regulatory landscape and a lot of that leads to starting with the US, potentially Europe, parts of Asia.

How would you segment Genesis's pipeline or product portfolio by distinct revenue-generating potential, and what stage is it at?

Expert: The segments that are public are oncology and immunology. As of right now, I am not aware of any disclosed clinical trials. That means I suppose there's no current phase since it's all preclinical right now. Then, of course, the other product portfolio is the platform, the commercialisation of the platform and that is through the Incyte and current Gilead partnerships. The details of that are public.

How do you rank the pipeline, based on revenue generation capabilities? What might be the key assumptions behind the ranking?

Expert: The pipeline, I think oncology and immunology are very high revenue-generating. The thing is that, at least as I'm aware of and as public information, all of the programmes are still preclinical, so your probability of success is you are doing an expected value and anything that's preclinical is still low. Very high if any of these drugs succeed. They are multibillion-dollar per year drugs, but the expected value is low because it's preclinical. The platform deals, there's already an upfront component and then there are milestone-based. That one, I think the potential upside is not as high as your actual drug pipeline, but your likelihood of success is that your expected values or at least your probability is higher, because you receive payments for different milestones.

Analyst: Could you rank oncology, immunology and platform from one to three?

Expert: I will probably [say] platform as one because expected value is higher and then two and three, then for oncology and immunology.

What is the revenue model for each of Genesis's segments? Is it milestone-driven, collaboration-based or royalty-based?

Expert: Platform is up front. The revenue model is upfront payments plus milestone-based payments and I believe that also includes a share of royalties as well. For oncology and immunology, the revenue model will be that "If Genesis brings the asset to the clinic, then it's your own." You get revenue from that or you could sell the asset.

Based on publicly available information, could you discuss a use case for each of Genesis's segments?

Expert: The use case for the drugs is to solve unmet medical need and to drug patients and then the use case for the platform is to assist pharma partners with their drug pipeline.

Analyst: Could you provide a use case for each segment, starting with oncology specifically?

Expert: The use case is to discover novel oncology drugs.

Analyst: What does the company provide? How do customers, partners, peers or patients interact with it?

Expert: The company provides a drug. The customer is, if it's a patient, the patient will take the drug because the focus of the company is small molecules. It's likely orally administered and taken on some regular cadence and hopefully provides therapeutic benefit. For the platform, the use case is a drug discovery team and the drug discovery team will partner with Genesis and consume the outputs of the platform in order to advance. Those outputs are, for example, a set of recommended small molecule designs and they will use that to advance their own drug pipeline.

Analyst: What has the company disclosed about the mechanism of action, starting with the oncology segment?

Expert: The mechanism of action, the thing that the company has disclosed a pan-mutant PIK3CA target. It is working on a potent and selective pan-mutant allosteric inhibitor of PIK3CA. That's the only public explicit one in oncology. There's another one that is a cell death promoter. Then, for immunology, let's see what is publicly disclosed. The mechanism of action for the immunology ones tends to be well-validated targets where the current standard of care or standard of research is that people are working on biologics, but there is no small molecule available and so the mechanism of action there is the same target, but Genesis's angle is that it is using small molecules as the modality instead of biologics.

Analyst: Could you discuss the platform's features? What inputs and outputs is the platform generating?

Expert: For the platform, there are a variety of different inputs. The most important input is the target. What is the target that, as of the time, the main focus of the company was not novel target discovery, but more taking well-known targets and drugging them in ways that other companies would not be able to drug. The most important input to the platform is the target. Additionally, you can also depending on which stage you are in, hit ID or lead-up, etc, you can either provide a set of existing products or existing designs and what their associated potency values are. Then, from there, the platform would do a few things. The first one is that it would generate new and novel designs. The second one is that it would screen them for both potency and ADMET features. In order to do that, it would use a combination of AI-based models. One big thing that a company does is structure prediction and so it uses different models to predict the three-dimensional shape of the protein as well as how the potential small molecule would bind to that target.

One of the features that the company has released publicly is its own model called Pearl, which is a competitor to AlphaFold or to Boltz or to Chai that does diffusion-based ML prediction of protein and ligand binding. In addition to that, Genesis also has a lot of proprietary ML, especially ML and some physics-based methods, to predict the ADMET profile, absorption and how the compound will be metabolised in the body for things that require crossing the blood-brain barrier, how well the molecule potentially would be able to cross the blood-brain barrier. It has all of that and then after doing screening, it presents all of the information to the scientists. The scientists are able to digest that, visualise that, perform additional filtering or annotations and then get a short list of small molecule designs that they then advance into their testing. All of the data that comes out of testing then gets ingested back into the platform. The models are retrained and that loop is repeated.

Analyst: How would you populate the pipeline for each – oncology, immunology and platform? What are the primary disease areas, lead programmes or biological mechanisms that the company is targeting in each category?

Expert: I think I have already answered that question. Oncology and immunology are the therapeutic areas and the mechanism actions are what I provided to you.

Could you specify the industry that best describes Genesis's core operations?

Expert: Two of them, biotechnology and artificial intelligence.

What has the biotechnology and AI market's AAGR been over the past three years – less than 0%, 0-5%, 6-10%, 11-20% or more than 20%?

Expert: I would say that for biology, I believe that the biotech market's expected growth rate is 11-20%. On AI, especially the AI-enabled biotech, the growth rate there is more than 20%.

What do you think have been the 1-2 key drivers of market growth over the past three years?

Expert: Actually, both biotech and, obviously, AI. The biggest driver has just been the advent of AI and how AI and various computation methods enable companies to shorten the loop. Typically, the previous paradigm is that you generate designs and you hand-generate designs. You evaluate them manually and then you choose a few, bring them to the lab and then test them, but now AI can generate new designs and screen them at a vast scale prior to a scientist even needing to make a decision. It's able to search computational space at a much faster rate than humans could and so that's the big difference.

What do you think the market's AAGR will be over the next three years?

Expert: I would say, for biotech, I think it will continue to be 11-20%. For the AI-enabled segment, it will certainly be more than 20%.

In your opinion, what will be the 1-2 key drivers of future market growth?

Expert: The biggest changes will be that, I think, the fuel for the AI-driven growth is two things. One is compute and the second one is data. For compute, right now, compute is expensive, but as compute becomes more widely accessible and the cost of tokens go down, compute will become a lot cheaper and a lot more widely used. The other big bottleneck is data and there's a lot of investment into data generation to enable AI models to make better predictions. Those are the specific drivers for that.

To what degree do you think the industry is correlated to economic cyclicality? Is it counter-cyclical, very cyclical, somewhat cyclical, somewhat resilient or resilient?

Expert: I would say it's somewhat resilient. People will always get sick and need treatment. So, there's always going to be a demand for therapeutics. The reason why it's not completely resilient is the level of investment. The demand is always there, but the funding is the thing that is dependent on the market. The funding is what enables new research, enables compute, enables data generation and the funding is what is sensitive to the market, so that is certainly a limitation.

Analyst: How might the answer differ from the perspective of other stakeholders, such as patients, healthcare providers or VC investors?

Expert: That was my answer already. For patients and healthcare providers, the need will always be there. I think the need for medicine is resilient to the market, but of course, for VC investors and funding, that's where it's a lot more cyclical.

Based on public information, what impactful technological evolutions or innovations should we anticipate in the biotechnology and AI market over the next 3-5 years?

Expert: A lot of the recent ones, AlphaFold, I think, was a good example of a diffusion-based structure prediction system or a foundational model for that. I would expect a lot more architectural in the technological innovations in that and I think again what drives the technological innovations there are doing additional modalities. Right now, I guess maybe predicting more things. Right now, a lot of tools in the industry are quite segmented, so design is segmented from structure prediction, which is segmented from toxicology predictions, but I think over time, all of these will become a single prediction that a single foundational model or frontier model will be able to do. I think that's one big innovation that is happening in the field. The second one is the use of large language models. A lot of this research can orchestrate and drive a lot of this computational research. Instead of needing engineers or researchers to manually launch scripts or to tie together workflow one with workflow two, I think large language models will be able to automate and automatically tie a lot of that together.

Analyst: How do companies in the space typically approach commercialisation strategies and lead assets?

Expert: The commercialisation strategy of the lead shares. I think it's a combination of you either bringing the asset all the way to market yourself, so that's option one. Option two is that you sell your asset or you partner with a large pharma company and that can be after you have phase 1. It can be at any stage, but I think most deals in this space right now, especially if you have some type of clinical data, be it phase 1 or phase 2.

Do you see any downside risks that might materially impact market growth over the next three years?

Expert: Not that I'm aware of. There is a lot of discussion, right now, about AI models, because biotech is considered a dual-use field where a model's ability to design a drug that addresses a patient need can also be used to design a bioweapon. So, I think there will be some increasing scrutiny in that space and making sure that the models are safe, but I think that the industry and regulation will figure out how to allow good companies to continue doing that.

How fragmented do you consider the biotechnology and AI industry to be? Is the majority of market share captured by 2-5 players? Is it a monopoly? Is it fragmented or highly fragmented?

Expert: I would say it's still very fragmented. The biotech space, if you look, there are so many different diseases that affect humanity. There are a lot of both large pharma companies or small players and I think it will continue to be highly fragmented that way.

Analyst: How fragmented might the AI-enabled biotech industry be?

Expert: I would say it's still very fragmented because it's still so early in the space. You have major players like Isomorphic Labs, probably the most well-funded one of them all. Then, you have some players that are purely in the platform, like Chai or Boltz. I would say the whole space is very nascent and so I don't think there is a lot of market consolidation yet.

How protected is the biotechnology and AI market? What are the barriers to entry?

Expert: In the market, I would say, there are some barriers. The barrier to entry is that, for the AI-enabled space, you need access to compute. You need data that, hopefully, you have some data advantage and you need talent. So that's not insurmountable. There are certainly lots of new companies that are tackling this space. I would say the barrier to entry is still I would consider it low, even though, certainly, the more compute, data and better talent you have, the better and sometimes it takes a little bit of time and funding to acquire critical mass. Generally, there is a lot of money floating around the market right now, so I think that the barrier is low.

What are the barriers to scale for existing players in the market?

Expert: Barriers to scale are compute and data. Compute is extremely expensive, as we know from how much the frontier labs are spending. Data is extremely expensive as well because it costs hundreds of thousands of dollars to generate a single data point in biology. It needs to be done via a lab that has good protocols for data generation. So that is certainly a bottleneck.

How would you describe each player's USP based on breadth of offerings, tech enablement, geographic footprint, IP filings, patient profile partnerships, funding, etc?
