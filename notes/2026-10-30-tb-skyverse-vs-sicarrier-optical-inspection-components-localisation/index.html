00:46:55] What are the future prospects for X-ray detectors in semiconductor metrology?

Expert [00:47:06]: In fact, X-ray inspection technology still has an irreplaceable role to play in the semiconductor industry. Typical semiconductor inspection includes traditional optical inspection, namely the bright-field and dark-field methods just mentioned. It can only inspect the surface, checking for small defects, even down to tens of nanometers, and then checking the precision of the lithography exposure, i.e., whether critical dimensions are consistent. There are also electron-based methods, such as SEM and TEM. However, such methods are destructive to the chip under test. Generally, the chip has to be sliced or have its surface scanned by an electron beam, which causes damage to the metrology and inspection. There is also AFM, which can resolve fine details down to atomic-scale morphology, but it is slow and can cover only a very small field of view.

As for X-ray, first of all, it is a non-destructive testing technique. X-rays can penetrate chips to provide information on film thickness, chemical composition, and interface stress, and they offer relatively fast inspection speed – a unique feature. With the advancement of chip technology, people may feel that the earlier process-node race – from 28nm, 14nm, and 7nm down to 2nm and 3nm – is approaching physical limits. The main focus now may be, for example, Huawei’s proposed Tau Scaling Law, with 3D packaging as the next step. This could be the main direction of chip technology competition in the coming period, using advanced packaging to substantially improve the performance of overall chip systems.

This kind of 3D packaging actually imposes high requirements on the detection of internal defects in chips, particularly the tiny TSVs inside. This is exactly one of the best application scenarios for X-ray inspection: it can perform three-dimensional imaging and achieve spatial resolution below 100nm for very high-resolution inspection. Therefore, the more advanced the process, the more stringent and urgent the requirements for high-end X-ray inspection equipment.

Correspondingly, X-ray detectors need to be paired with these complete instruments, such as the XRD, XRF, XPS, and SAXS we previously developed, as well as micro-CT or nano-CT. They will basically have good market prospects. There is another technical difficulty. For TDI cameras, domestic products have already achieved a breakthrough. In the X-ray field, silicon drift detectors used in XRF can detect the elemental composition of thin films. They measure the fluorescence spectra emitted by each element to identify its composition. At present, this remains quite difficult. There are not yet mature suppliers in China, and the country basically relies on imports. Some domestic suppliers may buy foreign probes and then assemble them into complete detectors, but these are not 100% domestically manufactured.
