01 The reinstatement account

Trump told CNBC he felt strongly Huang should return and said a thorough administration investigation found Huang had done nothing wrong in the chip business. Asked what the attorney general told him, he said investigators felt Huang himself was exonerated, then added that he was not sure that was the right word. He said he had been in touch with the board both directly and through others, worked with JD Vance, Scott Bessent and Howard Lutnick, and was represented at the board, declining to say whether he called into its meeting. David Faber said people close to the board had insisted as recently as last week that Huang was not coming back and asked what changed; Trump said he convinced them, and that it was far easier once the stock had fallen than it would have been a week and a half earlier, when, as he put it, everybody was riding high. Asked whether Huang would have been reinstated without him, he said no. He defended the board against Jim Cramer’s criticism, saying it had been put in a bad position and acted responsibly, and said he spoke to Huang the night before and asked only that he work hard.

02 National security assurance, hedged

Trump said the board asked whether Huang posed a national-security problem and that the administration, after what he called a lot of research, answered no. He said the assurance covered everything the administration knows, and that the conclusion was both his own and that of his national-security team. Asked whether he assured the board Huang would not be prosecuted, he said he did not use that word but told them the administration believes Huang is fine, and that he gave them enough assurance. Asked whether the assurance was in writing, he pointed to the public statement he put out that morning. He declined to say what the CIA told him beyond saying he has clearance and would not have gone ahead otherwise, and would not discuss reporting that he was alerted to the matter last year. He argued the bigger security risk would be losing Huang, saying China could try to hire him, while adding there was never a suggestion of that. He said Xi Jinping, at a recent summit, called Huang a brilliant man but did not know him well. These are his characterizations; the interview does not establish the formal status of any investigation.

03 Advanced chips vs. commodity chips

Trump drew a line between older chips and advanced ones such as Blackwell. He said Huang was allowed to sell older chips, which he called “the 200” and described as semi-obsolete, but not Blackwell, which he said cannot be copied because of its complexity; he put the lead over China at anywhere from a few years to ten. He said that if a customer resold chips to China, that would be the customer’s problem, and that Huang did not know about it, at least as far as the administration can tell. Once a generation such as Blackwell is obsolete, he said, he would have no problem with it being sold more widely, since China will have its own version by then; he added that chips superior to Blackwell are coming. He said he understands Huang’s argument that if the US does not sell to China, China will make the chips itself. He did not discuss what NVIDIA is shipping to China today.

04 Market moves and the AI trade

Asked whether last week’s tech selloff affected his thinking, Trump said probably, but that the bigger factor was the long term. He claimed the market fell roughly $5 trillion after the election and is up roughly $25 trillion since he took office, and said the board’s decision got easier once NVIDIA had lost 50% of its value “or whatever the number was”; all of these are his figures. He said he is not worried about an AI bubble now that Huang is back, comparing the loss of a star executive to a baseball team losing one of its best players. Asked whether Huang is now too big to fail, he said no.

05 Tariffs, plants, and chip sovereignty

Trump argued tariffs are bringing chip manufacturing back to the US. He said Mr. Wei, who he said makes NVIDIA’s chips (TSMC’s C.C. Wei), is putting hundreds of billions of dollars into Arizona and, he believes, starting another plant in Texas to avoid tariffs, and he said chip tariffs will be 200% in two years. He extended the argument to autos, saying carmakers face tariffs of 25% to 40% a car in two years unless they build in the US, and citing a $4 billion Audi plant and a Ford plant in Detroit that he said is now running around the clock; he said the building would stop immediately if he removed tariffs. He blamed past presidents’ failure to use tariffs for the loss of US chip manufacturing to Taiwan. The plant figures and tariff rates are his.

06 Government’s role and limits

Asked by Cramer whether Washington would support NVIDIA if its financing arrangements ran into trouble, Trump said he did not think that would ever happen, that he does not believe the company has a financing problem, and compared its chip backlog favorably to Boeing’s. Asked whether he considered a government stake in NVIDIA, he said he would have loved one but did not want the government sued for insider trading. Pressed on where he draws the line on intervening in companies, and whether Microsoft, Alphabet, OpenAI or Anthropic could get similar treatment, he said there is no formula and described it as a matter of touch, citing his past protection of Elon Musk. Told that China hawks in his party and administration oppose Huang’s return, he said they will come around once he speaks to them. Reminded that he had earlier floated Tim Cook for the job, he said he still thinks Cook would be great but that no one can do what Huang does. He also said he sold 250 Boeing planes to China and thinks another 250 will follow, and that a trade deal to be announced will have China buying much more from US farmers; he gave no terms, and the figures are his alone.

01 Huang’s reinstatement and the rule-of-law framing

Gerstner recounts that he wrote to NVIDIA’s board urging it not to prejudge Huang before a hearing, having told CNBC last Thursday that a law violation was becoming increasingly unlikely. He credits the board for staying mentally flexible, gathering a fuller set of facts after an investigation he says has run for 14 months, and reversing course once the administration — per the president’s statements that morning, as Gerstner characterizes them — found no evidence of wrongdoing on national security grounds or at the Department of Justice; he cites no formal finding beyond the president’s remarks. Gerstner declines to detail any private conversations he had with administration officials beyond confirming he did not speak directly with the president.

02 Governance and board accountability

Asked whether the board should be replaced, Gerstner resists an immediate call for change but concedes the episode may prompt reassessment — potentially of certain board members or the addition of outside expertise in compliance and export controls. He defends Huang’s famously flat structure of 60 direct reports, noting nobody questioned it when NVIDIA was delivering an unprecedented annual cadence of new chip generations and full data-center buildouts that he says delivered tenfold returns for shareholders. He describes his direct conversation with Huang after reinstatement as personal and humbled, with Huang expressing gratitude rather than bravado and signaling commitment to employees, shareholders and the country. Gerstner adds that Huang will appear before the banking committee on a livestream, which he welcomes as a chance to rebuild confidence.

03 Market impact and AI capex sustainability

Wapner cites an Evercore note characterizing the episode as among the most consequential AI developments since ChatGPT’s launch, implying Washington now views the AI buildout as too strategically important to fail. Gerstner argues the reinstatement does not amount to an all-clear signal: he says NVIDIA’s valuation as of that morning implied negative growth for 2028 and 2029, which he calls unjustified, but says markets won’t believe otherwise until Q1/Q2 next year when AI-lab revenue growth either materializes or doesn’t. His point is that the roughly $1 trillion a year of AI infrastructure spending is paid for by the private sector rather than public money; he describes it as coming out of companies’ free cash flow and does not address debt financing, and he calls the buildout a core driver of US competitiveness.

04 Anthropic’s post-IPO trading and valuation

Gerstner attributes Anthropic’s weak post-IPO trading (down again the day of the interview, priced at $87 versus an $83 IPO price) to technical factors: 300 million employee shares were sellable on day one, and institutional holders — pension funds, endowments, hospitals exposed on a look-through basis — have been hedging roughly $1.5 trillion in newly created LP value. He expects four or five upcoming index inclusions to generate $30–50 billion of buy pressure, reversing the recent sell pressure, and says he expects the company to be worth $3 trillion by the end of next year at 10x forward revenue, a lower multiple, he says, than many of the market’s best companies.

05 Competitive landscape and broader capitalism argument

Gerstner says US frontier-AI labs’ unreleased internal capabilities — citing progress on problems like Navier-Stokes — have pulled further ahead of Chinese open-source models over the past six to nine months, even if public benchmarks don’t show it. He names Google, Elon Musk’s ventures, Meta, and open-source models as maintaining a competitive ecosystem alongside OpenAI and Anthropic. Closing on the administration’s hands-on role in corporate affairs, he argues little has structurally changed in government-business relations, but defends the episode as a demonstration of due process; he frames the US advantage as the combination of immigrant risk-takers like Huang, abundant venture capital, and reward for success, and points to a program creating 70 million free stock accounts for citizens under 18 as an effort to broaden ownership of the AI boom.

01 Founders as irreplaceable forces

The conversation was published a day before NVIDIA’s board reinstated Huang. After Senra reads Lütke’s own tweet back to him, Lütke argues that NVIDIA as a company effectively ends the moment Huang is no longer in charge, with any successor organization merely imitating the original rather than replacing it. He frames NVIDIA’s rise — turning silicon into thinking machines via CUDA and GPUs, sustained through a decade when the technology’s purpose was widely misunderstood — as the product of singular, irreplaceable vision rather than competitive pressure, citing Tensor Cores as an invention driven by foresight rather than market demand. He argues the bar for removing a builder of this magnitude should be far higher than scandal alone. Senra invokes Michael Moritz’s description of founders as forces of nature distinct from institutionally-bred CEOs, and Lütke calls Tim Cook the best-case managerial exception, extending the life of Steve Jobs-era Apple products without truly reinventing the company.

02 Huang as the industry’s statesman

Lütke credits Huang with reviewing large GPU orders himself and making sure startups and smaller customers could get supply even when he could have made more money elsewhere, calling him a statesman of the AI industry; he says this is what he has been told and that he has no inside information. He also says Huang championed open-source AI and that NVIDIA invested heavily in it. He says the speed of Huang’s exit looked too organized to him and asks who benefits from Huang being out, while saying he may be completely wrong and that more information will come.

03 Wealth creation vs. redistribution

Recounting a three-hour conversation with Jeff Bezos, Senra relays Bezos’s idea that the Forbes 400 is detrimental because it ranks ownership rather than value created for others — Amazon being worth $2 trillion while Bezos owns roughly $200 billion means he helped create $1.8 trillion for everyone else. Lütke extends this into a philosophy that creation must precede redistribution, warning that societies focused only on redistribution risk stagnation resembling the Middle Ages, when gaining resources required taking them from someone else. He calls markets the original form of superintelligence, crediting real-time price discovery with solving problems individuals can’t solve alone, while noting he isn’t a pure free-market absolutist and that monopolies must generally be checked — except in founder-driven missions like NVIDIA’s, where vision substituted for competition. The two also compare anonymous versus public philanthropy (Carnegie’s libraries, Michael Dell’s children’s savings accounts), with Lütke arguing public giving can be more effective because it signals and amplifies cause participation, and calling anonymous giving, counterintuitively, somewhat selfish.

04 Societal risk to founders

Lütke raises a concern beyond Huang: a poll he cites finding that a large share of people on one side of the political spectrum believe occasional violence against CEOs can be justified, which he attributes partly to narratives blaming problems on the wealthy. He warns that if founding a company increasingly carries physical safety risk, many people will choose not to pursue it, which he calls dangerous given that new business formation is currently at an all-time high — a trend he hopes won’t reverse.

05 The programmer job ending

Building on a talk by his friend DHH, the Ruby on Rails creator and a Shopify board member, who declared at a conference that the programming job as practiced since the early 2000s ended in 2026, Lütke says the terminal-typing coding role no longer exists in practice, replaced by a role centered on ambitious, wish-based creation. He says most people fail to extract value from AI due to insufficiently ambitious prompting, and describes having AI perform meta-analysis of a user’s own prompt history to surface where ambition or problem understanding was lacking. He affirms Senra’s comparison to DeepMind’s Go-playing system finding moves humans would never have thought of, saying programming is entering a similar phase, and frames AI as best understood as an instrument requiring mastery rather than a simple task-accelerator.

06 Rebuilding software through verification

Lütke says the industry historically built productivity through code abstractions, such as Ruby on Rails, because there were never enough engineers, but AI coding agents prefer explicit rules and large volumes of correct code over abstraction layers, reversing old incentives. He says asking AI to find implicit state machines in any codebase reveals how poorly humans actually wrote code, and that building formal models and running security tools exposes vulnerabilities, leading him to say the industry probably has to replace all human-written production code within the next six to twelve months, often by rebuilding it in safer languages like Rust. He says most companies are already in some form of code red on cybersecurity. He forecasts resulting software will be nearly bug-free, dramatically faster, and fully responsive to user intent — illustrated by Shopify rewriting its internal source-repository system, called Delta, in a single weekend to handle pull-request volume far higher than historical norms, a task he says previously would have been impractical given cost and staffing needs.

07 Shopify’s tools culture and entrepreneurship mission

Lütke says Shopify has always treated internal tool-building as high-status work, unlike most companies where it’s treated as a lesser assignment, leaving it well-positioned for the AI shift; nearly all engineering now runs through AI-agent “factories.” He describes the current moment as magical, comparing it to the Renaissance, and argues people haven’t priced in how much better software and quality of life will get as computers become fully responsive to user wishes. Closing on his broader mission, Lütke calls free markets, not capitalism per se, the mechanism for human thriving, criticizes patents as market-distorting monopolies, and — invoking Richard Branson’s framing that business is an idea that improves someone else’s life — urges using new AI tools to build products that make others’ lives better, noting approvingly that new business formation is currently at an all-time high.

01 The GTA VI launch and track record

Zelnick, speaking as Take-Two’s chairman and CEO, credits Rockstar Games with producing what he describes as the highest-scoring video game in history, achieving a 99 Metacritic score with 120 perfect reviews, a result he attributes to sustained creative effort rather than chance. He notes GTA V’s prior run was itself a surprise, selling over 235 million units across three console generations, with GTA Online still generating revenue thirteen years later alongside the new release. He does not give post-launch sales figures, but confirms preorders exceeded expectations and promises further disclosure soon.

02 Business model: recurrent spending and long-horizon bets

Zelnick traces recurrent consumer spending back to GTA IV’s first paid downloadable content, a Rockstar-Microsoft deal he describes as the origin of a now-dominant revenue stream making up about 80% of net bookings. He argues this model, now extended to NBA 2K’s year-round online engagement, is shifting emphasis away from big periodic releases toward ongoing engagement, comparing it to how people stay involved with World of Warcraft or Magic: The Gathering rather than binge-watching TV. He says Take-Two’s financial strength, built on this recurring base plus a 15-franchise catalog, lets the company pursue creative bets that competitors won’t attempt, under a stated approach of pairing creative ambition with financial caution. He describes a quarterly product-review process where business teams budget creative projects and then step back to let creators work.

03 Creative judgment, controversy, and ratings

Asked about handling controversial content, Zelnick says Take-Two aims to make art rather than just popular entertainment, leaning on two decades of shared intuition with Rockstar to judge creative edge cases, and says he personally encourages teams to push creative boundaries further rather than pull back. He ties this to rigorous ESRB compliance — he chairs the ESA on a rotating basis — and strict marketing age-gating, noting GTA VI is an M-rated title intended for ages 17 and up, and that he reminds friends with young children of that rating while leaving the choice to them as parents. He also credits Rockstar’s ability to evolve its trademark humor over time, contrasting it with Duke Nukem, a Take-Two-published title whose edgy humor had not aged well by its final release.

04 AI’s role versus creative fundamentals

On artificial intelligence, Zelnick says adoption is still early and that predictions of vast headcount reduction haven’t materialized at Take-Two; instead AI is making back-office work less tedious and more productive, citing faster pie-chart creation as a trivial example. He argues new technology historically raises productivity and GDP rather than cutting employment, pointing to stable US employment through the AI boom. He dismisses fears that AI disadvantages large publishers, noting no AI vendor offers exclusive access to one company over a competitor, and that making, marketing, and financing hits remains difficult regardless of tooling. He also rejects the idea that AI’s ability to replicate existing content is novel, comparing it to painters who have long reproduced Picasso without devaluing originals, and argues interactive entertainment has not reached the “creative asymptote” he believes film hit decades ago.

05 VR skepticism versus the mobile miss

Zelnick recounts being publicly skeptical of virtual reality as an entertainment medium, citing uncomfortable headsets, its solitary nature, and nausea within about twenty minutes of use, and says Take-Two invested almost nothing while competitors spent hundreds of millions, a stance he says proved correct. He contrasts this with an acknowledged failure on mobile, where he underestimated demand for short, light play sessions aimed at different demographics skewing older and more female, forcing Take-Two to catch up via acquisitions, culminating in the roughly $9.7 billion purchase of Zynga in 2022 after three years of relationship-building with its CEO, Frank Gibeau.

06 What comes next

Zelnick lists confirmed upcoming titles including WWE 2K, PGA Tour 2K, BioShock, Judas, and Topgolf (from mobile studio Nordeus), and says other titles are coming, while brushing off the hosts’ jokes about GTA VII. He points to international expansion in underserved markets like India, Africa, parts of Asia, Latin America and Russia, and predicts streaming will work well enough within two or three years, expanding console/PC total addressable market by removing hardware requirements. He says Take-Two will return to being a net cash company by the end of this fiscal year after taking on debt for the Zynga deal, reopening capacity for further M&A provided it fits strategically, financially, and culturally — the same three-part framework he says has made all of Take-Two’s prior acquisitions succeed.

THE SIGNAL

  • Rajendra says regulatory documentation (IND, CSR, BLA) is where life sciences has seen some of the fastest AI uptake, and this year’s models moved from speeding drafting to catching errors before FDA review.
  • Jiao says datasets lose value exponentially over time, and positive transfer from related data lets OpenAI eventually get the same capability elsewhere if one source is unavailable, a caution for startups selling data to labs (our read).
  • Boston Children’s: Jiao says o3 deep research worked through 300–400 unsolved genomic cases; in one, by her account, it dug into low-quality sequencing data and proposed a cause that follow-up sequencing confirmed.
  • In one collaboration, 2,000 agents searched 2,000 perturbations across 8M mouse-brain cells and found a cell-type-specific split between two genes in one protein complex, relevant to tuberous sclerosis, which months of manual analysis had missed.

01 Strategy and the Orchestra Bio deal

Jiao frames OpenAI Life Sciences around three pillars: training the best model (GPT Rosalind), building the best product/harness to make that capability usable, and partnering with academics, biotechs, and pharma to demonstrate real impact. She says a recurring problem is a “capability overhang” — models can do things scientists don’t know how to prompt for — which motivated acquiring Rajendra’s company, Orchestra Bio, to build a codex-like harness for scientists rather than coders. Rajendra frames his personal motivation around democratizing the expertise of elite clinicians, asserting that models are already superhuman at understanding clinical information.

02 Discovery: agents against the attention bottleneck

Jiao argues biology’s bottleneck has shifted from data scarcity to insight extraction — unlike math, where models solve stated problems, biology has vast accumulated sequencing and assay data that no human can fully search. She cites an early collaborator’s mouse-brain perturbation dataset as evidence that thousands of parallel agents can pursue every hypothesis simultaneously and communicate with one another, surfacing patterns invisible to manual review. On molecular design, Jiao says models are beginning to show glimmers of superhuman ability — comparable to the rare humans who can mentally visualize a protein’s 3D structure from its sequence — and expects AI-driven molecule design to change a lot within three to six months. Rajendra adds that even without full 10,000-agent deployments, scientists already use Codex-style sub-agents to re-explore old PhD datasets at a scale previously impossible.

03 Development: trials, regulatory filings, manufacturing

On clinical trials, Rajendra breaks the problem into three areas: designing better trials, enrolling the right patients, and reducing operational burden across fragmented systems (EDC, CTMS, EHR, TMF). He says AI can’t shorten biology’s inherent trial timelines but can prevent trials from veering off track, noting many trials miss deadlines due to enrollment and site issues. On regulatory submissions, Rajendra describes the process as fundamentally about managing an unmanageable corpus of institutional knowledge, calling it well-suited to large language models; he says the FDA has signaled openness to AI use and to more continuous submission of trial data, a long way from the truckloads of paper filings Conde recalls. On manufacturing (CMC), he calls it an underappreciated area ripe for agents — citing an example where agents trace a refrigerator temperature deviation to determine whether a batch must be rerun, given meticulous paper-based batch records.

04 Distribution and patient access

Rajendra notes OpenAI’s consumer base includes 1.2 billion ChatGPT users, of whom 330 million ask health questions — creating an opportunity to connect consumers and clinicians to clinical trial eligibility and care navigation. He describes insurance systems using blunt cost-containment instruments that are hard for ordinary patients to navigate, and says OpenAI is starting to work with clinicians and payers to apply more precision to authorization and reimbursement processes.

05 Data bottlenecks and safety approach

Jiao says biological training data is inherently noisier than code or chat text due to lab-to-lab variability and measurement bias, and expresses a wish for cleaner datasets like genomic sequences and structural biology data; she expects overall data demand to grow, possibly linearly, even as individual datasets experience exponential decay in value, since biology — unlike code — can’t be fully simulated synthetically. Asked for advice to founders building “data factory” startups, Jiao says OpenAI favors high-quality, low-noise raw experimental data and well-structured human-expert rubric data, and notes models can increasingly evaluate data quality themselves. On partnering, Rajendra tells outside founders that OpenAI wants to be the “front door” through plug-ins and MCP connectors, and urges them to build on it. On safety, Jiao says OpenAI cannot reliably infer intent from prompts alone, so it relies on what she calls trusted access: it verifies that a user works at a legitimate institution with a legitimate use and then, by her account, opens up the model’s full capabilities instead of guessing at intent and blocking specific requests; she adds that those users are also monitored by their own employers and labs. Rajendra discusses traceability and “chain of evidence” as the mechanism for building human trust in AI-derived scientific conclusions.

06 Outlook: one-person biotech

Jiao speculates that math-like “solved” breakthroughs will first emerge at atomic and chemical levels before extending to cellular and organism-level biology, on a roughly two-year horizon, though she stresses biology’s underlying reaction rates can’t be changed. Rajendra states his belief that a single person could soon start a biotech and run multiple drug programs, while cautioning that regulation and biological pace remain hard limits on how far acceleration can go.

THE SIGNAL

  • Cofsky’s Microsoft model: ROIC was 40%+ before FY23 and stays mid-to-high teens even with capex near operating cash flow and FCF positive only near decade-end, still above cost of capital but raising a utility-multiple debate.
  • He says 2026 (~$800B across hyperscalers, public neoclouds, Tesla/SpaceX) is the first year aggregate AI capex outruns operating cash flow and needs debt and equity (capex was about half of OCF in 2024-25).
  • Memory: Cofsky says a maker could lock up roughly two-thirds of capacity through decade-end in supply deals with price floors and ceilings near current levels; he sees 2027 tighter, with new supply only in 2028-29.
  • Snowflake angle: agents built on data pulled from systems of record could move app spend to data platforms; several companies also used up annual token budgets within months, pushing adoption of open-weight models and routers.

01 Resilience and optionality framework

Cofsky describes Janus Henderson’s Global Technology and Innovation Fund as built on a two-part philosophy: roughly two-thirds in “resilient” businesses with narrow outcome ranges (he names TSMC and ASML) and one-third in “optionality” positions resembling VC-style public-market bets, where losses are capped but upside is uncapped. He attributes this structure to the empirical reality that a small percentage of companies drive most equity-market returns, consistent with power-law dynamics in tech. The fund’s average holding period is two to four years, longer in small/mid-cap strategies, driven by this structure rather than targeted as a goal — optionality positions turn over more as theses are proven right (and trimmed) or wrong (and exited).

02 Software’s disruption math

Cofsky’s team studied three historical disruption waves — brick-and-mortar to e-commerce, on-premise to SaaS/cloud, and legacy media to streaming — tracking revenue, earnings, and multiples over the following decade. The pattern repeated: about three-quarters of incumbents became losers, one-quarter winners, with little divergence in the first few years but a sharp fundamental and multiple split emerging around year five, after which winners’ multiples recovered to or above pre-disruption levels while losers became value traps. Applying this to AI, he expects a similarly narrow cohort of software winners to emerge over years, with high volatility along the way.

He says the fund likes the infrastructure layer — Snowflake and Datadog — plus hyperscalers Microsoft and Amazon and, in vertical software, Guidewire; he says sticky systems of record such as ServiceNow, Workday, SAP, and Salesforce have the data and context to be winners. He flags Adobe, which he says the fund held through about 2021, as a company in a tough spot: its historically dominant, high-margin creative subscription franchise is vulnerable because generative AI’s probabilistic nature changes the interface for creativity, eroding new-user acquisition and pricing power while introducing real marginal costs for free/AI tier users. He also warns generally against companies carrying heavy debt loads, arguing fewer “degrees of freedom” hurts resilience amid disruption.

03 Hyperscalers, NeoClouds, and model economics

Cofsky favors hyperscalers (Microsoft, Amazon) over neoclouds, arguing the former are investing aggressively on their “front foot” and offer full-stack, chip-agnostic services to enterprises and sovereigns, while neoclouds remain tethered to NVIDIA’s ecosystem, dependent on debt financing, and focused on frontier labs’ overflow capacity and AI-first companies. He notes neoclouds often sign long-term contracts to match assets/liabilities, but current spot rates for GPU compute have risen above those contracted rates — meaning re-pricing today would yield higher revenue per gigawatt than existing deals.

On the open-weight versus closed-weight model debate, Cofsky describes harness layers (e.g., Claude’s “code” harness) and model routers that direct tasks to the cheapest adequate model, noting that even if frontier models are “160 IQ” and open-weight models “120 IQ,” the latter suffice for many tasks — driving growing token/dollar share toward open models even as total spend explodes. He cites one vibe-coding company where 85% of users default to automatic model selection rather than choosing manually, prioritizing outcome over specific model.

04 Memory, optical, and the capex cycle

Among optionality picks, Cofsky highlights Lumentum (optical content could rise ~10x per rack as GPU interconnects shift from copper to optics in “scale-up optical”) and Seagate (its HAMR technology and a hard-drive duopoly structure support rising pricing/margins for data storage needed by AI models). On memory, Janus owns Micron, betting on a capital-return story and multi-year supply tightness, even as the market prices it at historically low multiples; Cofsky flags the risk that long-term supply agreements (LTSAs) meant to smooth cyclicality are only as reliable as their enforceability, drawing a parallel to Microchip’s prior LTSA strategy that led to a deeper-than-usual downcycle. Janus’s preferred memory-adjacent exposure is actually semi-cap equipment names like Lam Research, ASML, and Applied Materials, which he views as structural winners regardless of cycle timing.

On capex scale, Cofsky compares the AI buildout (~$800B in 2026, projected over $1T in 2027) to historical cycles like railroads, the interstate highway system, and the dot-com buildout, arguing 2-3% of GDP devoted to infrastructure for several years isn’t historically unprecedented. He frames the question as how much AI revenue or benefit is needed by 2030 across labs, AI-first companies, enterprises and sovereigns to earn a return on that spending. He says the AI revolution is in its first inning and that, with capacity tight for the next couple of years, the capital cycle is not at its end.

05 Apple’s underweight position

Apple is a large holding for the fund (top five, Garber notes) but underweight relative to its index because of valuation and growth versus names Cofsky likes more. Cofsky frames Apple’s AI strategy as betting on a multi-model future: rather than building its own frontier model (where it has had “fits and starts”), Apple aims to integrate third-party open- and closed-weight models into its device ecosystem, leveraging its control over distribution and on-device compute. He names Meta’s AI/AR glasses as the key competitive risk — a new form factor potentially displacing the iPhone as the primary AI interface.
