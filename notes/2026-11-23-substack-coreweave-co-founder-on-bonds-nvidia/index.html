On October 13, Chamath Palihapitiya posted a chart with nothing but 👀 on it. CoreWeave’s 9.625% bonds due 2032 had just touched a 15% yield. A couple of hours later, Elon Musk replied with one word: “Yikes.”

Chamath Palihapitiya’s post and Elon Musk’s reply on X, October 13, 2026

The next day, CoreWeave fell 15.5%. Oracle dropped about 9%, Nebius fell 10%, and Chamath was back with another chart: “Neoclouds fell 12% today, the day after CoreWeave’s bond selloff.”

Source: Chamath Palihapitiya on X, October 14, 2026

That was the moment CoreWeave stopped being just another AI stock and became the poster boy for a historic tech selloff. When people want to argue that the AI buildout runs on borrowed money they post CoreWeave’s bonds. When they want to argue the demand is real they post CoreWeave’s backlog. Every big move in the stock now gets read as a verdict on the whole AI trade.

The bear case only got louder from there. D.A. Davidson’s Gil Luria said the equity could go to zero if the AI cycle sputters. By November 9, some of CoreWeave’s 2031 bonds were yielding more than 16% and its five-year credit default swaps were trading above 1,240 basis points.

Management hasn’t stayed quiet. In an October memo, CEO Michael Intrator said the market “misunderstands and acts irrationally” and that “we are confident in our backlog which more than supports all of our debt obligations.” On the Q3 call he said CoreWeave “will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields, nor do we need to.” And late on November 18, co-founder Brian Venturo disclosed that he’d bought $30M of the company’s 9.75% bonds due 2031 and plans to hold them to maturity: “I’ve never been more confident in CoreWeave.”

Michael Intrator on CNBC’s Squawk on the Street at the New York Stock Exchange, February 27, 2026. Source: CNBC / Getty Images

On Friday, CoreWeave closed at $39.51.

It went public at $40 in March 2025. Since that listing the backlog has grown from $15.1B to $132.5B, quarterly revenue has gone from $747M to $3.66B, and power connected has climbed from 360 MW to 1.7 GW.

Twenty months of growth and the share price is right back where it started.

The stock is down 71% from its May high and 26% last week alone. This came during Nvidia’s first earnings call without Jensen Huang, a Reuters report that the SEC is examining Nvidia’s accounting, and the Nasdaq turning negative for the year.

That’s why there’s no one more interesting to hear from right now than the people running the company. On November 18, the morning after that Nvidia call, we got on the phone with Brannin McBee, CoreWeave’s co-founder and Chief Development Officer, and Nick Robbins, its Vice President of Corporate Development and Investor Relations, to hear how they’re thinking about it. We asked about Nvidia’s backstops, the bonds everyone on X is posting about, token prices, Chinese models, and Elon Musk.

The call was on background and it was off the record, so we’re summarizing the conversation in our own words. Anything in quotation marks in this report comes from a public earnings call, filing, or post.

By the end of this report you’ll know how CoreWeave actually borrows money, what Nvidia’s new tone means for it, and which of management’s claims held up when we checked them against the filings.

1] Does CoreWeave Need Nvidia’s Money?

The fear going into Nvidia’s call was simple. Nvidia has been using its balance sheet to support the companies that buy its chips and investors assumed the biggest neocloud was the biggest beneficiary.

Management told us that’s a misconception. They said Nvidia hasn’t guaranteed any of CoreWeave’s data center leases or any of its debt and that the business doesn’t lean on direct financial support from Nvidia today.

The one arrangement that looked like a backstop was a commercial contract. In September 2025 CoreWeave disclosed an order under which Nvidia would buy any capacity CoreWeave couldn’t sell through April 2032, initially valued at $6.3B. Management told us the capacity tied to that deal was reallocated to other customers in the first quarter of this year.

They also said nothing has changed on chip allocation. CoreWeave described itself as one of Nvidia’s five or six largest customers globally and pointed out that it’s been first to market with each new Nvidia system.

Here’s what the record shows.

On the November 17 call, interim CEO Colette Kress said neoclouds remain an important part of the ecosystem. Then she added a qualifier.

“...we will continue to be disciplined and selective in our support.”

Colette Kress, Interim CEO, Nvidia, Q3 earnings call, November 17, 2026

Nvidia’s commitments tied to neocloud agreements fell to $32B from $36B a quarter earlier. Its written commentary added new language saying those commitments only take effect once a neocloud has secured its own financing and the capacity is deployed and accepted. When Bernstein’s Stacy Rasgon asked whether that was a change, Kress said the terms were the same as before.

Source: Nvidia Q3 FY2027 CFO Commentary

Most of Nvidia’s $118B of guarantees have nothing to do with CoreWeave. $105B is credit support for a single campus which reports tie to an OpenAI site in Ohio. Guarantees labeled for neoclouds total $3.5B.

Management read all of this as good news. Their interpretation is that Nvidia will keep using its balance sheet but will concentrate it on operators that have already shown they can secure power, raise financing, sign large customers, and run the hardware. They put CoreWeave alone in that group with Nebius the closest.

Two things complicate the clean version of that story.

First, we couldn’t confirm the reallocation of the $6.3B order. CoreWeave’s latest 10-Q mentions Nvidia only in connection with its January purchase of $2B of CoreWeave stock at $87.20 a share.

Second, Washington is paying attention. On November 19 four senators introduced the AUDIT AI Act, which would force more disclosure of AI financing arrangements. Senator Mark Warner specifically raised Nvidia financing companies that buy its products.

The market didn’t share management’s read either. CoreWeave rose about 9% after hours on Kress’s pricing comments, closed down 4% the next day, and was down 21% by Friday.

2] Is Anyone Leaving Nvidia?

CoreWeave is an Nvidia shop. If customers move to Google’s TPUs, Amazon’s Trainium, or AMD, that’s a problem. We’d heard estimates from industry analysts that as much as 70% of capacity by 2028 could be running on something other than Nvidia, so we asked.

Management said they aren’t seeing that shift in their own customer base. The exceptions are Google and Anthropic, which they said clearly intend to stand up a very large amount of TPU capacity over the next couple of years. Outside of those two they told us customers show almost no interest in other chips. Even Google and Anthropic would take every GPU CoreWeave could give them over the next 12 to 18 months.

The near term data backs that up. Kress said Nvidia compute is fully utilized across every hyperscaler and neocloud it serves and that older Hopper GPUs are being recontracted at up to twice their original price.

An Nvidia GB300 NVL72 deployment. Source: CoreWeave / Switch

Further out the picture is less comfortable. On November 13, SemiAnalysis cut its forecast for 2028 AI accelerator spending by 28%, to $758B from $1.054T and its 2030 number by 40%. Summaries of the update say Nvidia absorbs most of the reduction while Amazon’s Trainium forecasts were left unchanged.

So what management sees in its order book and what forecasters see in 2028 don’t line up yet. Both can be true. GPUs are sold out today and the share of new capacity going to custom chips can still rise from here.

3] The Bonds Everyone Is Watching

If you’ve been on X this fall you’ve seen the chart. CoreWeave’s 9.75% unsecured notes due 2031 trade around 81 cents on the dollar, which works out to a yield near 16%. From there it’s a short hop to posts claiming CoreWeave pays 15% on all of its debt.

Management told us this is what the market gets most wrong about the company.

The bonds are a hedging tool. CoreWeave’s unsecured notes are the only liquid, below investment grade security tied to this trade. A fund that’s long the stock, long the converts, or long another neocloud’s converts can short CoreWeave’s bonds as protection. Shorting the stock itself is too dangerous because these names can jump 35% in a day.

The market for that hedge is thin. They pointed to roughly $10B of bonds outstanding against a credit default swap market they sized in the hundreds of millions of dollars.

CoreWeave’s 5-year credit default swaps in mid-October. Source: CNBC

The bonds aren’t how CoreWeave funds itself. The main source is debt raised against specific GPUs and customer contracts, which they said trades at par and prices in the mid to high single digits. Anything beyond that is a choice between taking customer prepayments and issuing unsecured debt and right now the former is cheaper.

The filings mostly agree.

At September 30 CoreWeave had about $47.8B of debt before leases. Senior unsecured notes were $10.0B of it, or 21%. Asset-level term loans were the biggest piece at $17.6B. Another $10.8B sits in convertible notes with effective rates of 2% to 3%.

Source: CoreWeave Q3 2026 10-Q

On the Q3 call, CEO Mike Intrator put the weighted average cost of debt across the whole business at 7.9%, down 250 basis points from a year earlier. Only one facility in the 10-Q carries an effective rate near 15%. It’s CoreWeave’s oldest term loan and has $1.1B outstanding.

A bond’s yield is what a new buyer earns at today’s price. CoreWeave still pays the 9.75% coupon it agreed to. The yield only becomes CoreWeave’s problem when it has to borrow again in that market and Intrator said on the call that CoreWeave “will not use the unsecured debt market” at current yields.

Instead it’s raising an investment grade loan of at least $4B backed by chips and a Meta contract, which Bloomberg reports should price around 7% to 7.5%. Co-founder Brian Venturo also disclosed that he personally bought $30M face value of the 2031 notes.

Brian Venturo, co-founder and chief strategy officer. Source: CoreWeave

None of that makes the debt small.

Interest expense was $846M in the third quarter, four times GAAP operating profit of $207M. Add $19.3B of lease liabilities and total obligations come to $66.2B. About $10.6B of debt is due within a year against $9.8B of total liquidity, though much of that is term loans that pay down from contracted customer cash flows. The unsecured notes also have to be refinanced when they mature between 2030 and 2032, so a market that stays closed for years would matter.

Management’s point that yield and cost of capital are different things is correct. The debt load is still the main risk in this stock.

4] Prepayments and Pricing Power

We asked what customers get in return for paying upfront.

Management said standard terms through Q3 have been 25% upfront at full rate card pricing, with no discount attached. Eighteen months ago a 25% prepayment would’ve cost CoreWeave something on price. Today demand is strong enough that it doesn’t. Pricing only gives when a customer prepays far more, in the range of 50% to 75% and even then they said the implied cost is well inside where the unsecured bonds trade.

Public numbers fit. On the Q3 call CoreWeave said it collected prepayments on 70% of its deals this year, with some customers funding 100% of contract value upfront. It expects about $9B of prepayments and deposits over the following three months, more than $3B of it tied to infrastructure that’s already built.

Prices went up at the same time. CoreWeave raised list prices about 25% in July and another 10% later in the quarter. New four and five year contracts are pricing at $20M to $25M per megawatt a year and short term capacity is going for more than $40M.

Rivals report something similar. Nebius said its Q3 deals closed above $25M per megawatt and Nscale’s S-1 showed customers prepaying about 23% of contract value on average.

One detail from the 10-Q is worth knowing. Deferred revenue stood at $10.0B at the end of September and the filing shows $1.9B moved into customer liabilities during the quarter because the underlying contracts became cancellable. Prepaid cash isn’t always locked in.

Source: CoreWeave Q3 2026 10-Q, page 19

5] Execution Is the Product

This was the point management pushed hardest.

Their view is that the market has given too much credit to signed contracts and announced sites and too little to capacity that’s switched on and billing. They said private valuations have run away, that a backlog alone shouldn’t earn a premium, and that a company with land, power agreements, and signed customers but no delivered GPUs is hard to value richly. They described the last couple of years as a grace period in which everyone got the benefit of the doubt and said that period is ending.

They ranked the field for us. CoreWeave is first. Nebius sits 12 to 18 months behind. Everyone else is further back. By their count only six or seven companies in the world have run this kind of infrastructure at scale and CoreWeave is the only one of them built around AI.

We asked whether smaller neoclouds failing would be an opportunity. They said yes, with the caveat on valuations above.

The numbers support the ranking. CoreWeave ended September with more than 1.7 GW of active power, double where it started the year, against 4.6 GW contracted. It’s guiding to more than 1.85 GW by year end.

Source: CoreWeave Q3 2026 earnings presentation

Nebius just narrowed its 2026 revenue guidance after power delays at its Vineland, New Jersey site pushed capacity into

  1. BofA cut its estimate

of Nebius’s year end active power to 560 MW from 702 MW. CoreWeave passed that level in the third quarter of 2025, which puts Nebius roughly 15 months behind and inside the range management gave us.

Nscale, which filed for an IPO in September with $103B of contracts, had 55 MW live at the time.

Nvidia seems to be thinking the same way. Kress told analysts that allocation now depends on whether a customer has its capital in place and can put systems to work as soon as they ship.

Source: CoreWeave Q3 2026 earnings presentation

CoreWeave isn’t immune though. A year ago this month it cut its own 2025 guidance because a third party data center developer was running late and the stock fell 16% in a day.

6] Cheaper Tokens, Pricier GPUs

Kress said on the Nvidia call that token prices have fallen nearly 60% since May while GPU rental prices keep rising. We asked who absorbs that squeeze and how CoreWeave makes money selling tokens.

Management’s first answer was volume. Cheaper tokens mean far more of them get used and they said they’re watching total consumption grow in real time.

The second answer is more specific to CoreWeave. Its cash flow comes from multi-year take-or-pay contracts, which is what lets it keep building. Much of the capacity it sells by the token is hardware that a first contract already paid for. At that point the only question is how to earn the most from a paid off asset, whether that’s on demand, managed inference, or another two year contract. They told us the model was built to handle the pricing volatility they expect over the coming quarters and years.

The renewal math is public. CoreWeave said pricing on older Ampere and Hopper GPUs rose 30% to 50% in Q3, above the original contract rates. Renewals at current prices would add close to $4B of annualized revenue in 2027 and nearly $10B including 2028, with no new capex.

Token sales themselves are still small. Managed inference has passed $250M of booked annual recurring revenue, against a business running at about $14.6B a year.

There’s also a less flattering explanation for rising rents. Lenders have reportedly cut loan-to-value ratios for neoclouds from 80% to between 50% and 60%, which chokes off new supply and pushes buyers into the spot market. On that reading, tight credit explains part of the price increase.

7] Chinese Models and the Inference Middlemen

Every model on CoreWeave’s inference platform is open weight and many of the popular ones are Chinese. With Washington in the mood it’s in, we asked if that’s a risk.

Source: CoreWeave Q3 2026 earnings presentation

Management doesn’t think so. They host whatever customers ask for and customers currently treat models like Kimi, GLM, and DeepSeek as the standard for open weights. If those were restricted they expect demand to move to American alternatives. They named Nvidia’s Nemotron, where they said CoreWeave is one of the largest hosts and Reflection AI’s model, which they said CoreWeave was first to stand up.

CoreWeave’s catalog lists both alongside the Chinese models and Reflection’s Beam 1.0 appeared there in October. We couldn’t verify the claim to being first. For context, Artificial Analysis’s index in June had GLM 5.2 as the top open model with Nemotron 3 Ultra second.

Management was blunter about pure inference providers like Fireworks, Baseten, and Modal. As they see it, those companies rent capacity from lower tier clouds and resell it in smaller, shorter slices. A provider that depends entirely on someone else’s compute has no obvious moat once the clouds decide to sell to the end customer directly. They said CoreWeave made that same choice when it slowed sales to Microsoft in favor of Microsoft’s own customers.

They also pointed to recent security problems at Fireworks. That part is on the record. On November 9 Fireworks disclosed that attackers reached its development machines through a breached third party cloud provider, exposing customer billing data, datasets, and some Notion AI chat transcripts.

8] What CoreWeave Looks Like in 10 Years

If compute ends up a commodity like electricity, is CoreWeave a utility with utility margins?

Management’s answer was that it becomes a cloud with cloud margins. Their thesis is that cloud works as an oligopoly once supply and demand balance out and that the survivors are the ones with scale, a diversified customer base, and sticky software on top. Moving data and workloads is painful, so customers stay.

They also made an argument about hardware. When the market reaches equilibrium the pace of new chips slows, which stretches the useful life of what’s already installed. A fleet that’s fully depreciated and still rented out earns very high margins.

On diversification they said Microsoft was 85% of revenue backlog at the IPO and is under 10% today and that 10% of the Fortune 500 are now customers.

The Fortune 500 figure is public from the Q3 call. Backlog by customer isn’t disclosed, so we can’t confirm the Microsoft numbers. Revenue concentration is in the 10-Q. The largest customer a year ago went from 67% of quarterly revenue to 24%. A second customer is now 30%, so two customers still make up more than half of sales.

As for cloud margins, there’s a long way to go. AWS earned a 38.7% operating margin last quarter and Google Cloud 36.2%. CoreWeave’s adjusted operating margin was about 10% and its GAAP figure was under 6%.

Source: CoreWeave Q3 2026 earnings presentation

9] The Musk Problem

On October 25 Elon Musk replied to a Chamath Palihapitiya post that called neoclouds badly organized and short on capital. Musk wrote that debt markets had nearly shut for companies that depend on AI data centers and that the more than 200 neoclouds were headed for one outcome.

“...guppy-level death and consolidation.”

Elon Musk, X, October 25, 2026

Elon Musk. Source: Getty Images, via CNBC (October 26, 2026)

CoreWeave had already dropped 15% on October 14, the day after its bond yields spiked. By early November it was below $50.

We asked management why they stayed quiet. The answer was the calendar. CoreWeave observes a quiet period of about 45 days before each earnings report and the whole episode fell inside it. They told us it was deeply frustrating to watch and that they intend to respond to this kind of thing more directly from here.

On substance they agree with part of what Musk said. They don’t think 200 neoclouds is sustainable either. What they dispute is the idea that CoreWeave belongs in that bucket and they believe the debt commentary confused a bond’s yield with a company’s cost of capital.

Some public facts are relevant here. SpaceX competes directly for chips and Musk says it’s raised $40B of debt to buy GPUs. The Wall Street Journal reported that Jensen Huang told cloud executives Nvidia could simply hand capacity to Musk. Musk for his part has said SpaceX isn’t buying any neoclouds.

Readers can weigh that for themselves. A competitor’s criticism can be self-interested and accurate at once.

10] Late Cycle or Air Pocket?

We put the bear case to them directly. A record IPO from Anthropic and deals like Marvell buying Lumentum are the kind of thing that tends to show up late in a cycle. What happens if capital markets close for a year?

Management doesn’t think this is late cycle. They called it an air pocket, with Anthropic’s IPO soaking up capital and Huang’s resignation unsettling everyone at once. Then they listed the last six months from their side of the table. A blow-up at a large AI fund, Meta’s reversal on selling compute, a war, inflation coming back, the midterms, and finally Huang.

Their answer on survival was the contract structure. What stays financeable in almost any market, they said, is a long dated take-or-pay contract with a creditworthy customer and that’s what CoreWeave’s backlog is made of. They expect that to keep the asset-level loan market open to them even through a dislocation.

They went further and argued that bringing new capacity online is about to get harder across the industry because of technology, capital, and regulation. If less gets built than people expect, capacity that’s already in the ground becomes more valuable.

The evidence here is thinner because it’s about the future. What we can say is that 36% of CoreWeave’s $132.3B of remaining performance obligations is due within 24 months and that more than 75% of committed 2027 capex not yet financed is tied to investment grade and enterprise customers. The planned $4B loan will be an early test of whether lenders still see it management’s way.

Source: CoreWeave Q3 2026 earnings presentation

The days after our call weren’t kind to the air pocket theory. Anthropic listed on Thursday and gave back most of its first day gain on Friday. Nvidia fell 14% on the Reuters report. CoreWeave lost 12%.

Management also told us a detailed supplement on returns and financing was on its way to the investor relations site. We’ll go through it in a follow-up.

11] The Scorecard

We went into this call with a list of things the market is worried about. Here’s how management’s answers held up.

The pattern is consistent. Where management talked about how the business runs today the filings back them up. The bonds are a minority of the debt, the average cost of that debt is under 8%, prices are rising, and the lead over rivals is real.

Where the claims reach into the future or into things only they can see we’re left taking their word. That covers the Nvidia backstop, the backlog mix, and whether customers stay on Nvidia hardware through 2028.

12] What We’re Watching

CoreWeave now has a market value of about $22B against a $132.5B backlog and $66.2B of debt and leases. The equity is a thin slice on top of a very large, very leveraged machine. That’s why the stock can move 12% on a headline about someone else’s accounting.

Source: CoreWeave Q3 2026 10-Q, page 7
