The ANT IPO today created one of the craziest days in the market I have seen in a long time.

I am personally not that interested in investing in ANT.

What I am really interested in is how certain pairs move now that we have a frontier model on the public market.

Because I think that this is now going to give us a lot of direct insight into how the market is willing to value different segments of the AI trade.

And it also shows us another data point on how disruptive the market believes AI will be.

The next few days are going to be very revealing.

Below lets look at the pairs that most interest me and what signals they could bring.

Now let me get into some more details. All of this is just my opinion of course. There are many factors at play with price movement.

ANT ↑ / Traditional SaaS ↓

The concern here is obviously that software itself becomes easier to build. This is not a novel thought. This is what led to the software beat down earlier this year. AI is already being used to write code, build interfaces, and help develop applications. If that keeps getting easier and cheaper, more competitors can enter, features become easier to recreate, and businesses can build more of their own simpler tools.

This really gives customers more options. They might question a separate subscription when a similar feature comes bundled into something they already use. They could also need fewer paid users as more work gets automated.

So this move could reflect people questioning how much pricing power existing software companies can hold onto.

ANT ↑ / Traditional SaaS ↑

Here, people might think existing software companies can use the same technology to strengthen their own businesses. They already have customer relationships, useful data, and products built into how businesses operate. Adding AI could make those products more valuable and give customers more reasons to keep using them.

There’s also the cost side where those companies could use AI to build features faster, handle support more efficiently, and get more done with their existing teams.

Businesses still care about reliability, security, and whether a product works with everything else they use. People would be betting that those advantages hold up, and established software companies get a meaningful share of the AI opportunity.

ANT ↑ / IT services ↓

The pressure here is on the amount of labor needed to complete a project. If a smaller team can build an application, handle support, or process documents more quickly, customers may start asking for lower prices. A business built around billing for people’s time could have a harder time growing that revenue.

The services firms could become more efficient themselves, so there’s a potential benefit on their side too. The question is how much of that benefit they get to keep once customers start negotiating.

There could still be plenty of work helping businesses implement AI and connect their systems.

This move would suggest people are more concerned about lower prices and fewer billable hours than excited about those new projects.

ANT ↑ / Hyperscalers ↑ / Neoclouds ↓

People still like demand for cloud computing, but they’re getting picky about who’s in the best position to fund the growth.

The appeal of the bigger platforms would be their broader customer relationships, multiple sources of income, and more flexibility when spending plans change.

Specialist clouds have more riding on keeping expensive capacity busy and getting the financing right. A delayed deployment, a slower customer ramp, or weaker rental pricing can put more pressure on that business.

Both groups have to spend heavily of course. This would be people paying up for the businesses they think have more room to handle the bumps along the way.

ANT ↑ / Semis ↑ / Networking ↑

This would be people buying into the broader AI growth story. The thinking is that better, cheaper AI gets used more often, by more businesses, and for bigger jobs. That creates more demand for the chips, memory, and connections underneath it.

Efficiency improvements can still happen along the way and a task might require less computing power, while the total number of tasks grows much faster.

In this scenario, people would be getting more comfortable that there’s enough demand coming through to support continued hardware spending.

Confidence in the products people use would be carrying through to confidence in the suppliers helping deliver them.

ANT ↑ / Semis ↓ / Networking ↓

People still like AI, but they’re shifting where they want exposure. They could see more upside in the companies selling models while becoming more cautious about the returns available to hardware suppliers.

There are a few ways to get there.

Better utilization could let AI revenue grow faster than new hardware purchases.

More competition could pressure equipment pricing.

Or the infrastructure stocks could already have so much growth priced in that people see better opportunities elsewhere.

Around the IPO, some of this could also be people selling existing AI positions to fund a new one. The common thread would be enthusiasm for AI alongside more selectivity about how to own that growth.

ANT ↑ / Power and cooling ↑

Here, people mihgt think the physical buildout still has room to grow. More AI usage would continue creating demand for actual facilities, electrical equipment, and cooling.

The expectation would be that more of the planned spending turns into projects getting built and equipment getting installed.

Better efficiency can reduce the resources needed for an individual task, while greater usage and more demanding tasks push total requirements higher.

Higher equipment density can also create more work for cooling and electrical systems.

People would be betting that those growing requirements keep the physical expansion moving, even as the technology becomes more efficient.

Let’s see! Definitely something to keep an eye on and watch any rotation that might come into play.
