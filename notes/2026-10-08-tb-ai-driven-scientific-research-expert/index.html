00:07:50] Was the previous wave of AI for science companies using neural network methods to replace humans who previously used simulation software?

Expert [00:07:50]: That wave did not eliminate them either. In fact, they still coexist. Fundamentally, computational simulation is still used because this is determined by theory and principles. The question of how well a drug molecule binds to its target protein is scientifically clear in terms of binding activity, as is known from textbooks. It is the free energy of binding between the drug molecule and the protein. Free energy is a concept in statistical physics. There is a formula that can be written down explicitly. As long as the formula is evaluated correctly, in principle you can obtain results exactly the same as those from experiments. The premise is how to calculate it. Therefore, the accuracy of this computational method must be made sufficiently high. Whether the method uses AI models or molecular equation simulation methods, in any case, you need to calculate this formula. What follows is how to keep improving the accuracy of computational models so that this theory can be calculated correctly. In drug development, this is what needs to be done in most cases.

In the past, this simulation was done by some classical physical models and functions. Later, there may be some requirements for accuracy. A higher-dimensional nonlinear method called a neural network can be used to do this. That is, AI naturally transforms things into AI. However, if higher-dimensional nonlinear functions are used for calculation, there will be corresponding performance problems. Maybe in industry and pipelines, they don’t want to pay such high costs, so they make some trade-offs. In addition, it is found that in the classic model, as long as corresponding adjustments are made, the accuracy is sufficient. Therefore, in many cases, for pipeline applications, it is not the pursuit of absolute correctness in theory, but a balance between correctness and performance.

Expert [00:11:00]: That’s right. Throughout the entire AIDD or CADD pipeline, many different modules need to correspond to specific scenarios in drug R&D. Generally, drug R&D also has several milestones. The first is to initiate a project and determine which target protein is genuine. First, confirm that the target genuinely has corresponding biological sites and cellular pathways and can inhibit a particular disease. This is the first step in project initiation. This step mainly involves the investigation of protein targets. A three-dimensional structure of the protein may be needed. If you have the corresponding structure from experiments such as X-ray or NMR, you can obtain it. If not, it is possible to use an AlphaFold-like approach to perform structure prediction using deep learning. This is what DeepMind did well in the past. This is the first step.

The first milestone, target validation, is mainly about determining project initiation, which means deciding to work on this protein target. Next, corresponding drug molecules need to be designed based on the target protein's pocket and its active space. This design process involves several steps. First, after designing a large number of molecules, they must undergo screening. There are several key metrics for screening drug molecules. The first and most standard metric or the first step, is activity. The drug molecule, whether a small or large molecule, must be able to inhibit the activity of the protein. Only in this way can it be effective and achieve the desired therapeutic effect against the disease.

In the second step, when molecules enter the human body, they must not be easily metabolised, nor can they be toxic to the body. For example, they cannot attack the heart or liver. Specific metrics include liver microsomal stability, cardiotoxicity and the inability to cross the blood-brain barrier. These represent another set of indicators. In short, screening is a process similar to looking for a needle in a haystack, performing multiobjective optimisation screening across various metrics from a large pool of generated or designed molecules. Therefore, the first step, molecular design and molecular generation, is an area very suitable for deep learning methods, which, simply put, is about generating molecules. It is just that the generated molecules must match the shape of the protein pocket structure so that they can bind structurally. The binding relationship between a protein and a drug molecule is somewhat like using a key to open a lock, forming a lock-and-key relationship. There is a hole in the keyhole and the goal is to design a key to insert into that hole.

The structure of the drug molecule must be able to bind together, which is called a binder. This molecular structure must meet this requirement and there may be some standard requirements. For example, the molecule must be able to be physically synthesised because you need to be able to produce this drug molecule, which is stored in your computer. You can’t have no entity, so you must be able to produce it and have a better synthesis route. Or if it is an antibody, how the antibody is synthesised and how it is expressed in mice are questions that need to be considered at the survival stage.

Next, there are also indicators such as drug-likeness. In short, these are rules that are already relatively well known. Of course, calling them rules is rather general. They can be refined into dozens or perhaps a series of learnable materials and such data can enable deep learning models to learn. Therefore, with these rule-based materials in place, the model is then given a large number of publicly available drug-like molecules or FDA-approved drug molecules. You know what real and correct molecules look like, so you let AI learn from them. It becomes a model that has learned the language of chemistry, not the Chinese or English that we humans speak today, but the language of chemical molecular structure and you can give the model the ability to generate molecules.

Whether AI is appropriate for many problems depends on the kind of problem. For molecular design problems of this kind, the goal is to obtain a series of better molecules and this is achievable. However, regarding the activity evaluation step we just mentioned, if I now have a molecule, how do I know whether its activity is good? For this, you need a scientific indicator, such as activity, to evaluate it. Activity, as we just mentioned, is physically measurable and has a theoretical basis, so there is no need for AI to reinvent a theory. Because this theory is already known, the goal is simply to calculate it accurately.

There is a method called FEP, which is a free energy perturbation molecular simulation method. It truly captures the physical laws of the binding between proteins and molecules. Since these physical laws are known, this method can be used and quantitatively matched with experimental activity assays. In this way, the method forms a closed loop, which can explain experimental results and help medicinal chemists save a portion of experimental costs. Ideally, you would not need to conduct experiments in the future and could just rely on calculations. In actual practice, however, you might design dozens of molecules and evaluate them using the FEP method. Instead of synthesising all 50 molecules, you might now only need to synthesise the top three to five, because computational methods help you screen out a batch first. This is a relatively standard operation at present.
