THE SIGNAL

  • Patel puts OpenAI at 90%+ NVIDIA compute versus Anthropic at one-third or less, so NVIDIA’s turmoil and a weaker open-source ecosystem favor Anthropic and closed-source labs over OpenAI, which works more closely with NVIDIA.
  • Patel says Jensen’s personal pull lets NVIDIA lock up 60-70% of a supplier’s capacity at below-market prices; a less charismatic successor likely loses this, which could squeeze NVIDIA’s supply allocation and costs (our read).
  • Patel warns that tighter US curbs could trigger Chinese retaliation, since every NVIDIA server and Google TPU server contains Chinese content that China could cut off, a supply-chain tail risk for the whole AI hardware stack.
  • Patel says the US open-model frontier, led by Reflection and Thinking Machines, depends on heavy NVIDIA backing, and inference providers rely on Chinese open models, so both are exposed if NVIDIA’s investing slows and China policy tightens.

01 Succession and governance risk

Patel’s internal favorites are Ian Buck and Jonah Alben — Buck for charisma and dealmaking aptitude, Alben as the closest technical lead to Jensen — though he doubts either can match Jensen across technical, supply-chain, and dealmaking dimensions simultaneously. His preferred external picks are Broadcom’s Charlie Kawwas, whom he rates very highly, and Apple hardware chief Johny Srouji, whom he says are the only two people capable of navigating both supply chain and technical complexity at NVIDIA’s scale; he dismisses AMD’s Lisa Su as unlikely to leave her post. He also waves off wildcard ideas like Palo Alto Networks CEO Nikesh Arora, arguing NVIDIA’s competitive set (OpenAI, Broadcom, Google, Amazon, Anthropic, AMD chip teams) is far tougher than Palo Alto’s.

Operationally, Patel breaks NVIDIA’s Jensen-dependence into three legs: technical decision-making (no clear single technical lead besides possibly Alben, with many others reporting directly to Jensen), supply chain leverage (NVIDIA’s ability to lock up capacity via charisma/relationships, which a less charismatic successor likely can’t replicate), and NVIDIA’s role propping up the ecosystem (open source labs, startups, inference providers). He expects major AI-company investment decisions that previously moved on Jensen’s say-so to now move through committee review, slowing NVIDIA down against well-funded rivals.

02 China dynamics and export policy

Patel, who describes himself as having favored certain types of China restrictions for years, says NVIDIA/Jensen were the industry’s main defenders against export curbs, and that credibility is now gone amid investigations. He expects the policy pendulum to swing toward broader restrictions but hopes for nuance — hard limits on chip sales, TSMC/memory sales, and high-end equipment to China, but preserving some chip-rental access (e.g., via hyperscalers like Google renting to ByteDance, where margin/control stays in US hands) to avoid fully cutting off US leverage. He flags that data sales to China — via data companies supplying frontier-model-generated training environments — represent an unscrutinized channel he expects to draw attention next.

On Chinese labs, Patel says it’s an open secret that Chinese AI developers work closely with NVIDIA engineers (including via NVIDIA’s Shanghai offices) despite formal export bans, and that labs like Zhipu (sanctioned, supporting seven Chinese accelerator types) and DeepSeek (close to Huawei) have already hedged by diversifying hardware, making them comparatively resilient versus NVIDIA-reliant peers. He reassesses the AI 2027 forecast, saying roughly 70% of its verifiable predictions have been accurate so far; asked by the hosts about the forecast’s prediction that China “wakes up” around November 2026, he treats this as plausible but unresolved.

03 Venture capital and startup fallout

Patel says VCs are now largely excluded from direct lab investing as, in his words, OpenAI probably just did its last private round, which he says wasn’t that great, and Anthropic moves toward a public listing, pushing venture attention downstream to inference clouds and application companies. He frames NVIDIA/Jensen as the venture ecosystem’s most important backer, having propped up companies via investment and open-source model support; he questions whether firms like Fireworks and BaseTen can scale from roughly $1 billion to $10 billion in revenue without continued compute financing and open Chinese model access, and whether application companies like Harvey and Sierra can keep improving without open-source models — warning that reliance on closed-source models risks reducing them to mere wrappers around someone else’s model.

04 Anthropic’s benchmark jump and data strategy

Discussing SemiAnalysis’s own benchmark results on a new Anthropic model, Patel reports dramatic improvement in software engineering and chip design but flat performance elsewhere, which he attributes to reinforcement-learning environments being the actual vector for scaling — domains without unique proprietary data and RL environments don’t improve regardless of model scale. He notes Anthropic has pivoted from an earlier stance against building its own chip (previously seen as distracting from AGI-focused work) toward now believing models themselves can help design chips, and says the company builds most of its chip-design data internally rather than buying from vendors, while in biology it both runs its own wet lab and buys external data — concluding that specialized expert-generated data, not generic labeling, is where real business value now lies.

  • More NVIDIA damage may be coming: Reuters reported a second affair during recording and Thompson’s DC sources hint at worse; his own guess is that Megaspeed chips reached China’s military, making tighter bans likelier.
  • Thompson argues NVIDIA still leads on tokens per watt, so power limits on data centers help it, though he speculates that allocation favoring CoreWeave over Amazon may draw scrutiny once Huang is gone.
  • Thompson runs his printer, label printer and inventory app through Claude without touching their software, suggesting value shifts from app interfaces to agent-accessible systems of record (our read); agent-filled carts drove purchases he’d otherwise skip.

01 NVIDIA’s leaderless machine

NVIDIA’s entire structure, Thompson says, mirrors early Steve Jobs-era Apple: a functional organization where specialists execute and only the CEO synthesizes everything, enabling extraordinary speed (yearly product cadences, the Mellanox acquisition) but zero redundancy. He notes Huang spoke recently of running NVIDIA until age 120, and the company never underwent the succession wake-up call Apple got when Steve Jobs’s death was gradual rather than sudden. Both hosts flag this as a genuine governance failure that may explain why, despite the stock falling roughly 15% on the news, NVIDIA’s board still moved to oust him — a classic principal-agent/fiduciary-liability dynamic Thompson walks through at the start.

02 How Huang built the moat

NVIDIA’s foundational bet was the programmable shader concept underlying CUDA, a wager that nearly sank the company when early chips ran hot and underperformed rivals like ATI. The real validation came with AlexNet’s image recognition breakthrough on CUDA, which Thompson says was the true wake-up call predating ChatGPT by years. The 2019-20 Mellanox acquisition, which Thompson credits with the high-speed networking behind NVIDIA’s systems, let NVIDIA sell interconnected systems, not just chips — a moat AMD, per Thompson, still can’t match because it lacks comparable systems-software investment. Huang also personally toured Asia securing memory and supply-chain commitments, which Thompson says single-handedly convinced historically conservative Asian suppliers to scale for an AI boom that might not have happened without his evangelism.

03 Diversification bets and who benefits from his exit

Huang resisted NVIDIA ending up dependent on a handful of powerful buyers like Amazon, Google, and Microsoft who could squeeze margins the way they have with Intel, by backing Neoclouds, open-source models, and broad distribution. Thompson says this diversification effort, not altruism, kept the AI ecosystem more varied than it otherwise would be; losing Huang is framed as potentially good for NVIDIA’s pricing power (since TPUs remain the only real training-scale competitor) but bad for startups and the open ecosystem that depended on his patronage.

04 The China calculus and Washington’s revenge

Thompson’s central argument: Washington’s chip-ban logic is self-defeating because ecosystems can’t be dictated top-down — only demand from developers who already prefer CUDA and NVIDIA chips can prevent China from building an alternative. He traces the DC rupture to 2022, when NVIDIA complied with Biden-era bandwidth-based export rules by releasing a deliberately weaker chip within a month — technically legal but seen by security officials as bad-faith circumvention, souring relations from day one. Huang then lobbied Trump directly to reverse the H20 ban, deepening distrust among both Biden-era national security officials and bipartisan Trump administration figures (Sharp names Marco Rubio and Scott Bessent as increasingly impatient). On the Alice Huang allegations, both hosts stress distinction between fact and speculation: Sharp notes only about 1% of NVIDIA’s hardware allocation went to Megaspeed, and Thompson sees no evidence the relationship altered Huang’s already-public pro-China-sales policy.

05 Bubble financing and the exit’s broader cost

Huang had been functioning as an informal backstop for Neocloud and AI-buildout financing with NVIDIA’s balance sheet — a role Thompson doubts a more numbers-driven interim or future CEO will continue, noting Neocloud credit conditions were already tightening before this news. He frames the chip-ban escalation likely to follow as a long-run disaster for the US semiconductor industry, reviving the historical “sell n-minus-two” strategy argument: restricting access only accelerates China’s incentive to build independent alternatives it could later export globally, undercutting decades of US tech-transfer strategy.

The episode closes with a lengthy, unrelated celebration of the show’s 300th episode: Thompson describes building a 3D-printed bolt/parts storage system and label-printing workflow entirely through conversational AI (Claude) rather than CAD software, plus a long-running saga of getting an AI agent to click through macOS permission dialogs on a dedicated Mac mini.

  • Goldberg says the $500B compute financing platform also faced internal pushback at NVIDIA, and only Huang could get its six big PE/bank backers to sign on, so post-Huang management may want to pull back balance-sheet support.
  • Goldberg says Huang propped up neo-clouds because he preferred hundreds of small buyers to three large ones; if that funding shrinks, CoreWeave and Nebius face fewer new rivals while NVIDIA’s buyers concentrate.
  • Goldberg says Huang was already hitting TSMC’s allocation ceiling this year and was refused more, and that hyperscaler deals ran CEO to CEO, so Kress negotiates at a disadvantage.
  • Goldberg flags fast-growing Japanese neo-clouds serving Chinese customers, possibly rivaling Malaysia in scale, so Japan could become a target for new export rules (our read).

01 Resignation and succession vacuum

NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang resigned this week after New York Times reporting on an alleged affair with a woman purportedly connected to Chinese intelligence, followed by a second report of an affair with an internal direct report. Colette Kress was named interim CEO. Both hosts compare the moment to Apple’s loss of Steve Jobs, with Bajarin noting Huang served as the “north star” for both the company and the broader AI industry, giving long keynotes and personally setting NVIDIA’s culture under a flat management structure. Goldberg calls the board’s apparent lack of a succession plan “corporate governance one-zero-one” failure, though he speculates Huang himself may have resisted putting one in place. Both agree the board now faces added legal scrutiny — amid what they count as four or five ongoing federal investigations and upcoming congressional hearings — that will slow the CEO search; Goldberg guesses, calling it a made-up number, that a permanent CEO arrives around March of next year, and Bajarin suggests GTC as a natural target. Internal candidate Ian Buck is seen as a natural fit given tenure but inexperience with earnings calls; Goldberg cites Polymarket odds placing Buck first, Kress second, ARM’s Rene Haas an unexplained third, and Huang himself still at 6%.

02 NVIDIA’s balance-sheet role unravels

Goldberg’s central financial argument is that Huang personally functioned as a “central bank” for the AI buildout, using NVIDIA’s balance sheet to provide credit support and backstop investments that lowered neo-clouds’ cost of capital enough to make their data-center projects viable. He argues this bridged a gap between what capital markets were willing to pay and what Huang believed NVIDIA’s technology was worth — a gap he says no other current entity, not AMD and not typical Wall Street sources, is positioned to fill. Without that support, Goldberg expects NVIDIA to keep providing some backstop, but far less of it, while the widely-touted $500 billion compute financing platform — which he notes carries only a press release and no signed term sheet — likely stalls. Bajarin frames the risk in supply-chain terms: without pre-funding, the various players along the build-out (engineering, construction, materials) must now absorb more risk themselves roughly two years ahead of data-center completion dates.

03 Hyperscalers and TSMC gain leverage

Both hosts agree this shift tilts power toward Amazon, Google, and Microsoft, which can self-fund data centers and are, as Goldberg puts it, very tough price negotiators — he expects purchasing managers to “turn the screws” on upcoming NVIDIA GPU orders given the company’s current turmoil. They also expect hyperscalers to accelerate dual-sourcing efforts toward AMD and Google’s TPUs. On the supply side, Goldberg argues TSMC becomes more of a “kingmaker” in wafer allocation decisions now that Huang’s personal relationships and negotiating presence in Taiwan are gone, though he speculates TSMC might extend NVIDIA extra capacity next year if it sees the company genuinely threatened. Bajarin adds that if NVIDIA’s demand profile weakens, Broadcom stands to benefit most, which would flow through to Google TPU and AMD demand, given wafer-model assumptions about relative customer size.

04 Political-hit speculation and China policy

Goldberg lays out — explicitly as his own theory, not established fact — that the affair story’s DC origin (rather than sourcing from Beijing) suggests a coordinated effort by China-hawk political operatives to remove Huang, possibly motivated by resentment of his lobbying style, his closeness to the president, or his role as collateral damage in unrelated political rivalries. Both hosts separately question the practicality of further restricting chip sales to China, noting the Bureau of Industry and Security is “woefully underfunded” to enforce controls, and chips would simply reroute through Malaysia, Singapore, Japan, or Korea. Bajarin raises the risk of Chinese retaliation against supply-chain components (PCBs, substrates, trailing-edge parts) needed to build the very boards NVIDIA chips sit on.

05 Neo-cloud earnings diverge

CoreWeave shares rose roughly 20% while Nebius fell nearly 20% this week. Goldberg attributes Nebius’s decline to a straightforward delay in a data-center’s completion and power-on date pushed into next year, lowering guidance. CoreWeave’s gain is linked to strong overall numbers after weeks of credit-quality concerns, plus rising spot pricing — Goldberg says about 130,000 GPUs, which he describes as Hoppers, come off contract next year and can go to the spot market, where prices now sit above the original contract rates. CoreWeave also highlighted a shift away from long-term, hyperscaler-funded CapEx contracts toward shorter, prepayment-based agreements, which Goldberg flags as positive now but of uncertain sustainability once credit-support dynamics change. Bajarin raises tension between CoreWeave’s self-positioning as a hyperscaler alternative for enterprise inference versus its actual reliance on spot/training-driven revenue, while Goldberg notes customer concentration is heading in the right direction (top two customers now ~60% of revenue, down from higher shares last year and at IPO).

01 Math as the template

Patel’s central analogical argument is that mathematics is a preview of what happens to any verifiable domain once AI gets good enough. He traces the trajectory from AI failing basic 4-digit arithmetic in 2023, to struggling with high-school competition math in 2024, to winning an IMO gold medal in 2025, to solving open Erdős problems, to — about a month before writing — solving a Millennium Prize problem in 88 hours. He acknowledges Toby Ord’s critique that this progress is skewed toward verification-heavy work (finding counterexamples) rather than generating profound new mathematical questions, but counters that even without that ability, AI has already cracked a Millennium Prize problem.

02 Why AI research itself is verifiable

Patel argues AI research resembles the kind of math AI is already good at: combinatorial, persistence-driven discovery rather than deep theory generation. He uses the transformer as an example — citing a discussion with researcher Sholto Douglas and comments from Chris Olah — arguing all the transformer’s components (attention, residual connections, layer norm, Adam optimizer) existed in the literature before 2017, and the breakthrough was really combinatorial search, something AI is disproportionately good at. In the Appendix, he breaks AI R&D into three buckets — pretraining, post-training, and inference efficiency — and argues each has strong verifiable proxies (held-out loss, compute-to-reach-a-score, GPU utilization, latency), making the whole discipline trainable via RL, citing Anthropic’s internal CoBench benchmark and the public nanoGPT speedrun as examples.

03 Why automation compounds fast

Once AI matches top researchers, Patel argues progress accelerates via three multiplying factors: automated researchers work roughly 10x human speed (per OpenAI’s GDPval benchmark showing GPT-5.2 completing tasks 11x faster than humans), they’re as capable as the very best humans (Patel estimates ~80% of a lab’s algorithmic progress comes from its top 50 researchers), and there can be vastly more of them running in parallel — illustrated by a swarm of Claude instances formalizing Fermat’s Last Theorem. He notes diminishing returns from compute bottlenecks and coordination costs but argues the combination still yields multi-fold speedups.

04 Singularity versus spark

The critical open question, per Patel, is whether the rate AIs get smarter outpaces the rate ideas get harder to find (already true today, with labs needing to quadruple R&D compute and double headcount yearly just to hold pace). If smarter-faster wins, you get a software-only singularity — superintelligence within months without new compute. If ideas-harder-to-find wins, you get a “spark”: roughly 3-6 years of progress in one year (a jump Patel compares to going from GPT-3.5 to “GPT-6 Astra”), followed by a plateau until compute catches up — though Patel notes AI compute has already been growing ~3x a year and automated researchers would help design better chips and fabs regardless.

05 Learning from the real world

Patel addresses the objection that datacenter training can’t replicate real-world expertise (building a business, winning court cases, trading markets). He argues scaled RL training builds general meta-learning skills, drawing an analogy to human evolution producing general intelligence from a crude survival-only “verifier,” and to real humans like Napoleon (major victories by 27) and Rockefeller (running a top refinery by 25) who achieved mastery without decades of experience. He expects future AIs to reach top human performance in most domains within months of deployment via vastly more new RL environments, massive historical “backtest” prediction training (e.g., predicting general relativity from pre-1700 data), and parallelized real-world shadowing (e.g., an AI copy-set interviewing every employee at a company on day one).

06 Bottleneck objections addressed

In the Appendix, Patel confronts four objections: continual learning (he now thinks scaled in-context learning, not new architectures, may suffice, though he assigns this a real chance of failure), finding new verifiable proxies for future paradigms (he argues historically proxies predate paradigm shifts, e.g., translation benchmarks before transformers), data bottlenecks (he argues AI-generated data pipelines, citing a DeepSeek paper, already drive most RL gains), and experiment-compute scarcity (he calculates that by 2027 OpenAI’s compute fleet could support roughly 7,660 GPT-3-sized experiment runs per day across thousands of parallel automated researchers).
