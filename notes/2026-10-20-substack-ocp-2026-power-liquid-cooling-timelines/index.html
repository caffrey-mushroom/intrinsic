#thermal #power #datacenter

Substack · 2026-10-20

Core Insights

  1. In power and liquid cooling, there were no major architectural changes at OCP this year; the movement is in specs,

timing and detail.

  1. The NVIDIA and AMD keynotes restated existing roadmaps, and AMD’s only new launch, the Helion agentic rack, is a

product-line extension rather than an architectural change.

  1. The break-out sessions did show finer changes: NVIDIA wants SST deployment readiness by 2028, earlier than investors

generally expect; the runway for single-phase cold plates remains intact and should stay mainstream through 2030; capacitors inside the PSU may squeeze out the CBU before it ever scales; and the ±400 V versus 800 V argument has converged on voltage-to-ground, so tray-side specs are compatible and suppliers are not facing two product lines.

  1. We attended the 2026 OCP Global Summit in San Jose from October 12 to 15. Our note on power and liquid cooling below

draws on learnings from the show floor and 95 sessions, 22 of which we attended in person.

  1. We identify three key themes in power and liquid cooling and discuss the debates surrounding each theme, before

ending the note with a roundup of discussions with investors as well as some photographs from the show floor.

Key Themes

Theme 1: In 800 VDC, the main changes are in timing and where energy storage sits, rather than architecture

  1. On the facility side, NVIDIA moves the AC-to-800 VDC conversion upstream in three steps, from beside the rack to

medium voltage (660 kW power rack 2026, power center 2027, 5 MW SST power block 2028). The SST schedule is earlier than investors expected, but specs and certification are unfinished, so volume still sits beyond end-2028.

  1. Across rack configurations through the 570 kW generation, the rack interior remains on a 54 V busbar; the native 800

V rack is expected in 2028. Energy storage is distributed across BBUs inside the rack, PSU capacitors, CBUs in the sidecar and ESS at the SST; NVIDIA is using PSU capacitors rather than supercaps for power smoothing.

  1. The ±400 V / 800 V debate converges on voltage-to-ground and tray-side specs are compatible. AMD is pushing VPD

standardization at OCP, with the impact landing on supplier structure and double-sided cooling; adoption is now visible at scale on both GPUs and ASICs.

Theme 2: The single-phase cold-plate runway is longer than consensus; the change is in TIM, materials and the sidecar

  1. Single-phase has a life expectancy running to 2030; two-phase is still early, with the bottleneck in packaging, TIM

and the QD/manifold rather than the coolant itself. The next step for cold plates is microchannels, polymer and diamond, which are design and materials upgrades.

  1. The one new item is the AALC sidecar: an existing air-cooled hall can host a near-1 MW rack without touching facility

water, and it is also the most likely first commercial home for two-phase. The OCP workgroup still puts the immersion inflection at 2028.

Theme 3: Vera Rubin’s bottleneck is shifting toward full-rack testing and the data hall; rack weight is becoming a specification; quantum computing remains beyond 2029-30

Subscribed

  1. Foxconn’s 80% first-pass yield at L10 and NVIDIA’s one-minute tray assembly suggest that assembly is becoming less of

a constraint, while Pegatron identifies full-rack testing as a key determinant of output.

  1. Open DC v0.7 writes 15,000 lb for rack-plus-sidecar into the facility spec, and lightweight materials are starting to

appear.

  1. Fault-tolerant quantum sits beyond 2029-30, with DC hardware demand concentrated in cryo, FPGA/GPU decoders and

low-latency NICs.

Power: 800 VDC timing, where storage sits, and the ±400 V / 800 V convergence

±400 V vs 800 V: voltage-to-ground is converging, while distribution remains split between two approaches

  1. Mt Diablo 400 (Microsoft, Meta, Google) is a three-wire ±400 V system: beyond the positive and negative poles, a

third, current-carrying neutral runs from the source to the load and is grounded at the neutral. This gives 800 V pole-to-pole and ±400 V to ground. NVIDIA’s design and the OCP SST specification use a two-wire 800 V system with no neutral.

  1. The two camps each moved one step this year. The three CSPs set the tray-side power-module input specification as

“±400 V first, 800 V later,” partly because the lower 400 V insulation requirement to ground can simplify certification. NVIDIA retains a two-wire design, with current carried by the positive and negative conductors and a non-current-carrying protective earth (PE), as in both schemes. In the first phase, however, it grounds the output midpoint at the source through a high-value resistor (high-resistance grounding). Under normal operation, the positive and negative sit at +400 V and −400 V to ground, respectively, as in the three-wire scheme.

  1. The main difference appears in the fault-current path and protection design. NVIDIA says it adopted high-resistance

grounding to make initial regulatory approval easier. Insulation requirements and certification approaches have therefore converged to some extent, while distribution remains different, including whether a neutral is present and whether a single-pole breaker can be used. Our conclusion is that neither approach has a clear overall advantage; protection speed is likely to be a key factor.

  1. For tray-side PDB and brick suppliers, the specifications point to one product line. The CSP specification lists

shared ±400 V / 800 V module input (±10%, 800 V reinforced isolation), suggesting the same module can serve both types of halls and suppliers can design to the 800 V insulation class.

  1. On the show floor, Google’s and Delta’s products were labeled ±400 V, while NVIDIA’s PDB wall was labeled 800 V.

These are two labels for products in the same broad class.

AC/DC: NVIDIA moves AC-to-800 VDC conversion from beside the rack toward medium voltage, with SST deployment targeted earlier than expected

NVIDIA laid out three stages for 800 VDC at OCP:

  1. From 26Q3, a 660 kW power rack sits next to the compute racks, converts AC to 800 VDC and feeds the racks, one power

rack per four compute racks, with no change to the hall’s own AC distribution;

  1. From 27Q2, several of the same power racks are consolidated at the end of the row into a power center serving a whole

row, aimed at row-level retrofit and colo;

  1. From 28Q1, 34.5 kV medium voltage goes straight into the SST, each 5 MW SST power block carries a set of energy

storage, and 6+1 blocks serve 30 MW of compute, the end state NVIDIA says it is aiming for, cutting grid-to-chip conversion from seven stages to four.

Our judgment is that 1) and 2) is most likely to happen, but content per W in AC/DC on the power rack does not rise quickly; what really gets added is BBU, CBU and the rest of the contents.

On 3), “deployment ready” in 1Q28 is earlier than the end-2028-to-2029 most investors assume, yet neither the SST spec nor certification of the SSCB protection device is finished, so we still think meaningful volume waits until after end-2028. NVIDIA’s purpose in putting a date out now is to push the industry to fix the SST spec first.

DC/DC (inside the rack): the 54 V busbar runs to 570 kW; the native 800 V rack waits until 2028

  1. NVIDIA’s rack power path is GB200/GB300 at 145 kW → Vera Rubin NVL72 at 330 kW → 570 kW next → native 800 V at about

1 MW (from 2028). Through 570 kW, the rack interior is expected to remain on a 54 V busbar; a key difference is that 800 V entering the rack first passes a 1RU, 90-95 kW DC/DC shelf that steps it to 54 V. OCP’s LVDC working group draws the boundary between 54 V and 800 V-straight-to-tray at 500-700 kW per rack, which matches. After native 800 V, 800 V goes directly to the tray PDB and then down to 50 V / 12 V: the three CSPs’ power-module spec (12/6/3 kW, DOSA common pinout, expected to be finalized in 2H27) defines this stage, while a second step from high voltage directly to 12 V does not yet have a stated timeline.

  1. Breaking it down: 1) what gets added inside the rack in 2026-28 is the DC/DC shelf, from the same suppliers as the

power shelf; 2) the DC/DC shelf is expected to remain in use after native 800 V; NVIDIA says it will be used long-term in storage and networking racks, and some industry participants expect the wider hall (including networking, storage and CDU) to move to 800 V; 3) bricks moving to a multi-supplier common pinout is a price pressure from 2028 onward, on the same logic as VPD.

BBU/CBU demand is defined by grid specifications

The ride-through spec jointly published by Google, Meta, Microsoft and NVIDIA requires a data center to hold through grid disturbances for 0.15-2 seconds depending on the voltage band and to return to 90% load within 2 seconds, with the load-ramp-rate spec due in 27Q1. Behind it, data-center load-loss events have grown from 1.5 GW (2024) to 3.8 GW (2026), and NERC’s first large-load standards go to FERC at year-end. What it means for the supply chain: storage moves from optional to a mandatory BOM item, and its job widens from backup to power smoothing and load averaging (the SST is most efficient at a steady 90-95% load, with the ESS absorbing the GPUs’ load swings).

Where BBU/CBU sits: from inside the rack out to three layers, with NVIDIA’s PSU capacitors squeezing supercap CBUs out of the way

Storage splits into three layers:

  1. Electrolytic capacitors inside the PSU do power smoothing; NVIDIA’s Vera Rubin carries 400 J per GPU and cuts

training peak power by 13-51%, and NVIDIA says this is cheaper than adding batteries or supercaps, suggesting supercaps may see limited use for power smoothing (400 J per GPU covers about 0.2 seconds, so longer-duration ride-through storage would need to sit at the SST);

  1. CBUs/BBUs inside the sidecar, where both the Mt Diablo sidecar and NVIDIA’s power rack can take BBUs;
  2. ESS at the SST, where NVIDIA argues for keeping only minimal storage in the rack and putting the rest at the SST, and

Google’s next-generation rack is designed without rack-level storage, with batteries moved to row level. Cell requirements rise at the same time: per-cell power goes from 120 W to 200-300 W, and battery makers see potential limits to LFP alone and expect a shift toward NCA+LFP in 2028. Our view is that BBU volume is likely to persist but migrate upstream, while demand for cell power density rises; on NVIDIA’s platform, PSU capacitors could displace supercap CBUs before they scale materially, leaving demand more concentrated in CSP sidecars.

VPD: AMD pushes standardization at OCP; the impact is on supplier structure and thermal design

AMD’s MI455X is the first AMD GPU in volume using VPD, with current density rising across three generations from 0.88 to 2 A/mm²; the next-generation 3 A/mm² VPD module goes out to RFP on an AMD-defined common pinout (end-2027), and AMD proposes contributing the pinout and qualification standard to OCP.

We see three layers of impact:

  1. Supplier structure around the standard, where AMD named MPS, Infineon, TI, Delta, ADI and Murata as partners in the

room; second-stage VR has typically been a module on AMD’s platform (earlier generations used a 9×10 two-phase power block), and this time the same footprint moves to four phases with a lower profile so it can sit directly under the GPU; the OCP-backed common footprint lets several vendors compete in one form factor and be interchangeable, so pricing power may increasingly sit with whoever defines the spec and differentiation moves to module current density, reliability and double-sided cooling;

  1. Thermals and the board, where moving the VR to the back of the GPU requires a double-sided cold plate and a cavity in

the PCB, so each accelerator adds one more cold-plate face and a board-level design stage;

  1. Components, where the 8:1 IBC creates a 6 V intermediate bus, high-frequency magnetics are the bottleneck, and new

parts such as the TLVR choke were already on the show floor. As we noted in a recent report, VPD adoption appears to be broadening, and the trend may continue as more platforms adopt the approach.

Debate 1: ±400 V vs 800 V

  1. The 800 V two-wire camp (NVIDIA, the OCP SST spec): higher power density for the same conductor and fewer components,

but higher fault energy.

  1. The ±400 V three-wire camp (the CSPs’ Mt Diablo): more load flexibility and lower fault energy, but it needs midpoint

balancing and the fault paths are more complex. NVIDIA’s first phase grounds the midpoint through a high resistance, matching the three-wire scheme on voltage-to-ground; industry views suggest neither approach has a clear overall advantage, while protection speed is likely to be a key factor, while the grounding method decides whether a single-pole SSCB can be used.

  1. We think ±400 V and 800 V coexist long-term, tray-side specs are compatible and suppliers face one product line, with

the difference confined to protection and grounding at system level; NVIDIA’s two-wire high-resistance grounding requires breaking both poles, so SSCB content per rack is roughly double that of the three-wire scheme, but the certification bar for two-pole devices rises with it.

Debate 2: is the Solid State Circuit Breaker necessary?

  1. For: Google says the rack-side reverse-blocking diode is necessary, that an SSCB clears a fault in 0.2 ms against 13

ms for a conventional MCCB, and that the MCCB’s clearing time drops neighboring healthy racks by more than 10%. Siemens measured it: on the same 800 V short, the SSCB opened within 15 µs and passed under 500 A, while the MCCB and the fuse let 27 kA and 17 kA through.

  1. Against: Schneider argues conventional breakers are sufficient where there is a current-limiting converter upstream

(a DC UPS, some SSTs), and that going all-SSCB doubles the number of remote power panels, with cost and thermal management still unformed; the UL certification path exists but is incomplete, and the 125 A SSCB is one of the remaining concerns on NVIDIA’s readiness table.

  1. Our conclusion is that the SSCB appears necessary under native 800 V and the SST architecture, though it may not be

required during the transition (behind a power rack); it is a schedule risk for 2027 deployment, not a contest between technology paths.

Liquid Cooling: Single-Phase Still Has Runway; Double-Sided Cold Plates and Better Materials Add Volume and ASP

Cold plate: single-phase design and materials keep improving, and both volume and unit price move up

  1. The four panelists (Google, Meta, Microsoft and Corintis) described a long runway for single-phase, and none offered

a date for two-phase. Corintis argued that the cold plate does not have a single fixed power limit; the constraints are local heat flux, tray height and QD/manifold hydraulics.

  1. Google said skiving has yet to reach its physical limit.
  2. The design upgrades already have measured results: Corintis’s microfluidic core cut junction-to-inlet rise on an

NVIDIA H200 at 1 LPM by 19% on the GPU and 23% on HBM.

  1. Microsoft’s unitary-cell non-uniform flow (10-140% per region) cut junction temperature by up to 10 °C at identical

power map, TIM and flow.

  1. JWS’s micro-channel POC cut GPU cold-plate pressure drop by 44%.
  2. Wiwynn’s PPS-plus-glass-fiber top cover cut cold-plate weight by 66% and the inner-manifold body by 52%.
  3. On materials, Google is moving to engineered polymers and higher-conductivity composite bases because of copper price

volatility, ultra-fine skiving yield and fleet issues (debris, leak detection, water chemistry), while Auras is pushing a 900-2,000 W/m·K diamond-copper base paired with 45 °C inlet water.

  1. On demand, cold plate went from under 10% to over 90% of top-tier accelerator TDP in 24 months, the market runs from

roughly US$7b (2025) to about US$27b (2030), and D2C supply stays tight until 2028.

  1. Looking at it as a whole, the cold plate is a design contest rather than a commoditized part: for the same GPU it

moves from a skived copper block to a microchannel, diamond or polymer composite part, so unit price and content per GPU rise together and tight supply supports pricing.

10.The content goes to suppliers that can co-design microchannels, materials and weight reduction.

Image 4: Auras cold plates for the AMD MI355 (left) and the ConnectX-8 SuperNIC (right). Source: FUNDA; Auras booth.

TIM and the rest of the junction stack: the TIM-free path now has measured numbers

  1. Wiwynn bonded laser-cut single-crystal diamond microchannels (0.1 mm fins) with indium directly onto a silicon TTV

with no TIM at all: at 3 kW / 360 W/cm², Tj max fell from 106.2 to 77.1 °C and thermal resistance was 38.2% below a copper cold plate with PCM TIM, short of the 59.5% simulated, with the gap in the bonding layer.

  1. Cold plate and TIM together account for 65-85% of total thermal resistance, which suggests the junction stack may be

a key area for further improvement.

  1. The module side is removing interfaces at the same time: in-module direct-to-chip (integrated cold plate plus

blind-mate QDs) cuts the stack from four thermal resistances to two, simulating 74.9 °C at 500 W against a 95 °C limit, but a 2 mm QD alone adds 6-10 psi, leak and QD interoperability are unresolved, and an external cold plate with dry TIM remains viable below 200 W.

  1. Testing is converging too: hyperscalers want a common TTV and test TIM, because supplier self-test and customer

in-house test often disagree.

  1. We think value at the junction shifts from “applying TIM” to “bonding and integration”, which over the medium term

moves content away from TIM material makers and toward cold-plate makers that can do bonding and packaging co-design; volume through 2027 stays with PCM TIM, as neither diamond nor in-module has a production timeline.

Two-phase cold plate: standards first, deployment timing remains open

  1. Google, Meta and Microsoft did not provide a date for two-phase cold plates, and the six two-phase vendors on the

panel focused first on aligning refrigerant class, QD interface and test format.

  1. Chemours says the industry currently sits on R-515B, whose GWP (global warming potential, relative to CO₂ at 1) is

still above 150; the EU F-gas rules are expected to push this application below GWP 150 in 2030, so today’s choice may need to change to meet the regulation and two-phase systems may require another refrigerant transition.

  1. Changing generation of refrigerant means re-qualifying pressure ratings, seals and material compatibility across cold

plate and CDU, which helps explain why customers are not yet committing to large-scale two-phase deployment and why the panel is currently focusing on refrigerant class and QD interface.

  1. We think single-phase stays mainstream until 2028-29, and that two-phase’s first commercial home is the sidecar and

the CDU, not the GPU cold plate.

Air cooling remains relevant: general compute may favor a wider air-cooled rack over liquid cooling

  1. Microsoft and Lenovo proposed a 900 mm-wide air-cooled rack (750 mm chassis) for general compute: +50% airflow area,

one-third lower air velocity and 20-30% lower fan power per kW; heatsink volume rises from 2,325 cm³ to 3,274 cm³ (+41%), supporting a CPU of roughly 870 W up to 970 W.

  1. CPU TDP keeps climbing: top-of-stack is 650 W today and heads above 1,000 W by 2030, DDR6 runs up to 32 channels per

socket, and LPDDR6 SOCAMM is judged not cost-effective for general compute, meaning general-compute memory stays on RDIMM while SOCAMM/LPDDR volume stays concentrated on AI server platforms, and 32 channels per socket pushes both board area and thermal requirements up.

  1. The slides estimate that an all-liquid facility costs 50% more than a hybrid one and that ORW is overkill for general

compute; but this is early data, both companies stressed that this is not an OCP contribution and does not yet have a formal spec or workstream, and facility HVAC and TCO have not been worked through.

  1. Air also remains part of AI racks: AMD’s 246 kW Helios rack still rejects about 27 kW (roughly a tenth) to air.
  2. For the supply chain, liquid cooling may remain concentrated in AI racks: the next generation of CPU racks goes to

bigger heatsinks, wider chassis and lower-speed fans, a spec upgrade rather than displacement for fan, heatsink and chassis suppliers, and a TAM boundary for the cold-plate and CDU group.

AALC / L2A sidecar: an existing hall can host a near-1 MW rack without touching facility water

  1. The Mega AALC sidecar led by AMD and Meta at OCP places a heat exchanger, fans and a coolant management unit in the

footprint of one double-wide rack next to the IT rack: the rack runs liquid internally and rejects heat to room air, so a hall with air only and no facility water can host a 0.5-1 MW rack without retrofit.

  1. The design envelope is roughly 30 °C ambient and 40 °C liquid inlet, and it uses 30-40% less cooling power than an

RDHx.

  1. The spec requires support for both single-phase and two-phase coolant, but the scale they reach differs sharply:

single-phase needs more than ten rack widths to handle 1 MW (about 100 kW per rack width, so one double-wide only reaches the 200 kW class), which means reaching 0.5-1 MW inside a single double-wide sidecar requires two-phase coolant; the slides gave no number for how much narrower it gets.

  1. The white paper has gone to OCP and spec 1.0 is due at year-end. The matching product on the floor is Microsoft’s

Atlas L2A sidecar: 256 kW per unit, 1:1 with VR200/VR300, today supplied mainly by Delta (show-floor information).

  1. Our view is that this is the most practical bridge for 2026-27, and for thermal players and ODMs it is a new category

that integrates heat exchanger, pump, fans and CDU functions into a sidecar; the cost is floor space and one unit per rack, which is also why two-phase’s first commercial home may be the sidecar.

Image 5: Microsoft’s Atlas L2A sidecar, front with 36 fans and 256 kW (top) and rear with pump, manifold and filter (bottom). Source: FUNDA; Microsoft booth.

Immersion: the inflection point is expected around 2028, with reliability central to the debate

  1. The OCP Immersion project keeps its 2028 inflection; the new white paper argues from reliability: immersion MTBF is

3.4-12× that of air, while cold-plate systems get less reliable as more components go onto cold plates.

  1. On standards, the immersion version of the ORv3 power spec is published, UL 2417 fluid certification is live and the

material-compatibility spec lands in 2027; fluids are converging on PAO, with the final spec waiting for the first OEM to build immersion-native hardware (around 2028).

  1. On market size, Promersion puts cold plate at roughly US$7b in 2025 and US$27b in 2030, and immersion at about US$5b

in 2028 and US$18b in 2030. Through 2030 the volume is in cold plate and CDU. The estimates suggest immersion is unlikely to become material before 2028.

Debate 1: has single-phase hit the wall?

  1. The wall camp: Promersion argues that 2026 accelerators at about 2.3 kW have already crossed single-phase’s economic

boundary of roughly 2.1 kW, and that the physical limit is about 4 kW per device (roughly 17 bar on an 830 mm² die), which accelerators reach around 2029.

  1. The runway camp: measured data from three hyperscalers puts the bottleneck in packaging, TIM, tray height and QD

hydraulics, not the coolant.

  1. We lean toward the runway camp: two-phase is unlikely to become mainstream before 2028-29, standardization is the

precondition, and the refrigerant rules may yet change once more.

Debate 2: is the GPU cold plate differentiated or a standard part?

  1. The differentiation camp: diamond and non-uniform flow both show 10-25% measured improvement.
  2. The standard-part camp: Meta wants a reference cold plate and drop-in second sources, Google is moving to polymer,

and QD interoperability may be making the interface less of a barrier.

  1. Our reading is that standardization stops at the test baseline, so unit-price pressure on GPU cold plates is limited,

and the added content sits in cold plates beyond the GPU (CPU, DIMM, NIC, switch, optical module, SSD) and in the spec step-up of CDUs and QD/manifold (CDU 2.3 MW, QD from 2” to 3-4”).

Rack Test, Rack Weight and Quantum Computing

The rack bottleneck moves from assembly to full-rack test, while the data hall standardizes

  1. NVIDIA says Vera Rubin capacity is now “thousands of racks a week,” with a VR compute tray assembled in 1 minute

against 90 minutes for GB200; FII’s VR versus GB comparison is 94% vs 56% automation, 80% vs 39% first-pass yield at L10 and 3.5 vs 4.5 months NPI, with more than 1,000 test bays and over 100 MW of test power, and Rubin ramping from September.

  1. Foxconn’s message is that assembly is becoming less of a constraint; Pegatron points to the next bottleneck:

full-rack testing is emerging as a key determinant of output, each rack takes about 1.27 days to validate, and VR full production is expected in 27Q1.

  1. Acceptance runs in three gates: L10 tray, L11 full rack (including power-shelf firmware, leak and flow) and L12 at

the customer site running fabric, NCCL and training tests.

  1. For new data halls, standardization may be as important as capacity constraints: new builds follow Open DC v0.7

(written jointly by Google, Meta, NVIDIA and AMD), with a 12 MW minimum cluster and row power per linear foot rising from 125 kW (2027) to 350 kW (from 2028), aligning the rack-to-hall interface and shortening dock time.

  1. A more immediate constraint is the existing hall, where power density and floor loading were mostly not designed for

these racks, and Vantage says roughly 40% of capacity in existing data centers sits idle, with planning now done by density and weight per row (the panel moderator separately cited about 30% of rack/CDU commissioning failing, unverified).

  1. The facility constraint on 2027 volume is therefore likely to fall on retrofits of older halls, while new builds are

spec-led.

Rack weight and Open DC

  1. Open DC writes weight into the facility spec: 8,000 lb (3,629 kg) per rack and 15,000 lb (6,804 kg) for rack plus

sidecar, equivalent to roughly 3,900-4,200 kg/m². Today AMD’s Helios is about 7,000 lb; Wiwynn expects a single-width AI rack to pass 4,000 kg in 2028, already above the Open DC per-rack limit.

  1. Three weight-reduction paths run in parallel: raise floor loading first, then move power and storage out of the

compute rack (sidecar, SST), and only then substitute materials (Wiwynn’s PPS cold-plate cover at −66% and an aluminum HX at −52%, both still in test).

  1. Taking them separately: new builds are solved by Open DC raising the loading spec (Helios’s 7,000 lb sits inside the

8,000 lb limit); the remaining challenges are existing halls and the single-width rack that may exceed 4,000 kg in 2028. These may require moving power and storage out of the rack (sidecar, SST) or changing materials.

  1. Weight therefore becomes a new design constraint for new halls in 2027-28, adds content for mechanical parts and

sidecar structures near term, and only later becomes a factor in cold-plate and manifold materials.

Quantum computing: hybrid configurations are likely to remain the norm through 2030

  1. The five quantum sessions pointed to GPU+QPU+CPU hybrid configurations through 2030, with panelists not expecting a

quantum computer to fit into a 3U rack within the next 5-10 years.

  1. Superconducting (IQM): more than 25 systems sold, physical qubits from 150 (2025) to 40,000 (2030), fault tolerance

after 2030, using NVQLink for real-time QEC decoding.

  1. Photonic (Xanadu): room-temperature operation is a differentiator; logical qubits from 200 (2029) to 1,000+ (2031).
  2. Quantum computing’s demand for conventional data center hardware concentrates in cryo, FPGA/GPU decoders and

microsecond-latency NICs. Our conclusion is that there is currently no material, quantifiable impact on the supply chain we track; if that changes, we think it first shows up in demand for GPUs, FPGAs and low-latency NICs.

Conversations with Investors

Investors at the show mostly asked about two things, neocloud supplier selection and what neoclouds actually need, and the real deployment of 800 VDC and ±400 V.

Neocloud supplier selection and what they need

  1. We noticed that some investors are interested in the OEM versus ODM business model and how the two compete in the AI

server generation.

  1. At the show we met CoreWeave’s head of AI infrastructure and senior executives from other neoclouds, and talked to

the sales side of the ODMs:

a. Neoclouds are likely to continue buying end-to-end solutions from OEMs.

b. ODM strength is in manufacturing and design, with less capability in combining hardware and software, which aligns with neoclouds’ needs.

c. What neoclouds care about most is time-to-market, diversity of compute facilities and the service that follows. We therefore think OEMs are likely to retain a competitive edge over ODMs in both enterprise and neocloud, while the ODM market concentrates further on hyperscalers.

Investors are still asking about real deployment of 800 VDC and ±400 V

Investors at OCP remain focused on the actual deployment timing of HVDC. Our impression after the show: ±400 V is on track to deploy in 2027, and NVIDIA’s 800 V is likely to reach volume after 2H27, consistent with our earlier channel checks.
