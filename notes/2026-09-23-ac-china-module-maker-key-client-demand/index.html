Within the current 800G and 1.6T bill of materials, which component faces the most acute shortage? What is driving it?

Currently, the tightest component is the DSP chip. Silicon photonics chips have at least three viable suppliers, and CW sources have over five, whereas DSP capacity is significantly tighter. There are two core drivers: First, global supply for optical module EDSPs is heavily concentrated, with Marvell and Broadcom together commanding an 80% market share. Second, fabrication technology poses a major bottleneck: 800G DSPs require 5nm processes, while 1.6T DSPs necessitate 3nm nodes. Currently, viable foundry options are restricted to Samsung and TSMC. Samsung's yields remain sub-optimal, funneling wafer demand almost entirely into TSMC. However, TSMC's 3nm capacity is largely consumed by Nvidia GPUs, Apple APs, and Broadcom custom ASICs (switching/accelerator chips), leaving minimal wafer allocation for 1.6T DSPs. These dynamics push DSP procurement lead times past 6 months.

Regarding NPO (Near-Packaged Optics), how does the industry evaluate external versus internal laser source architectures, and what is the trajectory moving forward?

The architectural divergence between external and internal laser sources hinges on whether the laser emitter is physically decoupled from the primary module chassis. For example, in a 3.2T transmission design, integrating the laser, photodetectors, and optical modems onto a unified module constitutes an internal architecture. Currently, given the delicate trade-offs between thermal dissipation and power density, the architecture we are co-developing and deploying with clients is an external ELSFP (External Laser Source Small Form-factor Pluggable) form factor. This decouples the laser from the optical engine to the front faceplate of the chassis, while leaving the photodetectors and modems seated on the host board. In parallel, the company is developing integrated internal laser solutions targeted for release in 2H2027. Both architectures are currently progressing concurrently, and it remains premature to declare a definitive winner. Over the long run, however, if integrated internal modules successfully resolve heat dissipation and power consumption constraints, their cost and integration advantages could make them the primary architecture.

Is it accurate to assume that in NPO designs, if thermal and power hurdles are cleared, the industry will favor internal laser designs, falling back to external configurations only if those challenges persist?

That is an accurate assessment. The core engineering calculus determining whether to use external or internal configurations centers squarely on power consumption and thermal dissipation.

It has been noted that internal architectures can curb power consumption by leveraging lower-power CW silicon photonics chips (e.g., 120–150mW). Is this technical approach viable?

Yes, driving down power draw is the prerequisite for deploying internal laser sources. This necessitates core material breakthroughs, such as swapping standard silicon wafers for novel substrates like indium phosphide; if such technologies mature and successfully lower overall thermal dissipation, the commercial feasibility of internal architectures will rise substantially.

Given that current NPO projects with North American clients deploy external laser sources, how are competing suppliers approaching this architectural choice?

NPO technology remains in an early stage of development, and the industry has yet to coalesce around a unified standard. Consequently, individual vendors are executing based on their internal R&D capabilities and client-specific co-design requests, developing external and internal architectures simultaneously. Vendors are actively shipping samples to clients, and which architecture ultimately dominates will become clear only after transceivers clear qualification and translate into volume production orders, guided by client adoption. This contrasts with 1.6T transceivers, where the architectural baseline is largely established, despite ongoing segmentation between standard versus silicon photonics designs and DSP-based versus LPO architectures.

Across the optical module PCB supply chain, which fabricators supply 1.6T mSAP substrates, and are there capacity bottlenecks?

The company maintains established relationships with several top-tier PCB fabricators, including Shennan Circuits, Wus Printed Circuit, and Kinwong Electronic. At present, domestic PCB manufacturing capacity is more than adequate. Although transceivers impose exacting technical specifications on PCBs, the physical panel sizes are compact, consuming a negligible fraction of overall fabricator capacity. So long as fabricators possess the required process know-how, production capacity is not a constraint. Accordingly, the PCB supply chain faces zero bottlenecks.

Beyond DSP chips, are there other latent material bottlenecks across the optical transceiver supply chain?

Beyond DSP chips, silicon photonics dies also carry supply chain risk. If silicon photonics foundry capacity remains constrained, shortages could readily emerge.
