SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion assessed NaV1.7 as a genetically supported pain target and Xenon’s development of XEN1701. People lacking functional NaV1.7 experience profound pain insensitivity, but previous inhibitors have not delivered commercially meaningful analgesia. The expert believes inadequate drug exposure within human nerves, rather than invalid target biology, has likely been the principal obstacle.

Uncertainty remains over whether pharmacological inhibition can reproduce congenital pain insensitivity, which may involve developmental changes and loss of peripheral nerve terminals.

Key Insights

Tissue penetration is the critical success factor. Potency and selectivity matter, but compounds must cross nerve barriers and reach pain-sensing fibers at sufficient free concentrations. The expert strongly rejected the theory that increased endogenous opioid activity primarily explains congenital pain insensitivity, citing evidence that the relevant molecular changes occur outside pain-sensing neurons. Dental pain may be the best initial proof-of-concept setting because Pfizer’s earlier compound showed a limited signal there despite apparently poor exposure. NaV1.8 may be more important in highly activated, depolarized neurons during acute postoperative pain. However, NaV1.7 could ultimately match or exceed NaV1.8 efficacy. Combining NaV1.7 and NaV1.8 inhibition could theoretically provide additive benefit, but this requires clinical testing. Xenon’s modeled receptor-occupancy claim was viewed cautiously. Models may not adequately reflect body-temperature potency, protein binding, or penetration into human nerve fibers. Central nervous system exposure could improve efficacy at spinal nerve terminals, but the necessity of central penetration remains unproven.
