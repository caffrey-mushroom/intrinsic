US Midterm Impact: How the Blue Wave Hits the Datacenter Buildout: AI Trade Down but Not

Out

Author: Doug OLaughlin, Dylan Patel, Daniel Nishball, Myron Xie, Jeremie Eliahou Ontiveros, Clara

Ee, Nigel Chiang, Zane Fong, Oliver Kennon, Reyk Knuhtsen, Sagar Kumathe, Jiwon Jeong, Minjae

Kang, Nicolas Bontigui, Ellie Holbrook, Julien Martin-Prin, Sherry Zhang

Date: Wednesday, November 4, 2026 (published 2026/11/04 23:04:52)

Executive Summary

Positive: Gigascalers, Neoclouds with live or permitted capacity, ASICs, long-cycle power and

industrials, enterprise networking and consumer semis, which gain from higher prices, cheaper

memory or less AI-build exposure.

Mixed: PCBs and substrates, foundry and debt markets, where slower volumes are partly offset by

tight supply, non-US demand or stronger credit.

Negative: anything tied to new US builds – delayed Texas projects, US-only industrials, Nvidia,

memory, commodity optics and WFE – as fewer GW get built.

Our estimates and opinions will change rapidly, and we will update you as new information comes

in. Expect updates for every model in the coming weeks.

Midterm Recap

The blue wave has happened. Democrats won both chambers of Congress and flipped the governorships

of Texas, Georgia, Ohio and Iowa, with candidates in both parties running against datacenters as the

cause of rising power bills. Our datacenter team has already flagged ~5.8 GW of capacity due in 2027-

1H28, ~90% of it in Texas, where key permits are missing or suspended. We are quickly moving from a

potential oversupply scenario to a tighter supply scenario.

We think that this lengthens the cycle and could thus be bullish for the AI complex over the medium-

term. Lower out-year GW expectations also lower the ARR the AI ecosystem needs to generate to pay

for the buildout.

Gigascalers: Higher Returns, FCF Turns Positive Earlier

We expect Gigascalers to benefit as supply additions slow. Rising GPU prices lift returns on existing

capacity, and fewer new builds mean more bargaining power with hardware suppliers. They also have

the lowest cost of capital, which keeps them at the bottom of the cost curve. Slower capex should swing

their FCF from meaningfully negative to positive over the next 24 months.

Exposure varies by company. Share of capacity in Texas at YE27 / YE28 / YE29 (the tenant if leased,

otherwise the owner):

Oracle: 30% / 28% / 28%, the highest, though most are already under construction.

Google: 22% / 17% / 15%, with 47% of its 2027 adds in Texas, though more than half of those are

under construction.

Meta: 13% / 17% / 17%, with 75% of Texas adds still Planned.

Microsoft: 10% / 12% / 11%.

Amazon: 3% / 6% / 9%, the lowest.

Among the labs, Anthropic is the most exposed, 25-30% of its capacity and 35-40% of annual adds in

Texas. OpenAI is 31% at YE27 but only 3% of its 2029 adds.

We see Amazon as the best positioned Gigascaler, with the least Texas exposure and a Texas pipeline

(92% still Planned) early enough to redirect.

Neoclouds: Less Supply Lifts Rental Prices, More Differentiation

We think Neoclouds outside Texas, Georgia, Ohio and Iowa, the four states where Democrats flipped the

governorship, are among the better-placed parts of the AI complex.

A more hostile permitting environment slows supply additions, which means less competition for capital

and higher GPU rental prices.

GPU rental prices have risen even with supply additions expected, and delays should speed up those

increases. The biggest beneficiaries have capacity already deployed, exposure to contract and on-

demand pricing, a low cost of capital, and little dependence on new projects in those four states, Texas

above all.

Datacenter Capacity at Risk

As a non-exhaustive first pass, our datacenter team reviewed permits at the most important US sites and

flagged ~5.8 GW of capacity due in 2027-1H28 where a key permit is missing or suspended (4.3 GW in

2027, 1.5 GW in 1H28). See the Datacenter Model team’s note for details.

Several takeaways:

Neoclouds and their developers carry ~4.6 GW, or four-fifths.

~5.2 GW is in Texas, where the most common gap is the air permit for on-site generation.

Tenants of the delayed capacity include Microsoft, Anthropic and OpenAI, adding to demand for

capacity that is already live or permitted.

Impact of Lower Supply on Neoclouds and Rental Market Dynamics

Less compute coming online pushes contract prices up and gives bargaining power to Neoclouds whose

capacity is not at risk. We expect buyers to sign longer terms at higher prices to secure supply.

Neoclouds with contracts up for renewal gain the most, since they can reprice existing capacity.

Our preferred name is Nebius (NBIS). None of its ~1.8 GW of 2027-29 adds are in the flipped states,

and it rents out more capacity on short-term contracts than most Neoclouds, so it captures higher rental

prices directly.

Developers are insulated but have less upside:

APLD (~1.3 GW) and WULF (~1.2 GW have no 2027-29 adds in the flipped states and mostly

contracted pipelines. However, as landlords on long, fixed-rate leases, they gain less from higher

rents than Neoclouds do.

BITF (Keel), IRM, WYFI and TLN: smaller or less contracted, with no flipped state adds at risk.

Nscale (private) has ~94% of its adds outside the flipped states.

Most affected, based on the sites flagged so far:

SpaceX: ~3.0 GW across two Texas campuses, the largest single exposure. It can offset this by

renting capacity speculatively at $30-50M per MW per year.

Crusoe (private): ~0.9 GW across two sites, for Microsoft and Anthropic.

Nexus (private): ~0.3 GW of an Anthropic TPU campus.

CloudBurst (private), RIOT, Nscale (private) and CleanSpark: ~0.1 GW or less each.

Capital and Markets: A Slower Pace of Debt Issuance

If the ~5.8 GW our datacenter team has flagged is pushed out, ~$310-365B of capex leaves the 2027-28

build schedule. That is ~$250-280B of AI IT equipment (at $45-50M per MW for Rubin-era GPUs and

$30-33M per MW for ~0.6 GW of TPUs) and $60-85B of datacenter capex at $10-15M per MW. At

standard LTVs (75% for AI IT, 85% for datacenters), $240-285B of debt no longer needs raising on the

original timeline, or ~3-4% of the ~$7T of AI and datacenter debt we expect outstanding by end-2029.

However, the same supply cut improves the economics of the remaining capacity, so issuance can fall by

more than implied by the capex alone. Scarce capacity lifts contract prices and pushes buyers toward

higher prepays, which lets operators fund more of each build from prepays and operating cash flow and

borrow less.

The effect is sharpest for Neoclouds, which borrow for most of each build. Hyperscalers fund theirs

largely from operating cash flow, so a slower buildout shows up for them mainly as lower capex.

Neoclouds and their developers hold ~4.6 GW of the flagged capacity, two-thirds of it SpaceX’s, or

~$185-220B of debt that no longer needs financing on schedule. Neoclouds whose capacity is not at risk

also get better pricing on new debt.

China: Limited Impact – HBM Is the Swing Factor

We see limited direct impact on China’s datacenter market, where onshore operators serve Chinese

hyperscalers. China has ample power, but unmet US demand cannot move there to use it.

The channel to watch is accelerators and HBM. Softer US demand pushes suppliers to sell elsewhere.

With official sales to China restricted, and a Democratic Congress likely to tighten limits further, we

think more could reach China through indirect channels at a premium, in volumes hard to gauge. That

would speed AI compute deployment in China, where we see HBM as the binding supply constraint.

Datacenter construction in China faces no binding constraints, so better chip access would benefit

wholesale colocation operators:

GDS: one of China’s largest wholesale operators, with Alibaba and Tencent as its top customers.

VNET: moved from retail to wholesale and runs three campuses serving ByteDance, totaling 650+

MW.

Datacenter Industrials: Backlogs at Risk – Texas-Heavy, US-Only

Names Hit First

The most at-risk are industrials trading on CY27-28 backlogs built on incremental capacity adds, where

follow-on orders could slip. We rank suppliers on (i) Texas, Ohio and Georgia exposure, (ii) ex-

Americas mix and (iii) build-cycle timing.

The most affected companies are:

STRL: Texas-heavy and earliest in the datacenter build cycle, which means project

cancellations/delays will show up at Sterling first. CEC is hiring ~80 electricians a week in Texas,

and Plateau is following Meta into Texas while operating Georgia campuses.

FIX: Texas units drove ~80% of Q3 same-store backlog growth, led by its Texas electrical and

modular operations. FIX has no non-US business.

FPS: The marginal supplier in electrical equipment providing swing capacity. If CY28 slows,

overflow orders to FPS could shrink. However, highly customized engineering and design spec-ins

at hyperscaler and AI lab customers are key offsets.

VRT: Americas were 69% of Q3 sales and ~75% of segment profit at a 29.4% margin. Demand

shifting to Asia comes at lower margins (17% at APAC). We also think lead times and pricing

should normalize once capacity catches up with GW demand.

CAT, INIO, FTAI, SEI: The flipped governors want datacenters to bring their own power, which

supports BTM demand. The risk is permitting: Texas has paused new onsite-gas permits and will

likely keep them paused until the 2027 session sets the terms, delaying new Texas gas sites.

PRIM: Our permit research ties PRIM to gas generation at Nexus’ Hubbard, TX campus, where

only the first phase is permitted.

Best relative performers will be long-cycle EPCs and power OEMs

BE: Non-combustion BE is better placed than the turbine and engine manufacturers, fuel cells

emit almost no NOx, so they need far less of the emissions control and air modeling that slow

turbine and engine permits in the states still issuing them.

PWR: We estimate two-thirds or more of the business is non-datacenter, with the largest piece

being transmission and distribution EPC work driven by long cycle tailwinds such as grid

hardening and high-voltage transmission upgrades.

GEV, ENR, MHI: Datacenters account for only ~25-30% of their backlog, with the remainder

coming from utilities due to coal plant retirements and replacement demand (from gas plants put

in place in the 90s and early 2000s). They also have service/aftermarket tails that are driven by the

size of the installed base instead of net GW adds.

Schneider, ABB: The cleanest geographic hedges, with only ~40% of Q3 revenue from North

America at Schneider and the Americas at ABB. Schneider’s East Asia growth came from

datacenter projects in Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia, and ABB is buying LV leadership in

Japan through its Mitsubishi Electric JV.

ASICs vs Nvidia: GPUs Get Cut First – ASIC Suppliers Hold Up

Better

Among the frontier labs’ compute preferences, Nvidia is the marginal supplier. Both Anthropic and

OpenAI will prioritize their ASIC compute. For Anthropic, TPUs remain its most favored source of

compute, and it has committed to these GWs with Broadcom. While Anthropic has leased a lot of GPU-

based compute, this is due to limited availability of their other preferred sources of compute (TPU and

Trainium). OpenAI will prioritize the ramp of its Jalapeno and second-gen Serrano ASICs, though

availability is much lower, so OpenAI is still far more GPU-dependent than Anthropic.

In short, we see a cut to total GW deployed disproportionately affecting Nvidia rather than ASIC

suppliers like Broadcom.

We believe that key AMD customers will try to keep their AMD commitments given AMD GPUs are

being offered at a very low effective price through the warrant deals with Meta and OpenAI. While

Anthropic doesn’t enjoy a warrant deal we believe that pricing is still favorable.

However, AMD would be at risk if MI455X delivers meaningfully worse perf/W than competing

platforms, which can only be determined through real world testing. For instance, we note that the lack

of silicon co-design in Helios results in higher power, such as from the re-timed backplane, which would

be unfavorable to AMD in a more power constrained environment.

Cerebras

Recently, we published our latest views on Cerebras for Accelerator Model subscribers. To recap, we

believe that OpenAI continues to have difficulty deploying its newer models on Cerebras hardware

economically and is unlikely to upsize the Cerebras compute deal to 2GW. In addition, we see risk to the

existing 750MW commitment. As the burden is on Cerebras to find datacenter capacity to deliver this

compute to OpenAI, a much tighter datacenter supply backdrop will make it harder for Cerebras to

deliver on its compute obligations, and therefore easier for OpenAI to exit the commitment.

PCBs and Substrates: Shortage Thesis Softens – ABF Stays Tight,

Just Less So

A slower AI buildout weakens the shortage thesis for PCBs and substrates. Our prior work had rising

accelerator volumes and more PCB and substrate content per system outrunning supply, which supported

higher shipments and sustained price increases. Delaying the datacenters that would absorb those

systems cuts the demand behind both.

In our June ABF deep dive, we showed high-end ABF substrate demand outgrowing accelerator

shipments, since packages grow larger and harder to yield with each generation. We now see 2027

package shipments up ~106% year on year and effective ABF area up ~136%, with supply covering only

~58% of demand.

For ABF area demand to stay flat in 2027, package shipments would have to come in ~58% below the

current roadmap, or 13% below 2026, since each package uses ~15% more ABF area. A realistic cut

lands nowhere near that, so ABF demand keeps growing. Every point off the unit roadmap narrows the

shortage, though, which is negative for substrates.

With the GW cuts above, we expect the ABF shortage to ease while staying tight. After a year of double-

digit increases at Unimicron and Nan Ya PCB, with Kinsus talking about 10-15% a month, we expect

fewer and smaller price increases from here. Nvidia-skewed suppliers (Ibiden, Unimicron) should take

more of the hit than TPU, AMD and ASIC suppliers (AT&S, SEMCO).

PCB demand has its own growth drivers. In our October note, Return of the Midplane: SpaceX’s

“Kessel” Vera Rubin NVL72, we flagged SpaceX’s custom Nvidia server architecture for orbital

deployments, which adds upside if terrestrial datacenters face constraints. We think SpaceX chose a PCB

midplane for the scale-up domain because cable cartridges handle launch pressure and temperature

swings poorly, and a midplane is easier to service. It is specced with EM-898K4, an M10-grade CCL

with 2nd-gen Q-glass, and EMC is the sole CCL vendor given its space track record.

HDI is also spreading to compute boards beyond Nvidia, as we flagged in High Density Interconnect,

High Demand Incoming in August. We expect PCB designs and CCL materials to converge on HDI from

2027 as signal and power densities rise.

Memory: The Curve Shifts Hard – HBM Takes the First Hit

A datacenter slowdown would hurt memory, though less than GPUs and ASICs. HBM is tied to

accelerator shipments and takes the first hit, falling roughly in line with GPUs, and server DRAM and

enterprise SSD demand weaken with it.

DRAM has a cushion, since high prices have held back demand outside the datacenter. Consumer

devices cut DRAM content, robotics and autonomous vehicles capped it, and general-purpose servers

were de-specced, and all of it can recover as prices fall. Suppliers can also shift HBM wafers back to

commodity DRAM (cDRAM), and with most of the slowdown landing in 2028, they have almost a year

to cut capex.

Prices would still fall in any of our scenarios. Each HBM wafer moved back yields 3.5-4.0x the cDRAM

bits, more than a return to normal content in phones, PCs and tablets could absorb. LTAs cushion

covered volumes, and we estimate contract price floors still support ~50% gross margins.

NAND has less cushion. Its upcycle ran on enterprise SSD demand from AI inference storage and HDD

shortages. Consumer NAND is less held back by price, so lower ASPs recover fewer bits, and the

supply-demand gap is shallower, so even minor server delays hit ASPs harder. SSD controllers benefit,

since cheaper NAND brings back consumer SSD units, which is what controller vendors sell on.

Overall, HBM and NAND take the direct hit, with cDRAM following. We are most negative on NAND,

where YMTC supply could add pressure. LTAs should prevent an inventory build-up, though suppliers

may lose some bargaining power.

Networking: Flight to Safety – High-Beta Optical Plays Take the

Hit

Networking stocks have split. The highest-beta names sit in relatively commoditized interconnect stocks,

while larger, steadier companies with enterprise exposure have underperformed An AI datacenter

slowdown would hit the well-known AI optical names hardest, and we expect investors to rotate toward

businesses with broader enterprise exposure. Capital moving from optics to memory, with little rebound

in optics, limits the downside from here.

We are most negative on higher-beta commodity plays:

TSEM, SSUMY, FUWAY: These Asian optical suppliers would be hurt the most. LTAs on

commoditized products limit their ASP gains even under medium-term supply constraints, and

large PIC wafer and laser capacity comes online from 2028, just before those LTAs roll off.

LITE: The UHP laser and Google OCS stories are well understood, and many investors exited in

1H26. China is set to add laser capacity, and LITE’s UHP lead is at risk with YJ testing 300mW

lasers and Dongshan qualifying at Nvidia. NPO lowers the bar for laser qualification, which adds

to that risk, and the Marvell deal, at a fixed 4.5 MRVL shares per LITE share, could cap medium-

term upside.

MXL, AAOI: We expect these tier-2 optical names to keep underperforming. A later rebound

could be compelling, given MXL’s exposure to a DSP shortage in China through end-2027 and

AAOI’s large pending DWDM laser orders.

We are concerned about businesses that could be squeezed on both revenue and costs:

CIEN: Large backlogs and shortages persisting into 2028 risk a demand overhang. Demand could

also soften on slower Texas long-haul expansion, made worse by ERCOT’s freeze on new large-

load (≥75MW) approvals, which would have supported inter-campus cluster buildouts. On costs,

CIEN is exposed to price increases on pump lasers (distinct from datacom UHP lasers) and

component shortages through at least 2027.

We are positive on safer businesses with meaningful enterprise exposure:

CSCO: Wins in scale-across optics and Meta’s frontend network, with AI only a low-double-digit

share of revenue (FY27 guide). Enterprise AI adoption could drive more deployments and a

hardware refresh over the longer term.

NTAP: Its large enterprise customer base insulates it. AI buildouts have driven database and

storage upgrades, but any revenue slowdown would arrive well after hardware peers’.

ANET: A TSMC 3nm shortage earlier this year capped revenue upside, and consistent beats and

attractive margins provide downside protection if the buildout slows. Longer term, we expect

Ethernet scale-up switching to take share from NVLink, driven by inference workloads and XPU

diversity, much as Ethernet displaced InfiniBand in scale-out. ALAB should benefit too, but we

favor ANET as the more direct play

Foundry & WFE: Tools Get Deferred – Fab Shells Keep Building

A sustained datacenter slowdown would hit WFE harder than foundry revenue, since foundries can defer

tool purchases while still building subsidized shells. The 35% investment tax credit (ITC) requires

construction to start before end-2026, with no extension so far, which could pull some groundbreakings

into this quarter without changing tool orders.

The slowdown would mostly hit 2028 foundry capacity, and with 6-12-month tool lead times, we expect

order cuts from 2H27. Tariffs, export licensing and CHIPS enforcement likely stay in place until 2029

regardless of the midterms, and tighter China rules would add downside for tool makers.

Memory exposure drives most of the risk:

Lam: 46% memory exposure (QSep26) puts NAND upgrades, and possibly a greenfield cycle, at

risk.

Applied Materials: 35-40% share in DRAM equipment (ex-litho). HBM wafers converted to

commodity DRAM yield 3-4x the bits, which could delay greenfield DRAM fabs and TSV tools,

and ICAPS growth only partly offsets that.

KLA: guided to 60% of CY27 process control revenue from memory.

Besi, Hanmi, Camtek, Onto, Advantest: exposed to weaker HBM and CoWoS units.

MKS, ICHOR: US subsystem suppliers, likely to feel it first.

TEL, SCREEN: exposed through DRAM.

China’s domestic WFE is insulated given limited US exposure, and tighter China rules could speed

localization at NAURA, AMEC and Piotech.

Among foundries, TSMC should hold demand, since datacenters that move outside the US still need its

chips, but a slower Arizona buildout beyond Fab 2 puts our $102B 2028 capex estimate at risk. Intel is

mixed: Foundry’s 2027 capex is at risk, while EMIB, client and server should hold up. Terafab could

face scrutiny over further incentives. Tower is the most exposed foundry, with SiPho reaching 70% of

revenue by 2030, and GlobalFoundries’ interposer and SiPho growth could slow. SMIC, UMC, Hua

Hong and VIS are relative winners given limited US exposure.

Consumer Semis: Cheaper Memory – PC OEMs Win

If datacenter delays free up memory bits and push prices down, the beneficiaries are the consumer

device makers and chip vendors squeezed during the shortage. PC OEMs gain most directly. HP said

memory reached ~35% of notebook BOM in July, double the prior quarter, so lower prices flow straight

into margins. Higher memory costs also pushed up end-product prices and built pent-up demand, so

OEMs such as Dell and HP should see both lower costs and higher shipments.

Chip vendors whose revenue tracks units, such as Qualcomm and MediaTek, should follow. We see the

most upside at MediaTek, since its low- and mid-range smartphones took the biggest hit from memory

costs and should recover fastest. Component suppliers such as Synaptics and Cirrus Logic gain on

volume, but limited pricing power with customers caps the benefit.
