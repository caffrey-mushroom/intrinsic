THE SIGNAL

  • Huang says NVIDIA content per gigawatt rose from about $18B to $20B-plus to $40B-plus (CPUs, networking, storage, security) and should keep rising, while power scarcity pushes buyers toward tokens-per-watt leaders, so share is increasing.
  • Huang puts NVIDIA at about 40% of CSP capex but about 80% of non-CSP capex (on-prem, neoclouds), each roughly half of revenue; about a third of the business is non-AI classical compute growing 30-40% a year.
  • Licensing math: about 20M GPUs shipped a year, roughly half to enterprises, at a couple thousand dollars per GPU per year implies about $20B of recurring revenue per annual cohort, compounding with the installed base (our read).
  • Huang says Elon Musk’s buildout is now counted as a CSP rather than enterprise and targets 10GW by end-2027 with a million-plus Vera Rubins; Gerstner notes 2027 consensus moved from flat to +74%, yet the stock rose only about 20%.

01 Compute scaling and rising intelligence demand

Huang frames rising compute demand as logically inevitable: reasoning and multi-step planning require vastly more inference than simple memorized regurgitation, and as models get better at productive tasks, usage compounds further. He cites an OpenAI effort using 10,000 agents (per co-host Clark Tang’s estimate, costing roughly $4 million in compute) to solve a Navier-Stokes problem, arguing such breakthroughs are now reachable because decades of prior scientific groundwork had narrowed the remaining search space. Huang expects the cost of intelligence to fall at a Moore’s-Law-like pace even as usage keeps rising, noting the original GPT cost millions of dollars to train versus around $100 today — evidence, in his view, of an insatiable and compounding appetite for intelligence.

02 Physical AI and robotics

Huang says NVIDIA’s “physical AI” business — robotics, self-driving, medical imaging, industrial sensing — generated $10 billion in the first six months of this year alone, matching its full-year total for last year, suggesting his earlier public estimate of a $100 billion opportunity was understated. He describes a three-computer stack (training system, Omniverse-based digital twin, and the embedded/robot computer) as NVIDIA’s unique full-stack advantage across every physical industry — automotive, logistics (FedEx), retail (Walmart), telecom (AT&T, Verizon), and healthcare.

03 Safety, regulation and the White House accord

Huang argues capability and safety cannot be separated, comparing them to a car’s braking and safety systems rather than independent features, and describes NVIDIA’s safety approach as modeled on raising children: start with zero-trust containment, expand access as confidence grows, and maintain out-of-band monitoring that can escalate or shut systems down. He and Gerstner discuss the White House superintelligence accord and a “three-legged stool” of US AI governance: frontier-model review by the Commerce Department, the accord itself (audits, board oversight, liability), and an “SI force” led by Jay Clayton. Huang says the industry’s leading labs, including Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, per Gerstner’s account, are aligned on moving fast and safely at the same time, not racing recklessly.

04 2027 bottlenecks and build-out risk

Both hosts and Huang discuss an estimate attributed to SemiAnalysis’s Dylan Patel of 43 gigawatts of new AI compute coming online next year — more than exists in the world today — with another roughly 40GW the year after. Huang says demand is there and then some, but identifies land, power, permitting, construction labor, and substrate/packaging manufacturing as key constraints. He notes NVIDIA recently announced 2 gigawatts in Australia (roughly an $80 billion deal) and argues NVIDIA’s global reach across every cloud, OEM, and region makes its business more resilient to any single country’s bottlenecks than competitors’. He also discusses Elon Musk’s SpaceX compute buildout and the jointly developed orbital-server concept.

05 Payback math and the NVIDIA moat

Addressing bubble fears, Gerstner lays out math showing current lab revenue (roughly $200 billion combined across frontier and inference labs) needs to roughly double annually to justify trillions in infrastructure spend, and Huang responds that it is unnecessary to extrapolate any single lab to multi-trillion-dollar revenue, since two-thirds of build-out serves non-lab demand. He argues NVIDIA’s fungibility — the same hardware serving training, inference, and agentic workloads for any customer or geography — lowers financing risk compared to specialized chips. He also notes giant customer order books exceed what NVIDIA can currently supply, that new orders arrive constantly, and that NVIDIA’s relationship with TSMC underpins its ability to plan five years ahead.

06 New business models and closing optimism

Huang describes two emerging growth vectors beyond hardware sales: software licensing (potentially thousands of dollars per GPU per year across tens of millions of shipped units) and revenue-share arrangements with clouds NVIDIA backs as an investment and offtake partner. He notes buybacks now exceed 50% of free cash flow (60-some percent last quarter) and that investments will shrink as a share of cash generation because opportunities like OpenAI and Anthropic won’t come around again. Closing, Huang says he is “99.99%” confident the next decade will be net positive, attributing doomer narratives to underestimating human capacity and the amount of good work happening independent of any single actor.

THE SIGNAL

  • Brockman argues security spend should vastly exceed software spend as AI makes software cheap; hosts put security at low single digits of IT budgets today (our read: security TAM expands, application software deflates).
  • Brockman frames security posture as fundamentals plus how much AI cyber capability defenders use, requiring a new vulnerability sweep each model release; he cites model-driven South Korean bank hacks (our read: recurring frontier-model consumption).
  • The hosts note GPT-6 rolled out to over a billion people; it uses adaptive reasoning that answers quickly, then keeps thinking; Brockman calls bringing memory to free users a huge engineering effort, showing free-tier compute cost shapes product decisions.
  • Brockman says clinical-trial regulatory filings take about a year to write and AI drafts them far faster; he sees near-term wins in N-of-1 diseases where existing cures sit unnoticed in published literature.

01 Math as proof of new knowledge

Brockman, drawing on his own math background, says the ~400 resolved problems aren’t just machine-checked brute force (he contrasts this with the historically “incomprehensible” four-color theorem proof) but contain genuine insights that domain experts are finding surprising. He frames this as a milestone showing AI can now meaningfully accelerate scientific discovery, though he stops short of claiming AI is yet generating whole new theories. Host Sarah Guo raises the adjacent challenge of communicating advanced results to non-specialists; Brockman ties this to alignment techniques OpenAI has discussed since 2017 — debate and iterative amplification — where one AI checks another’s claims so humans can verify outputs (like book summaries) without needing to verify the full underlying work themselves.

02 Biology and the drug-discovery pipeline

Brockman argues there’s no first-principles reason neural nets should be better at natural language than at biology, suggesting language success implies biology should eventually “crack” too, though data scarcity (unlike math/physics) is the key bottleneck. Elad Gil notes drug development often takes some combination of ~15 years and roughly $1.5B, split between costly preclinical/mouse studies and even costlier clinical trials; Brockman argues AI can compress non-biological bottlenecks first — regulatory filings being a concrete example he says AI can already draft faster. He expects ~80% of what’s possible to arrive quickly, while deeper bottlenecks (data generation, wet-lab innovation) persist, and suggests regulatory regimes (comparing US, Australia) will need to adapt once neural nets can be shown to outperform traditional trials on predicting efficacy or toxicity — analogizing to how self-driving car regulation would need to shift if AI driving proved far safer than humans.

03 Dots and the end of UI

Brockman describes his own always-on assistant Dots as proactively surfacing undone tasks, calendar conflicts, and inbox/Slack context without being asked — he calls it a change in “form factor,” not capability, built on an Astra-level model with its own cloud computer. He argues consumer and enterprise assistants will converge, with the main remaining difference being compute cost (free-tier mass consumer products vs. “peak intelligence” workplace tools), and notes OpenAI’s unique position of combining 1.2 billion ChatGPT consumers with a leading enterprise/coding business. On interface design, Brockman argues the best interface is no interface at all, pointing to GPT-6’s ability to generate bespoke, on-the-fly interfaces (e.g., for flight booking or bike assembly) rather than fixed menus — though he concedes enterprise settings still need admin controls and toggles that complicate the no-UI vision.

04 Reframing cybersecurity’s physics

Brockman says the internet was built for resilience, not security, with protections “bolted on” after early worms and viruses — and argues AI can now let organizations pay down that accumulated tech debt via techniques like formal verification of software, previously too expensive to apply broadly. He describes OpenAI’s internal security sprint (25% of engineers reassigned, peak model used to find and fix vulnerabilities until issues were “saturated”) as a repeatable process needed each model generation, alongside training models to write secure code from the start. Externally, OpenAI has made a $1 billion commitment to help frontline defenders get access to its models, gives defenders access to frontier models through its Daybreak program, and is partnering with firms like CrowdStrike on coordinated response — part of Brockman’s broader claim that the current “defender’s window” won’t last and spend priorities must shift accordingly.

05 Human agency as the throughline

Brockman closes by restating OpenAI’s mission — ensuring AGI benefits all of humanity, with humans firmly in control even as models surpass human capability in specific domains. He invokes the story of Steve Jobs adding a handle to the iMac as a metaphor for giving humans a felt sense of control, and frames the company’s push toward agentic tools (Dots, math/bio applications, no-UI design) and security investment as all serving this same underlying goal of amplifying human agency rather than replacing it.

THE SIGNAL

  • Maia 300 targets 7x token throughput per rack and 2.5x performance per watt over Maia 200, with multi-gigawatt Maia deployments starting 2027; the rack doubles to 96 accelerators and rack-level HBM bandwidth rises 5x.
  • Dighe says HBM is a binding constraint in Microsoft’s fleet today; chip power goes from 750W to 950W while bandwidth grows 2.5x, so memory per accelerator outpaces compute (our read: positive for HBM4 demand).
  • One scale-up domain spans 128 racks: copper inside each rack, optics between switch tiers, all on standard Ethernet; Dighe expects more scale-up optics systems next year (our read: demand for merchant Ethernet switches and optics).
  • Maia is tuned for long-context, memory-bound decode, with other systems in a mixed fleet covering the rest; Dighe says agent-written kernels allow day-one model support.

01 Why Microsoft builds custom silicon

Dighe frames Microsoft’s chip efforts — Maia for AI, Cobalt for compute, Azure Boost for networking, Azure DPU for storage — as a systems-level philosophy rather than standalone chip projects, driven by Azure’s planet-scale visibility into customer workload requirements. Maia’s lineage started with Maia 100 targeting speech and convolutional neural network workloads pre-LLM, then pivoted to an inference-first bet with Maia 200 — a deliberate contrarian call at a time when industry attention centered on training. Maia 200 is already in production, reportedly outperforming some GPUs in Microsoft’s fleet, which Dighe cites as the evidence justifying the leap to Maia 300’s multi-gigawatt scaling plan.

02 The agentic workload shift and data-flow architecture

Dighe says workloads have moved from chat-based interactions to agents running long tasks, generating more tokens per interaction, more persistent KV cache as context grows, and a higher premium on memory bandwidth and capacity as core design priorities. Microsoft’s response is a software-defined data flow architecture (introduced in Maia 200, extended in Maia 300): data movement patterns are planned ahead of time, data is pinned to distributed on-die SRAM scratchpads, and communication and computation are overlapped. The chip uses a NUMA-based layout so data sits near the compute consuming it, and the same paradigm extends off-chip through specialized DMA engines into networking and rack-scale design, treating the network as a direct extension of that same data-flow approach rather than a bolt-on.

03 Memory architecture and chiplet trade-offs

The jump from six HBM3E stacks to eight HBM4 stacks, paired with a TSMC N2P chiplet design, delivers roughly 2.5x bandwidth per chip (to 18 TB/s) and a ~35-37% FLOPs increase, for only a roughly 200W power increase. Rather than splitting into two compute dies, Microsoft kept a single large compute die and used a chiplet-based memory interface die to extend the shoreline on both sides, fitting four HBM stacks per side while reserving N2P for compute only (not memory) to balance cost and power. Lower-precision MXFP4-class arithmetic is used to compress model weights and growing KV cache in HBM, with FP4-by-FP8 compute preserving output quality while raising achievable concurrency.

04 Prefill/decode and networking topology

Microsoft runs disaggregated prefill and decode instances across its fleet, matched based on measured prefill-to-decode ratios, within the same scale-up fabric so KV transfer avoids a slower scale-out network. The topology is two-tiered: tightly coupled Maia accelerators communicate directly via integrated, power-efficient NICs within a node (optimized for tensor parallelism), all 96 accelerators in a rack connect through a first-tier switch over copper, and a second tier of switches using optics links racks into super-pods of up to 12,288 accelerators. Microsoft uses standard Ethernet switches and cables to benefit from industry scale economics, layering its own transport protocol on top; Dighe says the industry will eventually move from near-package optics (NPO) toward co-packaged optics (CPO) for scale-up, starting with NPO for reliability and serviceability.

05 Power, cooling, and deployment flexibility

The 96-accelerator rack draws 167 kilowatts, and Microsoft designed Maia to run in both liquid-cooled and air-cooled data centers via a custom heat-exchanger unit that cools warm water with ambient air, letting racks hit full power capability even without native liquid-cooling plumbing — a decision Dighe says must be architected upfront, not retrofitted. Deployment locations are driven by internal product-team demand signals (Copilot, Foundry, GitHub Copilot), validated via shadow traffic before full regional rollout. On the software side, kernel-level optimizations are abstracted from internal customers entirely; agentic kernel optimization lets Microsoft continuously generate and refine optimized kernels without burdening application teams, and systems can be tuned at runtime for different latency/throughput service classes depending on workload type, such as meeting summarization versus low-latency agentic loops.

01 The AI trade’s selloff

The show opens debating why AI-adjacent stocks have sold off even as the Dow rises. Gerstner attributes it to a mix of factors — questions over whether AI revenue justifies CapEx, rising rates, and open-source commoditization fears — but frames the chop as healthy consolidation that avoids bubbles rather than a trend reversal. Josh Brown argues the week’s data revealed rates have been rising largely because of a glut of corporate bonds funding data-center buildouts, not just inflation, evidenced by rates coming in on signs of slower CapEx even amid hot CPI/PPI prints, while non-AI sectors (healthcare, staples, housing) rallied. Stephanie Link says she’s buying tech dips while staying diversified into the rest of the market, citing strong retail sales and jobless claims data, and argues healthy cash-generating semis (NVIDIA, Broadcom, Micron) are unfairly caught up in debt-driven selling aimed at over-levered names like CoreWeave ($50B debt) and Oracle ($167B debt). Malcolm Etheridge frames the moment as a possible shift toward companies that deploy AI efficiently rather than build it, citing Brian Moynihan’s comments on roughly $700M in expected AI-driven savings at his bank starting next year.

02 Anthropic’s investor day and Q3 numbers

Gerstner, an early Anthropic investor, attended the prior day’s investor meeting and says he cannot disclose what was said in the room, but describes the mood among attendees as a nine or ten out of ten on a one-to-three-year horizon — contrasting with a negative market reaction to the numbers that leaked. Kate Rooney reports Anthropic told investors Q3 revenue was just shy of $17 billion, up 45% sequentially (versus 140% sequential growth earlier in the year), with 80%+ adjusted profit margins; analysts like DA Davidson characterized the reaction as negative given sky-high expectations, and Anthropic is reportedly targeting an IPO before Thanksgiving at a floated $2 trillion valuation. Gerstner suggests the sequential slowdown partly reflects labs deliberately throttling distillation — large-scale querying by competitors, mostly Chinese, used to copy model capability — which he frames as a national security issue Dario Amodei is prioritizing, potentially normalizing an otherwise weaker quarter.

03 Open-source competition and run-rate metrics

Asked about a post by Gavin Baker suggesting frontier labs cut prices because open-weight models are taking share, Gerstner says each model generation produces more intelligence per unit of compute, letting labs choose to harvest margin or cut prices competitively (as AWS did for a decade) — while frontier-level intelligence will still command premium pricing even as lower-tier models like Haiku and Sonnet compete with open source on price; he adds there is little daylight between them, since Baker remains positive on OpenAI and Anthropic. He also addresses criticism, including from Baker, that labs’ run-rate revenue disclosures are misleading snapshots, agreeing they shouldn’t be released on a public-company monthly cadence but arguing deeper metrics — customer additions, Fortune 500/2000 penetration — tell a stronger story.

04 CoreWeave and the data-center debt debate

Gerstner confirms Altimeter no longer owns CoreWeave, having diversified out of AI broadly, but defends its management team and disputes narratives about its cost of capital, noting the stock is still roughly double its IPO price near $60 despite falling from an AI-multiple peak near $180. He says he would buy CoreWeave today, attributing the selloff to markets reassessing it as a GPU-rental business rather than an AI-multiple name, and notes some pre-IPO ventures funded by VCs and public markets are being washed out.

05 AI safety, regulation, and China

Responding to Satya Nadella’s post calling for a human-controlled mechanism to stop models behaving unexpectedly, Gerstner says there’s less disagreement among lab leaders than Twitter suggests, citing a recent White House meeting with Nadella, Jensen Huang, Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, Dario Amodei and Greg Brockman. He describes a three-part U.S. oversight structure: Commerce Department frontier-model review (which he says led to pulling a model called Mythos from production), the White House accord on superintelligence (Sarbanes-Oxley-style internal/external audits), and an oversight body led by Jay Clayton covering critical infrastructure and cyber — arguing this refutes senators’ claims that no regulatory work is underway. On China, Gerstner responds to Alexander Wang’s comment downplaying China as a geopolitical boogeyman by saying he favors trusting but verifying, noting President Xi and President Trump have now met twice this year with plans for two more meetings, with AI featured in talks between the countries’ top economic officials.

06 Snowflake’s agentic enterprise pivot

In the second half, Snowflake CEO Sridhar Ramaswamy says AI has moved from a novelty chatbot to agentic systems that can reason over enterprise data and take actions, which he calls the agentic enterprise driving Snowflake’s recent results. He argues software creation has become industrialized — mass-producible rather than hand-crafted — favoring data platforms that can reason over information in plain English, citing an internal data team with limited coding ability now delivering major impact across sales and finance. Asked why a CIO would choose Snowflake over Databricks, Ramaswamy cites tight integration, automatic enforcement of data access permissions within agents, and enterprise-grade readiness (disaster recovery, permissions), pointing to customer Kraft Heinz moving from biweekly to real-time competitive intelligence. On M&A, he says the bar for large acquisitions has risen because of integration difficulty, so Snowflake favors small, non-distracting tuck-ins like its recent acquisition of Natoma (which brings MCP technology connecting agents to enterprise applications), alongside heavy in-house R&D investment. He also announces an expanded partnership with Lumen Technologies to build a private, secure data lane letting enterprises move on-premises data into Snowflake without touching the public internet. Gerstner, who no longer holds Snowflake in his hedge fund but calls it one of his largest personal holdings, and Josh Brown and Stephanie Link, both of whom own the stock, press Ramaswamy on these points during the segment.
