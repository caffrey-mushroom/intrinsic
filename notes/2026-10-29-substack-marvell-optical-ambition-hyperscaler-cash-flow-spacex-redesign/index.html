LITE and MRVL

This was not on my bingo card.

But Marvell could become a powerhouse if it buys Lumentum.

I know I would want to own it if this comes together.

The short term could be rough, though. Marvell would be buying a very large business after a huge run in optics, and how much it pays will shape the returns for its own shareholders.

Bloomberg and Reuters report that the talks are advanced, with an announcement possible as early as next week. We are still working with a reported deal rather than an agreed price. (read this)

My estimate is somewhere around $1,200–$1,300 for LITE. That is the range I have in mind, and the strategic value is a big reason for it.

Think about what Marvell has been assembling. It helps hyperscalers design custom chips. It sells the technology connecting those chips to the rest of the system. Celestial AI brought in optical connections for processors and memory. Lumentum would bring the lasers and manufacturing behind those connections, along with optical switching.

Marvell and Lumentum have already demonstrated OCS together, so there is an existing technical relationship to build on.

The long-term ambition is big. Real big. From what I can imagine.

As these systems get larger, moving data increasingly becomes an enormous part of making the whole thing useful. A faster processor accomplishes less if it spends too much time waiting for data.

Power used moving that data also becomes power that could have gone toward computing.

Marvell wants to be involved when the customer designs the system, then sell more of the components that make it work.

Its investor-day model puts FY2031 interconnect revenue at roughly $37.5B at the midpoint, ahead of the roughly $30B target for custom silicon. Those are management’s long-term targets from before these acquisition reports.

Connectivity is central to where Marvell wants to go.

Owning Lumentum would give Marvell more influence over the product roadmap and the capacity supporting it. It could design the electronics and light source together, decide where to expand manufacturing, and earn more from each connection.

It would also have a deeper relationship with the customer. Replacing a supplier that helped design several parts of your AI system is a much bigger project than replacing one component.

And there is a time advantage. Building the manufacturing capability from scratch takes years. Buying Lumentum would bring an operating business, engineers who know how to make the products, and customers already planning around its output.

That brings me to the supply question.

My first reaction was to think about NVIDIA and everyone else buying from Lumentum. Marvell would gain a lot more control over an important supplier.

Existing commitments are part of the business it would be acquiring. NVIDIA has a multibillion-dollar purchase agreement and rights to future Lumentum capacity, alongside its separate $2B investment. Lumentum’s Wupen Yuen described its ultra-high-power laser arrangement as including volume, pricing and take-or-pay commitments. Customers have already put money and contractual commitments behind their demand.

The next product cycle is where I would expect customers to start thinking differently. If you compete with Marvell, you have another reason to secure supply somewhere else. You may also prefer to discuss your next design with an independent supplier rather than share that roadmap with a business competing against you elsewhere.

That is bullish for Coherent in my view. I would expect Marvell’s competitors to become more motivated to lock up supply with suppliers outside Lumentum.

Coherent is an obvious place to look. NVIDIA already has its own nonexclusive purchase and future-capacity agreement with Coherent, plus a $2B investment. The commercial relationship is already there. A Lumentum acquisition could give customers an extra reason to expand their commitments to Coherent and involve it earlier in future designs.

NVIDIA itself has also invested $2B in Marvell and partnered with it on NVLink Fusion and photonics. These businesses can work together in one area and compete in another. Marvell would have a strong incentive to keep selling to NVIDIA, while NVIDIA would have a strong incentive to keep multiple supply relationships healthy.

I also think a big premium for LITE would be bullish for Coherent and AAOI just by proxy.

If Marvell pays a large premium for Lumentum, a major strategic buyer is putting a price on the technology, manufacturing and customer relationships in optics.

That gives investors a real acquisition benchmark to work from.

It makes the replacement cost of these assets more visible. It also raises the value of having another independent supplier available after one of the largest has been acquired.

I would read a strong premium as bullish for both COHR and AAOI. The size of the benefit will depend on how much of that strategic value each business owns.

Coherent has a broader optical platform. AAOI gives us a different combination of laser manufacturing and transceivers.

That is why I want to separate AAOI’s laser capability from the finished modules when thinking about what someone might pay for it.

AAOI manufactures lasers. It can sell laser products externally or use them in the transceivers assembled within its own operation (as far as we know they want to keep their lasers internal for now). Making that component itself gives AAOI a manufacturing capability with value both inside its own modules and as a product sold to other customers.

So one thing worth asking is could AAOI keep the laser business and sell the transceiver operation?

In theory, yes. AAOI has even pursued a version of that structure before. In 2022 it agreed to sell its Chinese manufacturing facilities and certain transceiver assets to Yuhan while retaining Taiwan and Texas. The plan included continuing to supply lasers to the buyer’s transceiver business. AAOI terminated the transaction in September 2023 after citing the buyer’s breach of obligations. The sale never closed, but the proposed structure shows that management has already considered separating the parts of the business while keeping a supply relationship between them.

This is why I would look at AAOI in pieces.

A buyer could want the laser manufacturing and keep supplying independent module makers. AAOI could also explore a separation itself and retain a laser supply agreement with whoever owned the transceiver business. These are hypothetical ways to structure it. The existing customers and the way the factories supply each other would have to fit into the plan.

For a chip company, that could be pretty attractive. It gets a light source for its own optical products and a business selling lasers to others. A more focused laser supplier could serve customers who compete in finished modules, which may make those customers more comfortable putting business with it. Meanwhile, the transceiver operation has its own value through its hyperscaler relationships and ongoing product ramps.

AAOI is developing products that extend beyond conventional pluggable transceivers too as we know. It demonstrated an external laser source for CPO. That gives the laser operation a route into another part of the market as system designs change.

here would be important customer discussions around any transaction. AAOI’s 2023 disclosure of its Microsoft agreement describes a right of first refusal for certain acquisition offers and a change-of-control termination right in the laser arrangement. Amazon’s March 2025 agreement includes advance notice and a process to review and potentially negotiate over certain transactions. Those relationships would help determine the structure a buyer could put together.

Coming back to LITE, I think $1,200–$1,300 would put a serious value on what Marvell is buying. The cash-and-stock mix would then decide how much of that value LITE holders take out and how much stays invested in the combined business.

A simple half-cash, half-stock example would give a LITE holder $600–$650 in cash and the same initial value in Marvell shares at my estimated offer range. That is an illustration, with the eventual agreement determining how the stock portion is priced. For someone who wants to keep exposure to this industry, the Marvell shares could become a very interesting part of the offer.

Marvell has about $3.9B of cash and $5B of borrowings in its latest quarterly filing. A purchase of this scale would be a major financing decision. More debt would mean more interest expense. More stock would mean the future profits are shared among more owners. That helps explain why the acquirer’s stock could struggle while investors work out what they would earn per share after the deal.

I think the short term could be rough, and I would want exposure to the business Marvell is trying to build if this deal comes together. If Marvell brings its silicon, optical connections and Lumentum’s manufacturing together successfully, it could earn from a much larger portion of each AI deployment. And a premium paid for LITE would give the rest of the optical sector a concrete example of what a strategic buyer is willing to pay to own these capabilities.

AI spending and cash flow

The capex timelines

I feel confident about the supplier opportunity in 2027 after these calls.

Google expects another significant capex increase. Microsoft has moved some deliveries into early 2027 and expects spending to accelerate in the second half of its fiscal year. Oracle’s first forecast annual decline comes in FY29, which starts in June 2028. We still have a substantial amount of equipment to buy and install before reaching the period Oracle is talking about.

Meta also raised the bottom of its 2026 spending range to $135B–$145B from $130B–$145B, including finance-lease principal payments. It plans to give a numerical 2027 outlook with Q4 results.

That gives the infrastructure businesses time to keep growing. It also gives the hyperscalers more capacity to earn from.

The reason I want exposure to both is that the market can start valuing the future cash-flow improvement well before capex reaches its peak. Microsoft is already getting more revenue out of equipment it has installed. Oracle has put dates around when it expects its buildout to fund itself.

If those developments become more visible over the next few quarters, investors could become more willing to own the operators while the suppliers are still reporting strong growth.

We have to make that judgment business by business.

Google is investing heavily in Cloud while changing the economics of Search.

Microsoft has stronger current evidence of utilization gains.

Oracle’s plan depends on delivering a large amount of new capacity and collecting the revenue attached to it.

Their valuations will determine how much of those future benefits we are already paying for.

Supplier selection becomes more important as the architecture changes too because a customer can spend more overall and direct that money toward a different product. The SpaceX discussion we will get to soon gets into exactly that. More optical content can mean a larger opportunity for one supplier and a lost copper connection for another.

If spending keeps growing through 2027 and the hyperscalers start converting more of it into cash, there can be a period when both sides work.

The eventual rotation is about where the next improvement in earnings and cash flow is coming from, and how much the market has already priced into each stock.

The stocks can move before the spending does

This is the part I am keeping on my brain during all of these calls. I can feel good about the amount of equipment these companies will buy in 2027 and still want less money exposed to the suppliers in the long run.

The share price is already looking beyond the next few quarters.

Oracle has now given us an actual point in the calendar when it expects annual capex to start declining. FY29 begins in June 2028.

Google and Microsoft are still pointing to strong spending in 2027, so there is a lot of business between here and there.

But the market can start changing what it will pay for supplier earnings as soon as it gets a clearer picture of the years after that.

The first reduction in a valuation multiple can arrive while revenue and profit are still growing.

Imagine a supplier with a great year ahead. If investors become more confident that its earnings will keep compounding for another five years, they may pay more for each dollar of those earnings. If they start expecting growth to flatten after two years, they may pay less. The company can deliver exactly the strong quarter everyone expected and the stock can still struggle because the argument has moved further out.

That is why I am taking some profits and raising cash across the portfolio whenever we get a good few days. I still like the company theses. I want more breathing room in the portfolio, especially when so much of it benefits from the same spending cycle. Several different tickers can end up reacting to the same change in customer expectations.

Micron in 2018 is a useful example of how far apart the stock and the reported results can get. Its fiscal-year high closing price came in the quarter ending May, at $62.62. The following quarter’s highest close was lower, even as the company went on to report $8.44B of revenue and $3.53 of adjusted EPS for the August quarter, both above the preceding quarter. Those September results capped a record fiscal year. The stock had already moved ahead of the financial statements.

The useful lesson is to think about what earnings could look like after the strongest reported quarter. Trying to turn that one episode into a countdown for optics would give us a false sense of precision. The products and customer commitments today deserve their own analysis.

And the commitments have become quite interesting. SK hynix says signed LTAs cover more than 50% of its total production capacity for 2027 and 2028, across 14 major customers. It describes pricing that can move with market conditions inside an agreed range. That gives it a committed base of business while leaving part of its capacity exposed to future demand and pricing.

Micron says it has signed 26 multi-year take-or-pay strategic customer agreements, estimated to represent more than 35% of its revenue through 2030. About three-quarters of that estimated agreement revenue has a defined pricing framework, mostly with floors and ceilings. Pricing on the remaining quarter is negotiated periodically. These are management’s estimates of future revenue coverage, and the distinction between committed volume and the price earned on that volume matters.

That could support a more durable earnings base than folks typically associate with an old memory cycle. A customer committing to buy and pay for supply gives a manufacturer more confidence when building capacity. A pricing floor can also protect part of the return if market prices weaken. The rest of the business still moves with demand, product mix and the cost of bringing new factories online.

For optics, I want to understand the same economics through the actual agreements those companies have signed. Lumentum’s commitments discussed above give us evidence of customers reserving supply. The memory disclosures show how much more informative it becomes when we can see the volume coverage and pricing structure. Each supplier’s terms determine how much future profit is protected.

So I am holding two things in my head here.

The spending outlook gives these businesses a substantial runway.

The stocks can still get ahead of the earnings that runway eventually produces.

Raising cash lets me keep exposure to the opportunities I like while having more room if expectations reset.

Oracle’s timeline is one reason to keep looking further out, and the stronger customer commitments are one reason to spend that time company by company.

ORCL

Oracle clearly wanted investors to leave this presentation feeling better about how the build gets funded. A lot of time went into that, and I think it was worth doing. The revenue opportunity is huge. So is the amount of money Oracle has to put out before that revenue arrives.

The part that stood out to me was the $20B. Management expects up to another $20B of capital-market funding through FY30, currently split roughly evenly between equity and debt. It already completed a separate $20B equity program in the first quarter.

That puts a number around something that had felt much more open-ended.

There is now a date attached to the other side of the buildout. Oracle expects positive free cash flow in the second half of FY28, which is December 2027 through May 2028. It then targets at least $30B in FY29, starting June 2028, when it expects to fund growth internally.

Annual capex is supposed to decline from FY29 after the peak spending period across FY27 and FY28. FY28 could still be higher than FY27 as more capacity comes online.

So the cash-flow improvement starts while Oracle is still spending heavily. Revenue from the earlier construction starts catching up first. Lower capex adds another benefit later. If that plays out, shareholders could see a very different business financially by the time the spending cycle rolls over.

The financing examples help explain how Oracle expects to get there. Take the 1GW project it walked through. The illustration called for about $37B of capex in the first two years. With vendor financing, the cash Oracle needs during those two years falls to about $18B. The hardware supplier gets paid later, giving Oracle time to start collecting from the customer. If the customer brings its own hardware, the initial cash requirement can fall to $6B or less. Oracle earns a different amount of revenue in that arrangement, with a different margin profile.

That is a pretty big difference in how much cash Oracle has to find upfront.

It also gives Oracle more than one way to get a project built. A customer with its own hardware can use Oracle for the rest of the infrastructure. A vendor can help finance the equipment. A customer can pay ahead of receiving the service. Oracle said the financing mix for its additional contracted 7GW was largely solidified. These arrangements move some of the funding burden to the other businesses involved in the deployment.

We can already see how much customer funding contributes. Oracle received $11.4B of customer prepayments with a significant financing component in its latest quarter. That helped produce $23.1B of operating cash flow against $28.5B of capex, leaving negative $5.4B of reported free cash flow.

Strip out just those prepayments and the arithmetic is roughly negative $16.8B of free cash flow. That is my calculation. It shows the scale of the funding Oracle is getting from customers before the services are delivered.

I can see why customers would agree to this. If they need the capacity badly enough, helping fund it can get them closer to having it available. For Oracle, receiving the cash earlier makes the construction period easier to finance. Those particular prepayments accrue interest at a rate generally consistent with Oracle’s borrowing rate, so there is a price for getting the cash early. The capacity eventually has to earn enough to cover that financing and the cost of delivering the service.

The longer-term exposure is in the commitments behind the projects. Oracle’s filing describes data-center leases that will begin over the next few years and run for 15 to 19 years. A customer contract can be shorter. If that customer leaves or runs into financial trouble, Oracle could still have a long schedule of payments attached to the building and equipment.

That makes the customer itself part of the investment case. A financially strong customer with a durable need for the capacity gives Oracle a much better chance of collecting over the life of the project. If a contract ends early, Oracle’s ability to fill that capacity with other customers becomes part of protecting the return.

I came away with a clearer path through the expensive part of the cycle. Oracle had already discussed prepayments and vendor financing in September. This presentation added the multi-year funding plan and the dates when it expects cash generation to turn. That gives me a much better idea of how long shareholders are being asked to wait.

If Oracle gets the capacity into service, customers keep paying and revenue ramps as planned, then rising cash flow and eventually lower capex could work together in a big way. The market can start valuing that outcome before June 2028. Each completed site and each quarter of collected revenue makes the path easier to believe.

I want more ORCL shares.

GOOG

Wow, that was rough.

If there’s one major area that concerns me here, it’s Search monetization.

Search still generates about half of Alphabet’s revenue. It brought in $63.35B this quarter, growing 12% YoY after 17% in Q2. Management said currency was the biggest contributor to the slowdown. It also mentioned the end of the World Cup boost and changes affecting retail in Asia. Then there was the comment about AI Mode earning less revenue from its usage.

That is a direct acknowledgment that the transition is affecting growth.

Google is changing the experience at the center of its most important business, and the new experience is still developing commercially. Existing searches moving into a format that earns less can weigh on revenue even while people use Google more.

There is a reason to keep investing in the experience. Pichai says most AI Mode queries are incremental and roughly three times longer than traditional searches. If people start using Google for more complicated tasks and return more often, Google has more activity to monetize. The opportunity gets bigger if the product expands what users ask it to do.

The question is how quickly the money catches up with that usage.

That can have an enormous effect on Alphabet because of Search’s size. A small change in the economics of such a large business can outweigh a lot of growth elsewhere. Google has room to improve how it places ads and captures commercial intent in AI Mode. Shareholders are funding that transition at the same time as a much larger infrastructure build.

Cloud is where demand is showing up most clearly. Revenue reached $30.7B, up 103% YoY, with a 36.2% operating margin. Those are strong numbers.

Then look at what happened across Alphabet. Cloud added about $2.3B of operating profit compared with Q2. Alphabet-level costs increased by about $1.9B, largely from shared AI research and development, and Services profit fell slightly. Total operating profit ended up a little lower QoQ even though revenue rose about 6.5%.

A lot of Cloud’s additional earnings are being absorbed by spending elsewhere in Alphabet. The central cost bucket includes other expenses too, but shared AI development is part of what makes the Cloud business possible. That spending ultimately comes out of what shareholders keep. Cloud’s segment margin is useful, but Alphabet’s total profit is the number that captures the wider cost of supporting this growth.

The investment loss explains some of the headline EPS miss. Google reported $2.12 and said equity investment losses reduced EPS by $0.55. Add that back and it gets to $2.67, against the $3.02 adjusted consensus reported by Reuters. There was still an earnings shortfall after that adjustment.

Cloud’s revenue mix is changing as well. TPU system sales contributed materially to the growth. Google now sells complete systems alongside the services running on its own infrastructure. A system delivery can produce a large amount of revenue at once. A cloud customer keeps generating revenue as it consumes services. Both can be valuable, but the timing and cash requirements are different. A bigger share of system deliveries could make quarterly growth lumpier as customers take delivery of large orders.

Management said Google Cloud Platform still accounts for the vast majority of Cloud revenue and most of the backlog. It left the growth rate excluding TPU sales and the TPU margins undisclosed. The two revenue streams could grow at very different rates, and the combined number makes the recurring growth harder to judge.

Selling those systems also ties up cash in components and inventory ahead of delivery. The call linked the inventory to signed orders. Google is preparing to fulfill customer demand, and the cash goes out before that demand turns into collected revenue.

Operating cash flow was $42.3B against $51.4B of capex, leaving about negative $9.1B of free cash flow. The $5.2B European Commission fine was paid in the quarter. Adding that payment back still leaves roughly negative $3.9B by my calculation.

This is an expensive transition.

Google raised $28.7B of senior debt and received $12.4B from its market share-sale program during Q3. The stated purpose of that equity program was funding employee-equity tax obligations.

And spending is heading higher into year-end. Google kept its $195B–$205B annual capex range. Subtracting what it spent through September implies roughly $63B–$73B in Q4, about 23%–42% more than Q3. That is my arithmetic from the annual range. Expensive third-party capacity also arrived near the end of Q3, so more of that cost hits Cloud’s margin in Q4.

The third-party cost pressure and significant capex growth in 2027 were already discussed in July. The latest call reinforced both. For the suppliers, there is a lot of spending and a larger TPU ramp ahead. For Alphabet, the payoff depends on turning that demand into more profit across the whole business while improving the economics of AI Mode.

If Search monetization improves while Cloud keeps expanding, the earnings potential is substantial. Right now, the fastest-growing part of Alphabet is funding a lot of additional costs, and its biggest revenue source is working through a product transition. That combination explains why such strong Cloud growth can still leave shareholders disappointed with the quarter.

All in all, quite disappointed with this quarter and think this drop is justified. I still believe in the long term vision of GOOG. But think its going to take time.

MSFT

They are just crushing it.

Microsoft is getting more out of the equipment it already has. That was the part of this call I liked most for the hyperscaler thesis.

Azure grew 48% in constant currency, beating the revised 44%–45% guide. The comparable prior-quarter rate is 42% under Microsoft’s updated definition of Azure. Revenue reached $33.2B, up about 13% QoQ. So the acceleration is meaningful even after lining up the comparisons.

Management talked about improving GPU efficiency, putting more existing CPU capacity to work and earning more revenue per token. More work is running through the infrastructure.

Think about the cost already sitting in a data center. The building and equipment are there. Better software or scheduling can get more useful work out of that same fleet. More customer usage then spreads those costs over more revenue. The value of that engineering belongs to the operator. It can make room for additional workloads before another full round of equipment purchases. That helps explain why utilization can improve the economics well before capex reaches its peak.

This is why the hyperscaler and infrastructure theses can work at the same time. Suppliers get the next orders while Microsoft earns more from earlier purchases.

The next-quarter guide calls for Azure growth of 49%–50% in constant currency. Cloud gross margin is expected around 65%, down from 66% in Q1 as the expansion continues. Demand is strong enough to support another acceleration in the revenue guide, with the cost of serving that demand also rising.

The cash flow shows how much Microsoft is putting back into the business. It generated $58B of operating cash flow and spent $39.4B on cash purchases of property and equipment, roughly twice the year-ago amount. That leaves $18.6B of free cash flow, down from $25.7B a year earlier.

There is more cash going toward the build than that conventional calculation captures. Other investing outflows were $12.9B, up from $6.2B, with the increase primarily related to facilitating component purchases. The line includes other items too, but the disclosure shows why the amount available after the infrastructure ramp can be smaller than operating cash flow less property and equipment suggests.

The lower reported capex number also needs to be understood in the context of the delivery schedule. Microsoft spent $45.2B including finance leases after expecting more than $50B. Some third-party deliveries moved later. It now expects about $170B for calendar 2026, more than $50B next quarter and an acceleration in spending during January through June. The equipment is arriving later, and the spending follows it.

An earlier accounting change lowered the reported spending outlook too. Microsoft extended the useful life of data-center and office buildings from 15 to 25 years. It expected a small earnings benefit, and more future leases would consequently be classified as operating leases. Those sit outside Microsoft’s headline capex measure. Its underlying investment expectations stayed the same when it made that change.

The lease commitments give us another view of how far ahead it is building. Commitments for leases that had yet to begin increased during the quarter and will start over several years, through FY34. Microsoft is arranging capacity well beyond the equipment already in service.

Meanwhile, depreciation reached $11.1B, up from $7.1B a year earlier. More equipment entering service means more expense running through the income statement. Better utilization becomes increasingly valuable because Microsoft has to earn against a growing installed base.

OpenAI is a big part of that business. The filing shows $10B of commercial revenue from OpenAI, roughly 11% of Microsoft’s total, and $7.2B in related receivables. Microsoft also owns approximately 24% of OpenAI. The relationship gives Microsoft several ways to participate in its growth, with cloud revenue and investment exposure tied to the same customer.

The software side was more mixed. Microsoft 365 commercial seat growth slowed to 5%. Management described weaker execution in some traditional product sales, alongside strong GitHub consumption. Copilot usage and revenue are growing, but the call left the paid-seat count undisclosed. The strongest growth is coming from customers using more, while adding seats has become a slower business.

Across Microsoft, operating income grew about 21% on revenue growth of about 19%. That is operating leverage while the buildout is still underway. Reported EPS grew 32%, helped by an easier comparison for OpenAI investment effects. EPS growth excluding those effects was 18%.

If Microsoft keeps increasing the amount of work its infrastructure handles and the services customers buy from it, that installed base can support a larger stream of earnings. We already have evidence of that happening. If those earnings eventually grow faster than the cash required for expansion, more of that progress reaches shareholders as free cash flow. This quarter gives me more confidence in Microsoft’s ability to earn from the infrastructure it is building.

SpaceX

Edgewater Research has raised the possibility of xAI moving its Vera Rubin deployment toward optics. Irrational Analysis discussed the note in its October 24 post, Market Memo: Dancing in the Rain.

A separate October 25 post from Jukan (@jukan05) gave more detail. SpaceX had reportedly changed the layout, creating cable runs beyond 10 meters. It was now considering pluggable optical modules and active optical cables for connections that had originally been intended for 1.6T AECs.

For Credo, that could mean losing some planned copper cable sales just as its own optical cable is getting closer to shipping. The larger distance changes which products can serve the connection and how the spending is divided among suppliers.

I’m using Irrational Analysis’s public memo for the Edgewater attribution. The original Edgewater note’s title and full text remain unavailable. The layout, longer-than-10-meter and roughly 6,000-rack details come from Jukan’s separate October 25 post. Their attribution to Edgewater and SpaceX’s final design remain unverified.

AECs send data through copper, with chips inside the cable helping maintain the signal. Optical modules and active optical cables use fiber. As the distance between the equipment grows, optics gives the customer more reach.

Jukan’s post described roughly 6,000 Rubin racks targeted for the end of Q2 2027. Musk’s own October 20 call gave a target of 440,000 Vera Rubin computers by then. At that scale, changing the cable design could move a meaningful amount of business between suppliers.

The longer connections outside the rack are the ones we’re discussing here. NVIDIA still uses a passive copper NVLink backplane inside its published Rubin NVL72 design. Both copper and optics have a role in the cluster.

CRDO

Can Credo make more from the optical connection than it was making from the copper cable?

That is the question I raised back in September, when I asked whether optical revenue and gross profit could grow faster than the AEC business Credo eventually loses as optics moves into shorter connections. This SpaceX report gives us a specific deployment to follow. My earlier CRDO discussion

Credo already has a relationship with xAI. Its October 2024 AEC announcement included a network engineer assisting xAI describing the cables’ role in building large GPU clusters. So Credo comes into this with experience supplying the customer.

The new product is the ZeroFlap active light cable. Credo is targeting up to 50 meters of reach and expects to start shipping in 2027. It uses many lower-speed optical channels, with spare channels designed to take over when one weakens. Credo is trying to bring the reliability of its AECs to a longer connection using light.

With an AEC, Credo sells the finished cable. If the customer moves to optical modules built by somebody else, Credo might supply chips or other components inside them. To compare the two properly, we would need to add up Credo’s content at both ends of the optical link. That is the equivalent of the complete cable it was selling before.

Then I would look at the gross-profit dollars. A finished cable carries the cost of its components, assembly and testing. A chip has a different cost base. Even if a chip had a higher margin percentage, the smaller amount of revenue could leave Credo with fewer dollars of gross profit from that connection.

This is where the volume matters. A smaller optical position can still become a good business if Credo wins enough connections. It would need more volume to replace a larger amount of profit earned from each AEC. We need to know both how much Credo earns on the product and how many it can sell.

The ALC gives Credo a way to stay involved in the whole connection. Credo would be selling its own optical cable, taking responsibility for the components and the reliability of the finished product. That gives it more scope to preserve what it earns as the customer moves from copper to fiber.

It also takes on the cost of making that complete product. The selling price, the cost of the optical parts and the efficiency of production would determine how much reaches gross profit. A higher-priced optical cable could be valuable for Credo, but I would want to know how much of the extra price covers extra hardware.

Some optical sales could be entirely new business. A longer connection that Credo’s copper cable could never serve gives the ALC another place to compete. In that case, there is no existing Credo cable profit to replace. Across a growing cluster, the AEC business could continue expanding while the new optical business adds a second source of growth.

The SpaceX timing makes this more immediate. Credo says ALC shipments begin in 2027. The reported cluster target is the end of Q2. SpaceX would need to select products, qualify them and arrange supply before the finished cluster is running.

Credo needs to be ready during that purchasing window. If another optical product is already qualified and available, it could get the initial orders even if Credo’s cable becomes competitive later in the year. Credo could still win subsequent deployments or expansion, but the first wave would already have gone elsewhere.

Earnings updates on BBAI ZURA and NXPI

A few other portfolio updates are worth getting into before I wrap this up.

BBAI

I’m continuing to hold BBAI and give this a few more quarters. The Physical AI thesis is still early. We have more work coming in, and now I want to see that work turn into better margins and less cash going out the door.

The applications fit what we have been talking about. Management described more digital-twin and simulation work in shipyards, manufacturing, airports and supply chains, plus a two-year renewal with Children’s Minnesota. This is software that lets a customer model a physical operation and work out how to run it better. BBAI still gives us limited detail on how much revenue comes from each application.

Revenue grew 31% YoY to $43.6M, with much of that increase coming from Ask Sage, acquired in December 2025. There is progress in the existing business too. Management reported organic growth of 18.6% QoQ after 6.7% in Q2.

Since Q2, BBAI has announced more than 25 awards worth over $225M, including major recompetes. Backlog grew 22% QoQ to $330M, and the 2026 revenue outlook rose to $150M–$165M, about 5% higher at the midpoint.

Only about 23% of backlog was funded. Some of the announced value includes options and contract ceilings, so there is still a step between winning the award and collecting the revenue. That makes the rate at which those customers fund the work important for the next few quarters.

  • Gross margin was 31.2%, up from 22.4% a year ago. It has come down sequentially from 34.0% in Q1 and 32.8% in Q2.- The adjusted EBITDA loss narrowed to $9.1M from $11.6M in Q2.- SG&A grew about 24% to $31.3M, slower than revenue growth. It still represented roughly 72% of sales.- Operating cash outflow was $9.55M, almost unchanged from $9.59M a year earlier.

The detail that gave me more pause was the $76.7M goodwill impairment inside the $91.9M net loss. The 10-Q says BBAI lowered its long-term forecasts, which triggered the review. This year’s revenue guidance went up while the longer-term outlook used to value the business came down. Goodwill is part of what BBAI paid for acquisitions. The cash was spent earlier. The write-down says the updated outlook supports less of the value sitting on the balance sheet.

The valuation put equal weight on projected earnings and cash flows and on market comparisons, with expected EBITDA growth among the inputs. After the write-down, the estimated value of the reporting unit was equal to its carrying value. Another reduction in the forecast would be starting from there.

More revenue this year can coexist with lower expectations further out. Profit margins and the timing of future cash flows also affect that valuation. The filing leaves the detailed bridge between the old and new forecasts open.

It also leaves the charge unattributed to a particular acquisition or product. The disclosed issue is the weaker long-term forecast for the reporting unit as a whole.

The margin trend is why I am being patient about the financial improvement. Gross margin has declined each quarter this year, and operating cash outflow is almost unchanged from a year ago. If new work comes in at better margins, revenue growth will do much more to cover the expense base.

Almost $400M in cash and investments gives BBAI room to fund the current losses, with around $17M of convertible debt due in December.

Management also wants to make acquisitions. BBAI describes itself as an applications and platform business, with less capital tied up in infrastructure than a data-center operator. A lot of the spending decision therefore comes down to which businesses it buys and what it pays. After this write-down, I would give that decision as much attention as the next contract announcement.

The organic growth and backlog are enough for me to keep giving the thesis time. If that growth starts coming through with a better gross margin and lower cash burn, we will have a stronger business developing underneath the Physical AI story.

Not adding shares. Just hanging on for now.

ZURA

The useful ZURA update for me is the funding going into the next results. Wedbush kept its Outperform rating and $22 target on October 27 and said it would be an aggressive buyer below $4. The analyst points to November for results. ZURA’s own timing remains Q4.

ZURA ended September with $187.7M in cash, and management expects that to fund planned operations through at least the end of 2028. The Q3 net loss was $25.8M. On the current plan, the cash carries it through the upcoming Q4 results and the next set expected in the first half of 2027.

That works out to about $1.49 of gross cash per share before liabilities and future spending.

Wedbush’s roughly $3 downside-support estimate therefore gives value to the business beyond its cash. Both that estimate and the $22 target depend on the analyst’s assessment of the opportunity. The coming results will change that assessment, and the cash balance will keep declining as ZURA funds the work.

There is also about $114M left in its at-the-market share program, which allows it to sell shares into the market. It had used none of that program in the first nine months of 2026.

For now, ZURA has cash to reach several updates under its spending plan.

Now I wait for the data readout! Again, this is very binary and could easily drop 50% on bad data. High risk.

NXPI

NXPI has its first humanoid robotics design win. Woohoo!

The customer is an unnamed major U.S. tech business, and the design uses NXP’s S32K5 control chip. It is taking capabilities developed for vehicles and finding another place to sell them. We have the design win now, with the revenue and production timing still to come.

  • Revenue was $3.79B, up 19% YoY.- Non-GAAP EPS rose 35% to $4.20, with non-GAAP operating margin at 37.1%.- Automotive revenue reached $2.07B, up 13%. Industrial and IoT was $802M, up 39%.

The existing business is already giving us earnings growth. EPS grew much faster than revenue, and Industrial and IoT grew much faster than automotive. That gives NXP a profitable base from which to develop these newer opportunities.

Management expects AI-enabled processors to reach about 15% of Industrial and IoT processor revenue in 2026, more than double last year. The appeal is that NXP can earn more from each machine as its chips handle more of the processing and control. It can grow content even when growth in the number of machines is modest.

Management also expects annual price erosion to moderate as the chips take on more functions. That would help NXP keep more of the benefit from selling extra content. The more work the chip does for the customer, the stronger the case for defending its price.

For 2027, management expects double-digit revenue growth and non-GAAP gross margin to reach 60% during the year, helped by factory utilization, mix and efficiency. Current price increases are covering inflation. There are costs arriving alongside the growth. Capex is expected to rise to around 5% of revenue from roughly 2% this year, and NXP expects about $80M of joint-venture investee losses in 2027.

The bigger S32K5 and S32N automotive ramps are expected in 2028. NXP also expects data-center revenue to grow from over $500M this year to $1B in 2028. That gives us identifiable product ramps while the newer humanoid opportunity develops on its own schedule.

Orders were well ahead of revenue, and lead times extended. Management said customer restocking remained limited. Channel inventory was 11 weeks, flat from Q2 and up from nine weeks a year ago. That combination gives me more confidence that the order strength reflects demand beyond a simple inventory refill.

The Q4 midpoint guide is $3.85B of revenue, plus or minus $100M, and $4.36 in non-GAAP EPS. It would carry the earnings progress into another quarter ahead of the larger 2028 product ramps.

NXP generated $1.206B of non-GAAP free cash flow, or 31.9% of revenue. It added $5B to the buyback authorization, taking the available amount to about $6.3B. Actual Q3 repurchases were $103M, so the authorization leaves plenty of discretion over how quickly to use it.

Some of that cash also has to support the manufacturing buildout. Another $565M of equity contributions is committed to the Singapore joint venture through 2027. Those payments sit outside the net capex deducted in NXP’s free-cash-flow calculation, which is worth including when thinking about how much cash is available for buybacks.

If the existing business keeps improving while these newer products ramp, NXP has a way to compound earnings over several years. The humanoid win adds another future customer application to a business already generating substantial cash.
