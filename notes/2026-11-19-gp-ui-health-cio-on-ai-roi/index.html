SUMMARY

Overview

The expert, a technology leader at an academic health system, described a mixed but growing level of AI adoption. The organization is testing applications across clinical care, revenue cycle, imaging, and administrative work, with formal governance to review investments. It is balancing potential gains in care quality and efficiency against privacy, safety, workflow, and financial risks.

Key Insights

AI adoption is most likely to scale when its value is clear and measurable. Ambient documentation has gained traction because clinicians report practical benefits, and the organization has also seen increased professional revenue in outpatient care. Specialty tools may appeal to individual clinicians but fail to justify enterprise-wide spending when use is inconsistent, accuracy is uncertain, or benefits cannot be demonstrated. The organization discontinued an AI tool for detecting polyps during lower-GI procedures after finding insufficient evidence of impact. Technology alone is not enough. Vendors and healthcare organizations must redesign workflows and support staff adoption; otherwise, AI risks becoming a poorly integrated add-on. Clinical AI requires careful oversight. Human review is important, but the expert cautioned that reviewers may miss errors when AI outputs usually appear reliable. Liability standards for clinical AI also remain unsettled. The expert emphasized that scaling broad AI subscriptions is difficult without a credible productivity case, particularly in a sector with tight margins. Preferred vendors work collaboratively, support incremental investment, and help adapt processes. The moderator showed interest in the organization’s return-on-investment framework, EHR integration choices, and the challenge of making AI value measurable.
