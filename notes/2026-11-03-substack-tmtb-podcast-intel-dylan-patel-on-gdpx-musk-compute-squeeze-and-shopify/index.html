THE SIGNAL

  • Kan says xAI sold Anthropic short-term capacity this year at $40B+ per gigawatt versus the $12-15B of typical five-year contracts; Patel says Musk pulled compute from xAI’s own team to sell to Anthropic and Google.
  • Patel cites spot GPUs at about $15/hour versus roughly $4/hour TCO, and Musk’s compute funding at ~$60B cash plus a $40B raise against a $400-500B, 13GW plan (our read: resale margins are essential).
  • Patel says safety classifiers leave OpenAI and Anthropic behind Google, Meta and Chinese models on cyber and bio tasks, risking share in the very niches GDPx favors (our read).
  • Patel’s downside case: if hard-science and adversarial domains fail to monetize, existing tasks saturate, white-collar diffusion stays slow, and labs may miss their compute commitments, a key AI capex risk.

01 GDPx vs GDPval

Max Kan introduces GDPx as SemiAnalysis’s answer to what the team sees as a flawed approach in OpenAI’s GDPval, which selects evaluation domains based on their share of GDP or occupational taxonomies like SOC/ONet. Kan argues this methodology misses where AI revenue will actually concentrate: domains that can adopt new technology quickly and have high enough skill ceilings to extract continued ROI from ever-more-capable models. GDPx instead targets a narrower set — chip design, biology, cybersecurity, financial services, material science, and recursive self-improvement (RSI) — as the areas SemiAnalysis believes will absorb the bulk of frontier-model dollar spend, even if not the bulk of raw token volume.

02 Why diffusion will be slow

Dylan Patel lays out a thesis that broad white-collar AI adoption will lag expectations because companies face structural inertia — compensation structures, job definitions, and organizational habits don’t change quickly, even as model capability does. He contrasts this with domains where intelligence can be absorbed almost without limit: scientific fields far from the human frontier (material science, chip design) and adversarial fields where outcompeting rivals (finance, cybersecurity) creates continuous demand for more capable models. Patel frames this explicitly as SemiAnalysis’s house view, distinguishing it from the more commonly assumed broad-diffusion narrative, while noting the team is bullish that OpenAI, Anthropic and the other leading labs could deliver a trillion dollars of revenue within a couple of years — a timeline he jokes Kan and Megalaa will call bearish.

03 The engineering bottleneck in hard science

Pressed by a host on whether material-science spending could realistically scale given long R&D lag times, Patel argues that the dominant value pool is engineering, not discovery — citing Periodic Labs as a company focused on incrementally improving processes like material deposition rather than inventing new compounds outright. He uses concrete examples to illustrate scale: CPU substrate yields currently run 40-60% and could approach 95%; Intel’s wafer production generates roughly 100 terabytes of data per wafer that mostly goes unused because much of the scientific workforce still relies on Excel and MATLAB; and NVIDIA’s roughly two-and-a-half-year design-to-shipment cycle could compress toward a year with better simulation tooling, potentially accelerating adoption of technologies like co-packaged optics. Patel frames the primary constraint as siloed, slow-moving experimental loops rather than raw ideas or discovery itself.

04 Secrecy, benchmark results, and no human baseline

In response to a host’s question about why companies improving manufacturing efficiency with AI don’t publicize it the way software “hustler culture” does, Patel contrasts Silicon Valley’s openness with industrial firms that quietly improve products and let financial results speak for themselves. On actual results, Andrew Megalaa reports that SemiAnalysis’s PhD collaborators were surprised by how far model capability in material science had progressed, with Astra beating a published state-of-the-art benchmark result on a subtask — though the model’s overall GDPx material-science score was just 28%. Asked why GDPx omits human baselines, Megalaa explains that some tasks (like certain chip-design problems) represent work that would take teams of engineers rather than a single person, making a meaningful human comparison impractical; Patel adds that the goal is measuring when models become superhuman, not human-equivalent, since only superhuman performance will command sustained demand.

05 Musk’s compute strategy and the Goldman headline

Asked about Elon Musk’s post that Grok Bot is too expensive to run, Kan argues serving costs for personal AI agents could fall roughly 100x within a year, following the broader trend of declining intelligence costs, making current subsidization economically rational. Patel frames Musk’s broader strategy as turning SpaceX into a neocloud to fund an ambitious 13-gigawatt buildout (an estimated $400-500B), partly financed by reselling GPU capacity at a steep markup over true cost. On a headline a host said he couldn’t confirm, that Goldman is banning internal use of Claude Code and Codex, Megalaa notes that even frontier models score only about 50% on SemiAnalysis’s finance-analyst benchmark task, making open or on-prem alternatives even weaker by comparison; Patel calls such a ban shortsighted, predicting Goldman will still rely heavily on frontier tools for functions like penetration testing given security stakes.

Listen · Watch · Follow SHOP on Ethmos

THE SIGNAL

  • 74% of Q3 AI-driven purchases came from outside Shopify’s top 100 categories, showing agentic traffic leans toward the long tail, where Shopify’s fragmented merchant base is overrepresented compared with head-category catalogs (our read).
  • AI orders grew 3.3x while AI traffic grew 2.6x, implying better conversion per AI visit; Finkelstein expects this holiday season to mark AI moving from mainly discovery last year to actual conversion, so watch Q4 AI order mix.
  • Finkelstein says Shopify does not know which agent will win, so it is positioning as the neutral identity and checkout layer: Shop Pay credentials, purchase history, tracking and the Shopify catalog feeding merit-based results inside every agent.
  • Revenue grew 32% versus 29.5% GMV growth, so monetization per GMV dollar is still rising even as GMV slowed (our read); the miss came against a low-thirties guide that management says it hit.

01 Q3 numbers and the market’s reaction

Finkelstein opens by restating headline figures: GMV of $119 billion (up 29.5%), revenue of $3.7 billion (up 32%), and a free cash flow margin of 22%, which he calls rare at Shopify’s scale. He notes the company hit its own guide of low-thirties revenue growth and that big brands such as MAC, Clinique, Lululemon and Balmain joined Shopify (he later adds Estée Lauder). He also cites that since the start of 2025, Shopify has captured nearly half of all incremental US ecommerce dollars while representing only 14% of the market. Despite this, shares fell roughly 9% on what CNBC calls the company’s first revenue miss since 2022; Finkelstein dismisses the reaction as noise against what he calls a hundred-year-company scoreboard, noting it was his 46th earnings call since IPO.

02 AI channels: real but still small

Pressed by Sara Eisen on how much growth is attributable to AI commerce partnerships with OpenAI, Google, and Meta, Finkelstein says AI channels are becoming real discovery engines but remain a small fraction of total volume, with traffic up 2.6x and orders up 3.3x. He highlights that specificity is rewarded in this agentic environment — niche products like robot vacuums for pet hair or three-child car seats disproportionately benefit Shopify’s long-tail merchant base. He contrasts this with the much larger, already-scaled parts of the business: offline point-of-sale GMV just crossed $200 billion, and international is up 30%, framing Shopify as simultaneously building for an AI future while strengthening its current core.

03 Disintermediation risk and the agent “front door”

Eisen raises the disintermediation question directly, describing asking ChatGPT for a shoe rack and being routed to unfamiliar sites — and asking what happens if a consumer simply instructs an agent to purchase on their behalf, cutting out merchants’ own storefronts. Finkelstein counters that Shopify sees it the opposite way: Q3 marked personal agents going mainstream via agents including Muse and Instinct, and Shopify partnered with all of them from day one. He argues that connecting a Shop account to these agents brings three assets — shopping history for personalized recommendations, saved Shop Pay payment details, and purchase tracking — meaning the trust and identity built across Shopify’s 400 million connected shoppers travels with whichever agent a consumer uses. Carl Quintanilla extends the question to whether agents threaten legacy search rather than ecommerce broadly; Finkelstein says it still looks incremental, noting ecommerce remains under 20% of total US retail with Shopify holding 14% of that, implying a large runway regardless, and that merchant traffic from traditional search remains healthy alongside AI-driven traffic.

04 Growth deceleration and guidance

Eisen presses on GMV growth decelerating from 31.6% to 29.5% in Q3. Finkelstein reframes this as a sixth straight quarter of revenue growth in the low thirties, with Q4 guidance implying a seventh, and notes the $119 billion GMV figure represents the thirteenth consecutive quarter of GMV growth above 20%. He repeats that the company’s internal scoreboard is becoming the de facto commerce platform for top brands, pointing again to Lululemon, MAC and Estée Lauder as validation.

05 Consumer backdrop, pricing, and holiday outlook

Asked about macro pressure from rising gas prices and consumer confidence, Finkelstein says businesses are optimistic while shoppers feel some economic pressure, making each purchase more deliberate, but growth remains broad-based across merchant sizes, geographies, and channels. On whether AI-driven price comparison will force merchants into a race to the bottom, he says Shopify isn’t seeing broad price declines; instead, specific niche products — he cites “tin can” screenless phones for kids — are seeing strong agentic-driven sales by surfacing items consumers wouldn’t otherwise find. He predicts a strong holiday season and BFCM, citing broad-based strength, new-merchant growth, and healthy performance from older merchant cohorts, while staying watchful on FX and supply-chain risks.

THE SIGNAL

  • GPT-4 finished training around August 2022, 7-8 months before release, and OpenAI then bet the company on a much larger compute buildout; Altman says a GPT-3.8-level model wouldn’t have justified that bet, so capex follows capability jumps.
  • Altman says the internal ChatGPT prototype kept only about 5% of users, and most of leadership opposed launching; he shipped anyway because a small core of heavy users loved it, a useful lens for new AI products.
  • Altman now calls going without OpenAI equity a mistake: he made the choice for reasons he thought were good, but it has confused people; worth tracking alongside any future debate over his compensation (our read).
  • Altman says his hardest current problem is how fast to let progress go in what he calls the RSI era, citing governance and capability caps; enterprise products now face a near-critical-infrastructure bar for shipping.

01 Conviction without consensus

Altman traces OpenAI’s founding thesis to one of what he calls his few recurring great insights: that scaling — in companies, cities, careers, and neural networks — tends to produce superlinear returns at larger size. He says he held this intuition about neural networks years before formal scaling laws existed, and found it baffling that other researchers, even as late as 2018-2021, weren’t drawing the same conclusion despite looking at similar data. He recalls a once-persuasive counterargument — that models trained on existing data could never generate genuinely new knowledge — which he says is now understood to be wrong because models learn generalizable reasoning, not memorized facts.

He describes testing new ideas by explaining them to a smart but disciplined non-expert friend: if nobody is persuaded, he treats that as a signal to be more skeptical of himself; if everybody is persuaded, the idea was probably too obvious to be valuable. He also distinguishes genuine nonconsensus conviction from contrarianism for its own sake, saying founders who take pride in defying everyone’s advice should treat that instinct cautiously rather than as a virtue.

02 Equity, status, and being misunderstood

Altman says one of the biggest misunderstandings about him is his choice not to hold equity in OpenAI, which he now calls a mistake: he made it for what he thought were good reasons, but it has confused people. More broadly, he argues the US carries unusual skepticism toward concentrated tech power — a trait he calls partly healthy but one that, in his case, feels overcorrected. He contrasts this with India, which he says is OpenAI’s largest ChatGPT market by users, ahead of the US, and attributes greater optimism there to faster-growing, under-supplied economies having more immediate need for new products and services than developed ones.

03 Running OpenAI through internal conflict

On avoiding being surrounded by “yes men,” Altman says cultivating people willing to push back — including long-tenured friends and colleagues — takes active, ongoing effort, especially as one accumulates past wins and power. On the departures of talent who went on to found rival AI efforts (the host names Ilya, Dario and Elon), he suggests the common thread is each person concluding their own approach is the only trustworthy one given the stakes. He credits OpenAI’s survival of his firing (when roughly 700 employees threatened to resign) not to himself individually but to a rediscovery of a research-lab culture combining high conviction on mission with openness everywhere else.

04 Pluralism versus concentrated power

Altman confirms he wrote to Elon arguing it would be better if someone besides Google pursued superintelligence first, reflecting a belief in pluralism: multiple competing AI efforts reduce risk compared to either a single dominant actor or an uncontrolled handoff of power to AI systems themselves. He names two “cartoonish” failure modes — loss of human control to AI systems, and excessive power concentration in one company or country — and says he personally dislikes intense concentrations of power, extending that to a broader dislike of powerful individuals generally.

05 Founder judgment and building ambition

Drawing on his YC and investing experience (citing Stripe and Reddit as portfolio examples), Altman says he can form a strong but imperfect read on founders within 30-40 minutes, looking especially for clear communication, quick idea generation, and original thinking — though he says gauging someone’s capacity to endure pain is harder to assess directly. He argues ambition itself is teachable, something he calls one of YC’s most pleasant surprises, achieved through peer groups and exposure to successively harder challenges that build earned confidence rather than pure insecurity-driven drive.

06 Personal cost and what’s next

Altman describes losing spontaneity and ordinary freedoms — he singles out missing unstructured coffee-shop afternoons — and says his threshold for nervousness is now unusually high, with the last time he remembers being nervous coming just before OpenAI announced its Navier-Stokes result. On fears for his children’s future in an AI-shaped world, he says he trusts people broadly to adapt and that the world will most likely keep improving despite the risks he’s described. Asked what he wants to build next, he says accelerating AI-driven scientific discovery — new physics insight, medical treatments, energy sources — is what excites him most going forward.

THE SIGNAL

  • Davis says there is little competition for offtake: buyers want compute within 3-6 months and pay premiums plus big upfront deposits, while chip prices keep rising (our read: operators with live capacity gain pricing power).
  • Groq after the NVIDIA deal: instead of buying its way out of data center leases, Davis is keeping the 53MW, retrofitting some sites for liquid cooling, adding sites, and planning LPX (due next year) and Vera Rubin capacity.
  • Brokers and SPVs have tried selling Disruptive its own stock even though it never sells to third parties, so Davis suspects some secondary supply in marquee private names is made up.
  • Fund construction: about 10 checks averaging roughly $1B over 18-24 months, from a 35-40 company portfolio, with LPs pushing the size to the top of a $5-10B range.

01 The $10B thesis and market froth

Davis argues the opportunity set has narrowed to a handful of companies he calls “N of one” — he keeps a running list of 15-20 — that deserve outsized, concentrated capital even at high valuations, rather than spreading bets across thousands of venture-backed firms (he says the number of unicorns has gone parabolic since he started investing around 2011, to roughly 5,000-6,000 today). He says the firm’s historical strength has been writing large checks into companies already proven to be winning, likening it to betting on an undefeated NFL team with one game left, not picking unproven, pre-product-market-fit startups. Froth, in his telling, stems less from company behavior than from structural reality: AI startups now need massive upfront compute capital that can’t be bootstrapped, so the best companies absorb enormous private capital while staying private longer.

02 Compute costs, efficiency, and open models

Davis sees two top opportunities: the nascent US open-weights ecosystem catching up to China’s, and the compute shortage that rewards firms (NeoClouds) that know how to stand compute up. He is unconvinced compute costs will fall structurally for frontier competition, noting that claimed large efficiency gains or $10,000 benchmark-matching models don’t translate to usable products for sovereign or institutional customers, who still need custom-built, trusted systems, implying no real shortcut exists.

03 Why Disruptive built a fund now

Davis describes Disruptive’s original model — individual SPVs letting specific LPs opt into specific deals — as increasingly disfavored by top companies, who worry about smaller SPV sponsors that may slip under regulatory reporting thresholds misrepresenting whether they deployed capital, and about reputational risk from “gray area” players. He expects significant SPV-related fraud or disappointment to surface around marquee names, though he has no concrete example yet. Moving to a discretionary, named-LP fund structure, he argues, gives portfolio companies security and certainty about capital sources that SPVs can’t.

04 Palantir and Groq as case studies

On Palantir, Davis says Disruptive held the position roughly ten years pre-IPO and doubled down shortly before its COVID-era direct listing, betting that public markets would respond to Alex Karp’s distinctive, blunt leadership once outsiders got direct exposure to him — a bet he frames explicitly as a wager on a person and team rather than pure financials. On Groq, he describes the firm’s involvement predating NVIDIA’s roughly $20 billion non-exclusive licensing deal, after which much of Groq’s core team (including Jonathan) moved to NVIDIA while Disruptive retained the underlying assets, about 53 megawatts of existing data center capacity. Davis became executive chairman, repositioning Groq as an inference-focused NeoCloud that will sell offtake on NVIDIA’s upcoming LPX and Vera Rubin chip generations to hyperscalers, labs, and Disruptive’s own compute-constrained portfolio companies.

05 NeoCloud risk and defense tech prediction

Davis distinguishes “owner users” from speculative landlords, operators who take upfront deposits for compute capacity without adequate funding, technical diligence, or realistic timelines, citing memory prices rising roughly fivefold in two years and new chip generations arriving before old ones are even built out. He says there aren’t too many NeoClouds, just not enough good ones. Separately, he forecasts a major shift in defense: companies like Anduril and Shield AI are becoming the first new primes in a century, AI labs are increasingly becoming defense contractors amid safety and regulatory pressure, and he sees a large emerging business around AI safety and data protection, though he names no specific company.

06 Firm philosophy and personal background

Davis attributes his investing instincts partly to growing up around his grandfather, wildcatter Marvin Davis, and says Disruptive earns primarily through carry rather than management fees, with about 30 employees and a “family” culture he doesn’t want AI-driven. He pushes back on traditional value investing, referencing a recent letter in which he called it dead, arguing the goal should be buying great companies outright rather than seeking discounts, since exponential outcomes (like Anthropic’s rise from a modeled roughly $30 billion to roughly $100 billion in two years) dwarf the effect of entry-price discipline.

01 Google’s network inflection

Host Josh Chandley frames Google’s Network segment growth (a modest first increase since 2022) as the episode’s lead story, noting Google did not explain the cause. Guest Vincent Tessier (52 Entertainment) notes search fell below 50% of Google’s revenue for the first time (49% in Q3), calling it a slow diversification into cloud, YouTube, and network. He rules out AdMob gaining gaming market share or AppLovin MAX impressions as explanations, instead pointing to Google Ad Manager’s CTV growth with traditional and digital-native TV channels adopting the product. Vincent details AdMob’s strengths: strong Google-brand demand (AdWords long tail plus programmatic/DV360), banner inventory, and unmatched monetization in tier-two/tier-three countries, while AppLovin MAX still dominates gaming mediation.

Guest Warren Woodward (Uptick) adds a UA-side explanation: Google Ads iOS traffic, nonviable for roughly six years, is becoming reliable again following the mid-2025 launch of ODM, an on-device attribution method, paired with developer education and rebuilding UA-team trust. He cites the 10-Q’s explanation that the network revenue increase was driven mainly by higher AdMob revenue, partially offset by an AdSense decline. The group clarifies AdSense is web-based monetization (AdMob’s web counterpart) and its ongoing decline reflects years of falling referral traffic to ad-monetized media sites, now worsened by AI search reducing human pageviews.

02 CTV’s uneven maturation

On CTV, Vincent credits YouTube (generating roughly $3.7B quarterly on TV screens, per the host) and Google Ad Manager for securing publisher supply, including newer entrants like LinkedIn’s programmatic video. Warren outlines three reasons CTV UA is scaling: improved MMP integrations, better incrementality/media-mix modeling, and the fact that many buyers unknowingly purchase CTV inventory through DSPs. Still, both agree measurement lacks industry-standard logic and remains imprecise compared to traditional mobile attribution.

Vincent argues CTV works well for retailers and brands (citing Amazon’s one-click Prime Video shopping experience) but not yet for gaming performance, citing unreliable IP-based attribution outside the US and no publishers scaling CTV as a primary UA channel; the panel notes AppLovin’s CTV platform Wurl has gone quiet since its acquisition. Warren agrees, adding that CTV success in gaming is limited to titles with large mainstream IP that naturally overlaps non-gaming CTV audiences.

03 AI search and the monetization gap

The panel examines Google’s slowing search revenue growth (19% to 12%) despite accelerating query growth, attributing it to AI Overviews and AI Mode retaining user habit without yet carrying comparable ad placements. Warren broadens this to the chatbot “land grab,” noting OpenAI and Anthropic (both pursuing IPOs) are taking losses per user to stay ad-free/ad-light, forcing Google to delay Gemini monetization to compete, though he rates Gemini as currently behind ChatGPT and Claude. Both guests expect eventual monetization once user growth plateaus and markets demand profitability — Vincent compares it to Gmail’s two-decade free run before storage paywalls — but see AI-destined ad formats as difficult to design without disrupting chatbot UX. The group agrees the AI-search shift mainly hurts non-gaming apps (travel, shopping) and ad-supported web media, since gaming discovery rarely comes via search.

04 Meta’s pricier, better-targeted inventory

Discussing Meta’s 23% US/Canada price rise (ad revenue +26% to $63.1B, impressions +10%, price-per-ad +14%), Vincent and Warren diverge based on portfolio: Vincent’s gaming clients lean on Google/AppLovin rather than Meta, while Warren’s IAP-driven games treat Meta as a top-one-or-two channel, inverse to casual/hyper-casual titles where AppLovin dominates. Vincent credits Meta’s expanded feed inventory (beyond friends’ posts) and creative volume for driving impressions, while Josh frames CPM increases as targeting efficiency — fewer wasted impressions for the same conversions means stable ROAS despite higher sticker prices, a point Vincent reinforces by citing Meta’s AI creative tools (10M+ advertisers using them, usage up 25% QoQ) improving conversion rates.

Warren flags near-term distortions: a historically bad Q4-adjacent quarter driven by aggressive sports-betting/prediction-market bidders, record US political ad spend (cited by the host at $11.2B for midterms), and “irrational actors” bidding recklessly, echoing past “machine zone”-style waterfall disruption. Both guests advise UA teams to maintain budget flexibility rather than rigid quarterly spend plans, tucking in during irrational bidding periods even if it creates short-term cash-flow optics problems for finance teams. Separately, the panel notes Meta avoided mentioning Audience Network in earnings, attributing this to deprioritized resources and a strategic focus on promoting its Muse chatbot rather than advertising; Audience Network also remains limited by lack of iOS bidding, which both guests expect to eventually be solved via probabilistic attribution, as Google has done with ODM.

05 Mediation strategy listener question

Responding to a listener (a frequent commenter the hosts refer to by a numeric handle) asking whether large studios can run multiple mediation platforms simultaneously, Vincent confirms it’s possible but technically demanding, recommending studios split inventory by mediation strength (e.g., AdMob for banner/tier-two-three markets, AppLovin for US interstitial) rather than mixing mediators within the same ad opportunity or session. Vincent says new tools such as CloudX make multi-mediation setups easier. Both guests flag the key trade-off: AppLovin’s UA product, considered best-in-class for gaming, is unavailable unless a publisher commits 100% of ad-monetized inventory to AppLovin MAX, creating a lock-in dynamic UA teams must weigh against mediation flexibility.
