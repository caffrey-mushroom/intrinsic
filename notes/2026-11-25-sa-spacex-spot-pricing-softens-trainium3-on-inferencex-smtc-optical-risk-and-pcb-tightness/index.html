SpaceX Spot Pricing Softens, Narrowing Near-Term Upside

As we reported yesterday, SpaceX cut Blackwell spot quotes to $10–12/GPU-hour from ~$15–15.5 for

smaller clusters. Industry chatter suggests Anthropic is not renewing a ~50k H200 cluster at SpaceX. We

believe SpaceX is marketing their capacity for early 2027 at ~$5/GPU-hour. They is not alone. Our flash

update this morning shows sub-3-year rates easing ~10% M/M, reversing the gains in our November 13

update. Demand remains strong. After a year of thin volume and surge pricing in short-term contracts,

buyers have real supply to negotiate against.

For SPCX, this undercuts the most bullish scenarios. SpaceX rents at spot rather than locking in long-

term contracts. In October, we said spot exposure gives SPCX upside while rents stay elevated, with the

challenge being to sustain those economics across a much larger buildout. Supply is now arriving: H200

roll-offs and SpaceX’s own fleet growth add capacity, while compute previously rented to Chinese

offtakers needs new tenants under the new BIS rules. More broadly, we see less near-term upside to

rental pricing and more pressure on Blackwell spot rates into 2027.

Our model and buildout views are unchanged. We already carry GB300 at roughly $12/GPU-hour, or

~$50M per IT MW annually, and payback remains attractive at $10–12. The softer pricing does not

require a cut to SpaceX’s 2027 buildout. Most 2027 fleet growth is Vera Rubin, which has held a solid

premium. We would turn more cautious if VR quotes follow Blackwell lower.

Gemini 4 Neon: Cheaper List Price, Same Agent Bill

Gemini 4 Neon lists 25% below Claude Opus 5.5 but costs about the same on agent workloads. Agents

resend the whole conversation at every step, so the cache hit rate matters a lot for the bill. On our GDPx

benchmark, Neon read 88.0% of its input tokens from cache against 95.2% for Opus. The same

workload is $220K on Neon against $213K on Opus, a 4% premium for the cheaper model. As we

argued when Opus 5.5 launched, a lower list price does not guarantee a lower bill.

Blame the serving stack, not the TPUs. Every Gemini 4 model we have tested trails its rivals on caching,

and Google’s own Vertex caches Claude better than it caches Gemini. Per task, OpenAI still leads, and

Neon’s cost edge over Opus on shorter benchmarks fades in long-horizon coding. Because Google

charges no cache-write fee, Neon would be about 31% cheaper than Opus at the same hit rate. Near

term, Neon’s sticker price overstates its cost advantage.

To be clear, Gemini remains an impressive, frontier model. Its caching gap comes from best-effort

retention that evicts under load, which points to KV-memory constraints, a fixable engineering and

capacity problem. Google is already closing it: Gemini 4 caches far better than 3.1 Pro, and Vertex adds

guaranteed, paid cache retention on January 12.

InferenceX Adds Trainium3: 2x Perf per Dollar vs. Blackwell

Today, InferenceX published its first Trainium3 results, a major milestone for our engineering team. On

GLM-5.3 agentic workloads, Trainium3 delivers over 2x the performance per dollar vs. GB300 NVL72,

B300 and MI355X (at mid-range interactivity), helped by its wide expert-parallel serving approach.

InferenceX now covers every major accelerator family (Nvidia, AMD, TPU, OpenAI’s Jalapeño and

Trainium), and we expect to add new TPU and AMD SKUs, startup silicon and Chinese accelerators

soon.

The Profit Estimator, which pairs throughput with our AI Cloud TCO Model, shows what this means

economically. At 135 tok/s/user, Trainium3 earns $11.96 of profit per chip-hour at a 73.5% margin, vs.

$9.90 and 53.1% on GB300 NVL72, despite lower revenue per chip ($16.26 vs. $18.62). That cost

advantage supports why Anthropic already serves Claude on Trainium, and it strengthens Amazon’s push

to sell Trainium directly to outside customers.

Our Accelerator Model has the Trainium3 installed base at nearly 800k units exiting 2026, growing over

4x by end-2027. In our view, the InferenceX results support our aggressive estimates and positive stance

on the Trainium supply chain.

SMTC Earnings: A Longer Copper Cycle, but Potentially Shrinking Optical

Content

Semtech’s solid beat and outlook for over $1B of FY28 data center revenue confirmed what

management signaled in October. We think the negative stock reaction partly reflected deteriorating

semiconductor sentiment, alongside concerns about competitors’ vertical integration. DSP vendors are

integrating drivers into the DSP and could pursue TIAs next. Management now describes its pluggable

content as just the TIA, and it softened its 1.6T target from above 50% share by January to share that

finishes the year higher than today.

We still like copper near term. Our CIOE and OCP checks showed that NPO and CPO are not ready for

broad adoption, and Trainium4 using copper for immediate deployment where AWS had planned NPO is

one example. A longer copper cycle supports Semtech’s ACC narrative on Google’s TPU and its onboard

equalizer win at a second hyperscaler. We would temper expectations for TPUv9, though. We expect

Humufish volume production in the first half of 2028, and we model the same two ACC or AEC ports

per TPU as on TPUv8i. Google will choose ACC over AEC only if MediaTek’s SerDes is good enough.

We remain cautious on Semtech’s NPO/CPO opportunity. Market chatter suggested NPO designs would

use Semtech’s SOA earlier this year, but that may not be the case. In CPO, Semtech needs an SOA only

because its own laser lacks the output power, and a Lumentum UHP laser needs none. The TIA and

driver also move into the optical engine. If CPO dominates scale up/out after 2030, over 70% of

Semtech’s AI data center revenue could be gone.

SEMICON Europa Recap: The Demand Debate Hasn’t Reached the Supply Chain

No one we met in Munich had heard of customer orders being cut or seemed impacted by the end-

demand jitters debate. Tool lead times have roughly doubled across the board: SÜSS MicroTec bonders

went from 6 months to 12–18, DISCO dicers from 4–5 months to about 12, and Veeco equipment about

2x. AT&S and Delton now wait 12–24 months for the tools they buy.

Our PCB conversations exposed one of the tightest links in the supply chain. Chinese makers are serving

domestic demand, and everyone else is left scrambling for available capacity. CCL prices are at least

double historical levels, and glass cloth remains a gating input, with Kingboard and SCC seeing it tight

into 2028–2029.

The PCB shortages have reached test. Advantest and Aehr both said PCBs are limiting shipments, and

TIPS (small probe card vendor) was at Semicon exclusively to try and procure PCBs since usual supply

has dried up. Advantest is doubling test-cell capacity two years in a row, from about 2,500 to 5,000 this

year and to 10,000 next year, which should benefit test consumables. On optics test, FormFactor leads

for NPO, while CPO test is still open, with Technoprobe and MPI both in the running. Read the full note

here.

November ChipBook: AI’s Rising Layer Counts Tighten PCB and Substrate Supply

In this month’s ChipBook, we look at how AI is driving demand for PCBs and Substrates. Unit growth is

part of the story, but the bigger driver is the increase in layer counts. Typical PC boards have ~12 layers,

while AI server boards are moving to 40-60+ layers. Substrates that previously required 2-4 layers can

now require 24+ layers for large AI Accelerators. Chipbook data shows the strength of demand across

regions. Taiwan’s PCB imports reached $1 billion for the first time, and Singapore’s PCB imports rose

+30% month over month to a record high of more than $300 million. In the US, the PCB book-to-bill

ratio hit a record 1.77.

Substrate prices in Taiwan have surpassed their 2022–23 peak for the first time, and prices in Japan have

also reached a record high. Additionally, higher layer counts are putting pressure on the supply of

copper-clad laminate (CCL), and CCL prices are rising in China, Korea, and Japan. China recorded the

sharpest increase, with October CCL prices up more than 100% year over year. China started from the

lowest price base given its mix of lower-end laminates, but the scale of the increase shows that pricing

pressure extends well beyond the high-end materials used in AI servers. PCB and substrate suppliers’

ability to meet rising demand will depend in part on how quickly producers can expand capacity for key

inputs such as CCL, copper foil, and glass fiber.

Quick Hits

Graviton External: We think Annapurna is now pushing Gravinton5 as merchant silicon. Meta’s

large Graviton deal already validated its competitiveness. With AMD and INTC server CPUs

carrying ~65%/~60% gross margins, we think Amazon can sell Graviton at a 30% margin and still

undercut Turin Dense on cost per core, making it a real competitor to AMD and INTC in the high-

density cloud-native segment.

Crescent Island: Industry chatter suggests Chinese CSPs are ordering large volumes of Intel’s

Crescent Island PCIe inference accelerator (sold as the GPU D270), after Beijing put a hold on

Nvidia RTX Pro 5500 shipments. In our view, this adds another datapoint behind the Nvidia-China

weakness we expect over the coming year.
