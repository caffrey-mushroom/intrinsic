原标题：ASIC Industry – Broadcom & MediaTek – Senior Executive, Regional Sales at Rambus Inc (Conducted in Mandarin)

核心摘要

  1. Rambus区域销售高管认为，训练和推理芯片都不容易做，区别在于训练芯片需要HBM；博通拥有从HBM2到HBM4连续自研的HBM PHY，而联发科需向Synopsys或Cadence授权，单个先进制程IP就可能花费数千万美元，因此博通在大型CSP训练项目上占优。
  2. SerDes方面博通遥遥领先：约三年前就有224G，今年Computex前后已在自家204T以太网交换机上集成448G SerDes，而联发科224G才刚起步且不成熟，专家认为二者不属同一梯队。
  3. 博通芯片设计服务毛利率约60-70%甚至超过70%，联发科可接受约48-60%的毛利率；同一颗芯片从博通转到联发科可节省近20%成本，联发科还以响应快、愿意加班和配合修改需求取胜，而博通对SOW外变更收取高额NRE。
  4. 谷歌倾向采用多供应商的高低搭配策略，专家认为V9完全交给联发科、博通直接做V10的说法不可靠；ASIC市场规模已扩大一个数量级，Anthropic、OpenAI、特斯拉等新玩家加入，博通人力有限也无法独吞。
  5. 博通可提供从规格到前后端、封测的spec-in全流程服务，联发科、GUC和世芯多为netlist-in模式；OpenAI、Anthropic等AI实验室不愿养庞大后端团队，因此更可能留在博通。
  6. HBM方面，博通同时有四五个HBM训练项目，可合并多客户量与三星、美光、SK海力士议价，通常代客户采购HBM；小型设计服务商项目少、规格不一，更倾向让客户自行采购后托交，推理芯片使用的LPDDR、GDDR则由ODM或系统厂采购。
  7. 一颗300-400平方毫米的推理芯片封测后成本约1500美元，而整机柜售价可达约650万美元，说明系统集成附加值远大于芯片本身；博通的以太网交换机、PCIe交换机和Retimer在阿里、腾讯、百度等高性能数据中心中常见。
  8. 专家排名为博通第一、Marvell第二、联发科第三，其后是Socionext、GUC和世芯；高通收购Alphawave后也在进军数据中心ASIC，博通增长稳健、不太可能出现30-40%的收入骤降，Marvell则相对激进。

00:00:22] Today, I would like to consult you about the competitive landscape of the ASIC [application-specific integrated circuit] industry, its moats and some specific issues regarding volume and price. I mainly focus on two companies, Broadcom and MediaTek. I previously heard that Broadcom's moat mainly lies in its TPU [tensor processing unit] experience, vertical switch integration and breadth of IP but MediaTek's 224G lags behind it by some time. Has this situation changed recently? I notice that in the new V8, Broadcom is instead doing the training and MediaTek is doing the inference but I think inference should be more difficult. Do you think Broadcom's moat has weakened somewhat? Why, in the V8 generation, is MediaTek instead making the more difficult training chip?

Expert [00:00:54]: Whether for training or inference, it is not a simple chip to make. It is just that training chips require HBM, while inference generally uses, for example, LPDDR or GDDR. First of all, whether it is a training chip or an inference chip, it is actually not easy to make. It is not the case that inference is easier or training is easier. Going back to your question just now, whether it is for training or inference, Google's V8 is not a simple chip to make. There is no such thing as one being easier and the other harder. The difference is that when you make a training chip, it is paired with HBM. For example, the latest generation is HBM4, while the previous generation was HBM3 and HBM3E. In the high-bandwidth memory field, Broadcom has its own in-house PHY but MediaTek does not have its own HBM PHY. This is why MediaTek finds it difficult to win training ASIC orders. One of the factors is that it does not have such an in-house PHY. Is it the end of the world if it does not have an in-house PHY? Not necessarily. For example, it could also adopt Synopsys' or Cadence's HBM. It is just that, in comparison, Broadcom has an advantage, because its HBM is self-developed in-house and has a continuous lineage from its early HBM2, HBM3 and HBM3E to HBM4. This is why Broadcom often has the upper hand in the training projects of the larger CSPs.

MediaTek, on the other hand, does not have self-developed HBM technology, so it can only license it from major IP companies and then participate in these bids. Of course, it will also be at a slight cost disadvantage. After all, if you license IP such as HBM3 and HBM4 from these major companies and the IP is at an advanced process node such as TSMC's 3nm or 2nm node, the cost can easily be tens of millions of US dollars. A single IP alone can cost tens of millions of US dollars. In addition, in these high-speed chips, whether for training or inference, there will be a need for scale-up and scale-out, so high-speed interfaces such as Ethernet are also required.

Ethernet has advanced from 100G, 200G, 400G and 800G to the latest 1.6T and this is on the controller side. For the underlying PHY, the key is which generation of SerDes is used, such as 110G SerDes, 224G SerDes or the latest 448G SerDes. Broadcom is far ahead in this area. Even though MediaTek claims that it also has 224G SerDes now, Broadcom already had 224G SerDes roughly three years ago.

If I remember correctly, around the time of Computex Taipei this year, Broadcom already had 448G SerDes. It was not just IP-ready, because it used the 448G SerDes on its own high-speed Ethernet switches. When others were developing 102T, Broadcom was already working on 204T Ethernet switches and had successfully integrated its own 448G SerDes. However, MediaTek is only just starting with 224G, which is still not very mature. So do you think MediaTek and Broadcom are in the same tier of ASIC service providers? In my opinion, they are not in the same tier. Broadcom is capable of undertaking projects that MediaTek can undertake. It is just that the gross profit margin MediaTek is willing to accept is lower than Broadcom's. Broadcom almost exclusively works with large CSPs that have substantial volume.

As I just said, the gross profit margin requirements are different. Broadcom may have an average gross profit margin of 60-70% or even higher. However, MediaTek is a new ASIC design service supplier. Without the capabilities of Broadcom, it is certainly unable to enjoy a high gross profit margin of 60-70%. In order to win such business, it may even lower its margin requirements. For example, as long as the gross profit margin is 48%, 50% or 60%, it will be good enough for it. This also shows that the chiplets in the projects MediaTek secures have relatively low unit prices, whereas those in Broadcom's projects have relatively high unit prices. First, Broadcom has higher gross profit requirements. Second, Broadcom's designs are also more complex. This is a high-level comparison between Broadcom and MediaTek.

Client [00:09:07]: If you switch from Broadcom to MediaTek, roughly how much can you save?

Expert [00:09:07]: For the same chip, you can save nearly 20%. As I just said, Broadcom has high requirements for profit margins, which can be seen from its financial reports. The average gross margin for their chip design services is over 70%. However, as we all know, MediaTek has several major businesses. It makes mobile phone chips, digital TV chips and IoT chips. In fact, the gross margin of these consumer chips is only about 45-48%. Of course, the gross margin of the ASIC business is somewhat higher than that of its consumer business but not by much. Otherwise, if it were much higher, MediaTek's overall gross margin would not be only in the 40s. It could reach the 60s or 70s.

This explains why, in fact, although MediaTek has secured a good number of design wins in its ASIC business in recent years and its revenue growth has been rapid, its gross profit margin cannot match the high gross profit margin enjoyed by Broadcom. If it can later develop something that it can make but Broadcom cannot, then it may be possible for it to enjoy a 60-70% gross profit margin. However, no such phenomenon or trend has been observed so far. It is just that when MediaTek responds to RFQs, particularly for hyperscaler ASIC projects involving high-speed SerDes, it does so relatively quickly and proactively.

For example, you mentioned that Broadcom's engineers may all take an uninterrupted holiday over Thanksgiving. When Christmas comes, they may start their holiday in mid-December and remain on holiday until early January. As for MediaTek, of course it does not take Christmas off. By contrast, not all of its employees take leave during the Spring Festival. If foreign clients are not on holiday, there are still people on duty and some will work overtime to meet clients' deadlines. This is where design services in Asia differ from those in Europe and America. They are relatively hardworking and uncomplaining, work overtime for clients’ projects and flexibly mobilise these resources.

That is why Google later stopped relying on a single dominant supplier. In fact, Google’s approach now is to have at least two or more suppliers as far as possible. There may be a high-low tiering strategy, for example one company handles high-end products, another handles low-end products or one handles low-end and mid-range products and they are matched accordingly. The basis and standards for such matching depend entirely on what conditions are most favourable and reasonable for Google. Whether it is price, delivery time, the quality of support or whether they are willing to make changes again and again.

One thing Broadcom was often criticised for in the past was that, once a contract was signed, any changes beyond the SOW would incur an additional NRE fee and this extra NRE fee was generally not a small amount. Of course, this is also Broadcom's attempt to establish the rules of the game. Chip design services are, after all, a type of service work but Broadcom does not want this to become a matter of being servile to clients. Instead, it hopes clients will take the requirements of this project very seriously. The specifications it sets are as carefully considered as possible, rather than being vague at the start and then in the middle, wanting to do something ambitious and finally having to rein things in, with one feature removed and another added. In fact, this is the most troublesome problem for ASIC projects.

Broadcom's stance has always been relatively tough, that is, relatively firm. Once a contract is signed, it does not want anything changed. If it must be changed, the schedule, price and commercial terms will almost certainly have to be renegotiated. However, Taiwan-based ASIC design companies, whether it is Taiwan's MediaTek, GUC or Alchip, generally have a somewhat subordinate-to-superior relationship with their clients. If clients ask them to make changes, they usually won’t refuse but of course they will add some extra NRE. They won’t say no immediately. Most of them say 'yes but...' rather than 'no but...'. If it’s Broadcom, the answer is usually a no, a direct no.

Expert [00:19:18]: Meta is like that too. It also favours a multi-supplier strategy. Of course, Meta does not work only with Broadcom. Broadcom is not only a back-end service provider but also a spec-in service provider. As long as clients develop specifications, it even provides services for front-end, back-end, OSAT packaging and testing and PD. However, GUC, MediaTek and Alchip may currently have capabilities more in line with the netlist-in model. The so-called netlist-in means that the front-end design work is done by the clients themselves. The front-end work generally involves the know-how or business characteristics of the clients, so only they are familiar with their own digital circuits. As for those chip design service companies I just mentioned, they are actually more adept at back-end layout and physical design and at coordinating with OSAT companies such as ASE Technology and Allian Photonics and even with TSMC's 2.5D CoWoS. Then their design, such as its PPA, shows how performance, power consumption and area can be optimised to make the design smaller and more refined. This is where design experience and technical strength are compared. The latter three companies, namely MediaTek, GUC and Alchip, mostly provide netlist-in only. They rarely provide spec-in. They are therefore now deploying substantial resources in design services, hoping to gain traction both in ASIC design services and with their own-brand full-rack AI inference servers. They want to see whether they can also capture some market share. Therefore, ASIC design service is the area to watch. There are many projects of this kind, especially on the inference side and some relatively new players are also joining in, making the whole market more lively.

Client [00:28:58]: So it is highly likely that HBM will still be included, right?
