SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion assessed KRAS-mutant solid tumors, particularly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, where mortality remains high and therapeutic progress has lagged other gastrointestinal cancers. KRAS mutations reportedly occur in at least 80% of pancreatic cancers, with G12D and other G12 variants prominent.

The expert evaluated selective KRAS inhibitors, broader pan-RAS approaches, and SYNB021225, an early-stage oral pan-KRAS macrocyclic peptide designed to inhibit active and inactive KRAS states. Macrocyclic peptides may help address previously difficult targets through improved binding, cellular penetration, oral delivery, and potentially greater specificity.

Key Insights

Selective inhibitors may provide stronger efficacy against their intended mutation, while pan-RAS agents could offer broader coverage after resistance. Using broad inhibition too early may leave fewer options after alternative pathways emerge. RMC-6236 was viewed as potentially practice-changing if its reported survival benefit is reproduced in further phase III and real- world evidence. Rapid resistance remains a central concern. SYNB021225 must first demonstrate predictable exposure, tumor penetration, adequate half-life, and acceptable safety in phase I and II trials. The expert strongly favored combinations over monotherapy in pancreatic cancer, particularly adding KRAS inhibition to chemotherapy or PD-1-based immunotherapy. Rare but severe toxicities require close monitoring, including gastrointestinal, cardiac, hepatic, renal, ocular, skin, mucosal, and metabolic effects. Careful early trial design, biomarker stratification, biostatistical planning, and focused resource allocation were considered critical for smaller developers.

CZ: For KRAS- or RAS-targeted therapies, what do you think future drug development should focus on improving? Should the priority be greater efficacy, or are there other areas that need improvement?

QF: Based on our many years of experience with targeted therapy, some of the earliest successful small-molecule targets were EGFR and, later, ALK. For EGFR, studies such as the 2007 IPAC study in lung cancer helped establish the target. With EGFR and ALK, first-, second-, and third-generation drugs were developed, with each generation designed to overcome resistance to earlier drugs and provide stronger inhibition. I think that stepwise progression is very important. RAS is still at a very early stage, almost like an infant, whereas EGFR and ALK are already quite mature. RAS has been so difficult to drug because its molecular surface is very smooth, without an obvious pocket. It's difficult to lock onto the switch and prevent an activating mutation, or to switch it off once it has mutated. From a drug- development perspective, identifying a suitable binding pocket has been extremely challenging. The good news is that researchers have gradually found key points to target and have developed clever ways to exploit the pocket and shut down the target in its open and closed conformations.

The problem now is that the effects may not be stable. One concern is durability; another is that we don't yet know what unusual or rare toxicities may emerge. With EGFR and ALK, for example, rash and diarrhea are common adverse reactions. Some newer targeted agents have also been associated with ocular toxicity, specific skin and mucosal injuries, hyperlipidemia, and effects on various aspects of metabolism. For RAS inhibitors, we still don't know exactly what changes to expect. Current large datasets reveal some of the more obvious toxicities, but the deeper or less apparent risks are difficult to estimate. Fortunately, we may be able to draw on what we know about the RAS and PI3K- AKT pathways. They have some shared downstream signaling, and the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK pathway and the PI3K-AKT pathway ultimately converge on similar downstream processes. In breast cancer, for example, the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway has been studied in considerable depth, and downstream molecular markers can be predictive.

CZ: The companies developing these drugs generally seem to be following one of two approaches: selective inhibitors targeting specific mutations such as G12C or G12D, or broader pan-RAS inhibitors. What is your view of these two strategies?

QF: In targeted therapy, inhibiting a specific target may be more effective and potent. In China, for example, some FGFR inhibitors are approved for FGFR2-related indications, even though many of them can inhibit FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, and FGFR4; the indication is often based on which target the drug inhibits most strongly. KRAS faces a similar issue. G12C is a well-established target, as is G12D, and I would expect a selective inhibitor to work better against its intended target. With a broad-spectrum approach that inhibits all of RAS, in theory the drug may cover a wider range of targets, but it's difficult to say which strategy is better in every situation. In my view, selective inhibitors may have the edge in efficacy. The advantage of a pan-target drug is that, after resistance develops to a selective inhibitor, a broader drug may cover more targets and become an option in later lines of treatment. It's similar to the many anti- angiogenic targeted therapies we use. Once resistance develops to one drug, we look for another with broader coverage. Cabozantinib, for instance, has anti-angiogenic activity and also targets other pathways, including RAS- related targets, giving it relatively broad coverage. Sometimes we reserve drugs like that as a last-line option.

That's the theory, but in practice, we still have to make decisions based on clinical outcomes and how each patient's disease evolves. I think starting with a pan-target drug may be disadvantageous for later treatment. If you block all the obvious pathways right away, an unknown pathway may become activated later, making the disease more difficult to manage. It's similar to how cancer can become harder to treat as it progresses through successive lines of therapy.

CZ: The main focus of our discussion is Syneron Bio's oral pan-KRAS inhibitor, which uses macrocyclic peptide technology. This technology has attracted increasing attention in recent years. QF: I think this technology has real promise. Traditional small-molecule drugs often act on a single site, which can make their effect relatively narrow. Bypass signaling can then become activated, and resistance may develop quickly. Macrocyclic peptides may offer advantages in inhibiting these targets, including better cell penetration and the possibility of oral administration. I've seen platforms that use macrocyclic peptide design to develop drugs against targets that were previously difficult to drug, including certain signaling pathways where the platform may offer unique advantages. The first challenge is to develop a viable drug; the second is to achieve stable oral absorption. In treatment, the key is to maintain an effective drug concentration in the blood and adequate drug exposure in the tumor. That's also a critical endpoint in phase 1 trials. If the drug can achieve consistent, effective systemic exposure, reach tumor tissue at sufficient concentrations, and maintain an adequate half-life, those would be important advantages. At the same time, we need to pay close attention to potential toxicities that are not yet known. We may inhibit RAS signaling and suppress the tumor, but we must also consider effects on signaling in normal tissues and potential toxicity to the gastrointestinal tract, heart, liver, and kidneys. These issues can only be explored progressively through phase 1 and phase 2 clinical trials.

Overall, I think macrocyclic peptide platforms and the RAS inhibitors already on the market must first demonstrate an acceptable safety profile before we can fully assess efficacy.
