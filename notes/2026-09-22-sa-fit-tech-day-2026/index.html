Executive Summary

  • FIT is likely to play in the XPO ecosystem. They showcased ChipLink 448G, which is their CPC solution

used to connect XPO modules from the faceplate to a switch ASIC. We estimate 128 differential pairs (DPs) worth of copper and there is a possibility they have to be implemented using PAM6 modulation at 448G per lane.

  • Point2’s RF based plastic waveguide for short-reach (<50m) links directly competes with Active Electrical

Cables (AECs) and Active Optical Cables (AOCs) for NIC-to-TOR deployments but micro VCSELs remain the front runner thus far.

  • NTT co-developed a 500-pin ultra low-loss connector for detachable optical engine architectures with FIT

but we are not yet seeing large-scale manufacturing and deployments in the supply chain. 500-pins per connector over a relatively small surface area implies extremely high bandwidth density we are not yet seeing in the roadmap over the next 5 years.

  • Another emerging technology is multidimensional multiplexing that results in up to 34 Tbps per fiber strand.

Again, this is frontier and we are not seeing adoption any time soon.

  • Ams OSRAM expects to lean into the Taiwanese supply-chain ecosystem for implementation of its

wide-and-slow architecture. This may mean broader adoption of multi-core fiber expected to reduce fiber count.

ChipLink 448G (CPC) FIT showcased an XPO module with co-packaged copper (CPC) cables connecting the pluggable at the faceplate to the ASIC. A 448G per lane module would deliver a total of 25.6T of bandwidth, which is 2x the bandwidth of the first generation of XPO modules (12.8T bandwidth) we expect to be delivered with the Tomahawk 7 ASIC. At 448G per lane, it is still unclear if LPO is attainable and there is a possibility that the receive channel on the optical module is retimed or the copper channel adopts PAM6 modulation or both. A single 25.6T XPO module is the equivalent of eight 3.2T OSFP transceiver modules at 448G per lane. One end of the module uses eight MPO x16 fiber connectors (8 Tx and 8 Rx). The field pluggable module mates into a front panel receptacle and visible at the edge of the module below is a cold plate that is sandwiched between two 32-lane paddle cards. Coming off the faceplate on the interior of the tray is 128 differential pairs (DPs) worth of copper connectors and cables connecting the front panel receptacle to the switch ASIC. We expect FIT to be one of the suppliers of the CPC interface used for XPO deployments.

RF based plastic waveguides as alternative to AEC and AOCs We observe several alternative technologies in the ecosystem targeting NIC-to-ToR use cases and Point2’s e-Tube RF transceiver using plastic waveguide connections is one of them positioning as a replacement for Active Electrical Cables (AECs) and Active Optical Cables (AOCs). The transceiver converts electrical signals to millimeter-wave signals, which are then routed using plastic dielectric waveguides. We depict the module below. Notice that plastic waveguides are placed on top of RF transmitters using a 90-degree hollow waveguide array arrangement.

Assuming the technology works and scales, the value proposition for e-Tube adoption is its similar cost structure as copper, but unlike copper, the link loss does not scale with frequency. This makes it perfect for 100G per lane use cases and beyond. Additionally, plastic waveguides are much lighter compared to copper or optical fibers. Showcased in the event as an 800G OSFP form factor transceiver transmitting 200G per waveguide with 8 waveguides in total (4 transmit and 4 receive).

However, we think there is still some time before Point2 can ramp e-Tubes as evident in the demonstration of the 112G PAM4 signaling below. At 112G per lane, they are competing with AECs that are significantly more mature and have demonstrated performance at scale. It is also likely that they remain behind 224G PAM4 AEC deployments right now and we think that RF-based plastic waveguides may only be able to compete for NIC-to-ToR deployments at 448G per lane and beyond alongside micro VCSELs and micro LEDs in the 2029 timeframe and beyond. As of right now, micro VCSELs are the strongest contender gaining significant industry attention compared to alternative solutions.

Point2 recently obtained a Series B funding totaling 76 million USD, including participation from UMC Capital and NVentures (Nvidia’s venture capital arm).

NTT Innovative Devices showcase detachable optical engine socket design in partnership with FIT NTT Innovative devices (former CEO Yoshiaki Sato) presented several solutions in scale-up, scale-out and scale-across networking. To be clear, PEC-1 (co-packaged optics for transceivers) refers to the integration of optical modulation and DSP transceivers to scale between datacenters using optical fiber.

For scale-out, NTT showcased a Tomahawk 6 ASIC with several detachable optical engines around the switch ASIC. Detachable optical engines are attractive to hyperscalers and AI clouds outside of the Nvidia ecosystem and we expect these solutions to continue to gain traction.

Specifically, NTT co-developed a 500-pin detachable ultra low-loss connector used for detachable optical engine architectures. We have not seen broader adoption of this ultra-low loss connector in the ecosystem yet for the earlier NPO programs (TH6) and this appears to be an ambitious project as compared to the 64- or 128 pin connectors we expect to be developed for 3.2T and 6.4T optical engines respectively.

For scale-up, NTT is researching integrating lasers on chiplets. Specifically, they are attempting to create an internal thin membrane laser with a very thin active region. Such a thin membrane laser uses high index contrast between the thin active InP based layer and the surrounding material to increase the optical mode confinement, which leads to a lower lasing threshold current and higher gain. To be clear, integrated lasers on chiplets such as the ones being developed by Intel are not the same as ILS (internal laser source) adopted in NPO modules. We think this technology remains in its early stages of development, and it will be many years before it is being adopted at scale.

Multidimensional Multiplexing for High Throughput Communications In the FIT Tech Day panel, Foxconn’s Semiconductor Research Center showcased an interesting research paper on multidimensional multiplexing. They proposed a proof-of-concept involving the use of a Quantum Dot comb laser which can generate 23 wavelengths from a single laser source. These are integrated into a multi-core fiber with 7 cores enabling simultaneous wavelength and spatial multiplexing. This ends up creating multiplexing in the wavelength domain through the multi-wavelength comb laser source and spatial multiplexing by a multi-core fiber, thus supercharging the throughput passing through a single fiber. Foxconn termed this combination of comb laser wavelength multiplexing and spatial multiplexing (using multi-core fibers) as multidimensional multiplexing. At 212G PAM4 per wavelength, this arrangement can result in up to 34 Tbps per fiber, eliminating the need for multiple lasers and reducing system complexity and fiber count. Comb laser implementation is significant here since these lasers output multiple wavelengths from a single source, elegantly eliminating the need to use multiple single wavelength laser sources and having to multiplex them into a single fiber (or fiber core in this case). However, integrating multiple comb lasers into each core of a multi-core fiber is still a major challenge, and the use of an SOA with a multi wavelength quantum dot comb laser can lead to four-wave mixing, which can degrade link budget by introducing intensity noise across multiple bands (which a system with single wavelength laser sources can largely avoid).

Ams OSRAM makes a case for Wide-and-Slow Interconnects Ams OSRAM’s talk was centered around the need for interconnects which are designed for reduced power and latency to allow for bigger AI scale-up world sizes. The key point of the talk was that wide-and-slow interconnect architectures provide good reach and low latency for scale-up domains while simultaneously maintaining cost and power efficiency.

Ams OSRAM in this regard is uniquely positioned to partake in this opportunity since they have capabilities to produce both thin film microVCSELs as well as microLEDs, with all the manufacturing processes in-house. They aim to apply their micro-emitter manufacturing expertise towards AI photonics.

To support the Micro VCSELs ecosystem, ams OSRAM expects to lean into the Taiwanese supply-chain ecosystem for wide-and-slow. They also expect to rely on multi-core fibers, which is expected to significantly reduce fiber count and overall costs for a given number of micro-emitters because multiple cores are embedded in a single cladding.
