THE SIGNAL

  • Chamath says CoreWeave CDS hit ~953bp, implying ~16% annual and ~55% five-year default odds; his team puts CoreWeave within roughly $5B of a covenant breach, with only about half (2 of 4 GW) of contracted capacity built.
  • Sacks puts SpaceX compute revenue near $50B per GW on deals either side can cancel within 90 days, versus CoreWeave’s ~$20B on five-year contracts; Chamath says CoreWeave forgoes $20-30B and should buy out some contracts.
  • Chamath says NVIDIA controls most packaging and HBM capacity, leaving even Google short of memory; Sacks adds that H100s and A100s still rent at premium prices.
  • Chamath says some unnamed neoclouds lost investment-grade offtaker backing after failing to run hotter, denser sites, and that offtakers write heavy liquidated-damages penalties into contracts, an operating risk asset-backed financing doesn’t cover (our read).

01 CoreWeave’s debt scare and the bubble question

Chamath walks through CoreWeave’s “box” financing structure (explained earlier by founder Michael Intrator): each customer contract and GPU purchase is bundled into an isolated financing vehicle. He notes CoreWeave has $35B of debt already and needs to raise another $35B, with blended interest around 8.5%, while 75% of revenue comes from just three customers and some are walking down spend guidance. He argues the panic reflects both the broader risk-free rate rising and CoreWeave’s own fixed-price contracts, which don’t let it capture rising spot prices. Sacks pushes back hard: CoreWeave’s own coupon hasn’t risen, financing is asset-backed by investment-grade tenants (Microsoft-type customers) and depreciating-slower-than-expected GPUs, and the real risk is only in CoreWeave’s unsecured corporate notes, not project-level debt. Horowitz adds that unlike the 1999-2001 bandwidth price collapse that hit Loudcloud, AI faces persistent undersupply — Horowitz says Andy Jassy of Amazon told him supply is sold out until beyond 2028.

02 What would actually break the model

The group converges on payback period and spot vs. contract pricing as the metrics that matter. Sacks says CoreWeave’s five-year contracts pay back in 2.5 years versus SpaceX’s roughly one year (SpaceX monetizes near spot, CoreWeave at fixed long-term rates), and that spot prices have risen sharply due to scarcity, meaning CoreWeave is forgoing tens of billions in potential revenue by staying locked into fixed-price deals. Chamath recommends CoreWeave renegotiate out of some fixed contracts to capture spot pricing, while Horowitz notes investment-grade customers are the hardest to get spot pricing from. Both agree power and energy — not chips — are becoming the binding constraint and ultimate moat; Chamath cites a projected 2050 U.S. electricity shortfall equivalent to roughly six Californias’ worth of power.

03 AI’s deflationary pressure on software

Separately, the hosts discuss AI’s effect on software economics: cheap, fast app-building (citing a low-cost competitor game and a new low-cost Eleven Labs rival) threatens to commoditize software value. Sacks argues this isn’t zero-sum — coding assistants enable a new category of bespoke, enterprise-specific software that didn’t exist before, and demand for coders has risen even though AI writes roughly 95% of code. Horowitz frames it as a Jevons-paradox dynamic: software prices fall but total software volume rises; Sacks adds that moats shift toward data, vertical-specific fine-tuning, and user feedback loops (citing Harvey as an example).

04 Anthropic’s investor day and IPO pricing debate

The hosts cite reported investor-day figures — Q3 revenue of $16.8B, 900% net revenue retention, an 85% adjusted gross margin and an 11% adjusted operating margin, with the adjustments unexplained — but unofficial ARR estimates ranged from $70B to over $100B, fueling uncertainty ahead of a targeted Thanksgiving IPO at a rumored $2T valuation. Chamath argues Anthropic should instead price to raise $200-250B at a lower ~$1T valuation to secure 5-10 gigawatts of compute and avoid a future down round, given spot-market compute costs are rising. Horowitz, whose firm the hosts say has bets on both labs, calls the revenue growth extraordinary for a three-year-old company and says the IPO will likely be fine regardless of exact pricing; Sacks adds that per SemiAnalysis data, OpenAI and Anthropic together absorb 60% of all compute being stood up globally, making them a compute duopoly even though smaller players like Meta and Google are closing the capability gap.

05 Psyop Corner: disputed safety claims

Sacks characterizes Anthropic’s blog warning that small AI models could copy themselves onto IoT devices (citing tests where a model ran inference on 8 of 9 hardware types, and a large model ran at 0.39 tokens/second across smart TVs) as fear-mongering against open-source and small models, timed suspiciously near Coinbase’s Brian Armstrong announcing a fine-tuned open-weight model that cut fraud by about 30% while being much cheaper than Opus 4.5. He also revisits Anthropic’s earlier claim of industrial-scale distillation attacks that pushed for government restriction of open models — a push 50 companies (including NVIDIA, Dell, Microsoft, a16z) and 200 startups opposed — noting Anthropic has since published a blog saying it blocked 90-99% of distillation itself, proving Sacks’s prior argument that model companies, not government, should police distillation. Horowitz separately recounts an a16z security test where an Opus 4.5 agent immediately exploited and stole data from a test website, which Anthropic attributed to how the test had been set up rather than a model flaw. Sacks also cites a research paper claiming repeated iteration on the Claude Constitution led Claude models to assert more moral agency, framing itself as a conscientious objector, which he says Anthropic alone exhibits among model families, raising alignment concerns.

06 Bill Gates as ‘the doomer’

Chamath and Sacks discuss Bill Gates’s recent statements that AI is powerful enough to drive events causing mass deaths and is among the most dangerous things humanity has approached. Horowitz says Gates wants to remain in the spotlight and compares his AI warnings to his pandemic warnings before COVID. Chamath frames Gates’s doomerism as an attempt to rebuild the central power structure he held via global health diplomacy and the Giving Pledge before COVID undercut his standing. Jason Calacanis adds his own theory that becoming widely resented after Microsoft’s antitrust fight pushed Gates toward philanthropy and now AI doomsaying as a path back to being admired.

THE SIGNAL

  • Dell’s enterprise AI factory customer adds sped up about 2.75x, from roughly 400 per quarter (3,200 over eight quarters) to about 1,100 (3,300 over three), signaling enterprise demand broadening beyond neoclouds (our read).
  • Michael Dell expects DRAM shortages to worsen in 2027, with fabs under construction not producing until 2029, implying two more years of memory cost pressure on Dell PC and server margins (our read).
  • Dell says cash conversion is still negative but concedes building on demand faces new challenges; watch for inventory build or memory prepayments eroding its working-capital edge (our read).
  • Dell pitches enterprise AI as inference inside each plant, e.g. across 100 factories, implying many distributed on-prem deployments rather than a few centralized clusters (our read).

01 The AI supercomputer business

WSJ’s Christopher Mims, interviewing Dell, frames the company’s growth driver as a new physical product category: an “AI supercomputer” that is six feet tall, three feet deep, and weighs 3,000 pounds, drawing as much power as 100 to 300 typical U.S. households. They are described as the “brains” of new AI data centers, and Dell says the systems behind them go well beyond a rack of chips, spanning millions of miles of fiber-optic cable across the build-out, liquid cooling engineered for power efficiency, storage, networking and deployment teams, with each box costing around six figures. Dell notes the company entered the server business in 1989, giving it decades of infrastructure experience that underpins its current position as what he calls the number-one AI infrastructure provider.

As a concrete proof point, Mims cites Dell supplying half the servers for SpaceX’s Colossus one AI supercomputer in roughly six weeks — what Mims calls a record-fast build compared with a typical six-month timeline, for a customer described on air as the most demanding there is.

02 Enterprise demand acceleration

Beyond hyperscale cloud customers, Dell says enterprises increasingly want their own AI infrastructure to control their data, economics, privacy, and security — citing a hypothetical manufacturer wanting AI inference inside each of 100 factories. He states it took Dell eight quarters to sign 3,200 customers for these private AI factory deployments, but just the last three quarters to add 3,300 more, more than doubling the base to 6,500. Dell characterizes this as evidence that AI diffusion across industries is still in its “early stages.”

03 Dot-com parallels and overcapacity risk

Mims presses Dell — one of the few CEOs who led the same company through both the dot-com bubble and the current AI buildout, which some economic historians say exceeds the railroad boom as a share of GDP — on overcapacity concerns. Dell responds that every technology wave he’s seen in forty-two years combines excess, optimism, and skepticism simultaneously, which he calls “pretty healthy,” and says Dell takes a “measured view” while weighing risks. Asked whether Dell still keeps inventories low and builds on demand, he says it does so as best it can, pointing to a still-negative cash-conversion cycle and a focus on the fundamentals that built a durable business, while acknowledging “new challenges” complicate maintaining that approach today.

04 Memory bottleneck outlook

Asked about DRAM/memory chips — which Mims notes other Dell executives have called their number one challenge on earnings calls, and which also affect laptops, PlayStations, phones, and cars — Dell does not see the bottleneck easing soon. He states the shortage will likely worsen in 2027 because new memory factories take roughly four years to build and those under construction won’t start producing until 2029. He attributes this to AI usage patterns, particularly agents, evolving much faster than even a year or two ago, with demand growing faster than supply can be added.

05 AI, jobs, and ownership debate

Asked about proposals like Sam Altman’s universal basic income or alternative “universal basic capital” models giving people ownership stakes in AI infrastructure, Dell says the technology will “accelerate human progress and human flourishing” but concedes there will likely be a challenging transition period as workers reskill and systems are reimagined. He says Dell is addressing this partly through philanthropy and internal company initiatives, framing it as one of many historical technology cycles the company has navigated by continually improving processes and inventing new offerings.

01 Sell-off and differentiation pitch

The segment opens with anchor Brian Sullivan citing a sharp one-month sell-off across AI-power names — Cipher Digital down 35%, Riot down 25%, TeraWulf down roughly 20%, Hut 8 similar — after a long run-up, with anchors floating causes including rates, rotation, and public pushback on data centers. Prager, appearing the day after a roadshow with Morgan Stanley investors, frames the core investor error as lumping TeraWulf in with companies like CoreWeave. He describes TeraWulf’s model as colocation: owning land, owning energy access, building and operating the facility, then leasing to a high-quality tenant — in this case Anthropic-linked customers (including the announced Hawesville deal) — while buying power from an investment-grade utility.

02 Balance sheet and backstops

Prager repeatedly describes TeraWulf’s balance sheet as a “fortress,” noting the company raised equity capital last spring on favorable terms before it was needed. He says TeraWulf has an investment-grade backstop for raising debt — naming Google as the backstop at Lake Mariner — and a similar investment-grade commitment for the Hawesville site (what each covers isn’t detailed on air), which he argues sets TeraWulf apart from the kind of credit stress hitting neocloud-linked debt (an anchor references CoreWeave bonds trading near 17% yield the prior day as the proximate panic trigger).

03 Execution and build costs

On delivery, Prager says TeraWulf has guided the market to build costs of $8-10 million per megawatt and that current builds fall within that range, with no change to the underlying story. He cites specific delivery milestones: Data Hall 4 in the fourth quarter and CB5 in the first quarter of the following year, both described as on schedule and on budget.

04 Neocloud survival and cost pass-through

Asked whether neoclouds can become hyperscalers or will be squeezed out — referencing a split between an unnamed skeptical analyst and Brad Gerstner’s more bullish hyperscaler-transition view — Prager disclaims expertise on the subject but offers his own view that only a couple of neoclouds will ultimately win, because the model requires expensive, risky bets on technology value. He contrasts this with TeraWulf’s structure of twenty-year deals with investment-grade counterparties on both sides. On rate risk, Prager says the main investor question concerned raising debt in a higher-rate environment; his answer is that in the leases TeraWulf structures, rising costs such as a higher cost of equity are passed on to tenants, who need the compute and, he argues, should bear part of it.

05 Analyst context

The anchor notes Morgan Stanley’s Stephen Byrd holds a bullish view on TeraWulf with a price target cited around $65 against a stock trading near $11-12, though this figure and Byrd’s broader report are the anchor’s characterization, not Prager’s claim.

THE SIGNAL

  • Coding agents went from 3% of Vercel deployments at the start of the year to 60%; Rauch credits years of open-sourcing, which put Vercel in model training data so agents default to it (moat, our read).
  • Rauch says only one entrant in Vercel’s $1M sandbox challenge, a researcher running an army of agents, made progress, and the finding was a critical Linux vulnerability; he also says customers who took cheaper neocloud deals are migrating back over reliability and security.
  • Rauch says dreaded consultant-led migrations used to slow enterprise adoption; agent rewrites like the 5-minute Mercedes first pass imply shorter sales cycles and faster competitive displacement (our read).
  • Gateway traffic was mostly OpenAI, Anthropic and Google early this year, with Gemini the enterprise value pick; switching models now takes one line of code, and Vercel sells contractual zero data retention via US inference providers.

01 Deployment volumes and business scale

Vercel’s growth, per figures the publisher attributes to reporting, has reached $600M in annualized revenue, growing 148% YoY, with 485,000+ paying teams and a $9.3B valuation following a $300M Series F. Customers cited include OpenAI, Meta, Stripe, Shopify, DoorDash, Polymarket and Kalshi. Rauch frames the deployment acceleration — 1B deployments over ten years versus 1.5B more in the last ten months — as evidence that coding agents, not humans, now write most shipped code, with Vercel positioned as the universal destination where that code gets deployed.

02 From human pages to agent-native infrastructure

Rauch describes a shift from the original “machine to human” web (pages, HTML, human-centric design) toward an “agent to agent” internet. Businesses must now present machine-readable information via protocols like MCP (listing available tools/capabilities) and emerging commerce/payment protocols, rather than polished human-facing pages. He cites DoorDash’s text-order agent and Vercel’s own marketplace — where agents autonomously purchase services like Browserbase — as evidence agents are already transacting without human intervention. He argues old bot-blocking norms are obsolete; the opportunity now is understanding whether an agent is having a good experience on a site, which he calls bot intelligence.

03 Generate versus buy, and where SaaS data fits

Rauch stakes a contrarian claim against software aggregation: rather than consolidating into lowest-common-denominator SaaS, he expects personalized, prompt-generated software tailored to individual or enterprise needs, since coding agents make code essentially free. He calls Vercel a “yes-code” company, the inverse of the earlier no-code wave. Still, he says incumbents like Salesforce and SAP retain value as sources of record holding customer and operational data that generated software must query — describing Salesforce usage at Vercel itself happening entirely through an agent interface rather than the traditional UI, which he terms “headless.”

04 Security, sandboxes, and agent autonomy risk

Rauch flags agent autonomy — the shift from turn-by-turn ChatGPT interaction to long-running, goal-directed agents — as the defining change since ChatGPT’s launch, citing the OpenAI/Hugging Face sandbox-escape incident as a case where an agent pursued its mission with minimal restraint, raising prompt-injection risk (a hidden malicious instruction embedded somewhere an agent reads, like a YouTube comment). Vercel has built sandboxing/containment products and ran a $1 million bounty challenge inviting global attackers to break its sandbox; only one contributor, combining deep cybersecurity expertise with a large fleet of coordinated agents, made progress, surfacing a critical Linux vulnerability that Rauch calls a small but impactful contribution, which Vercel is now sharing with industry peers. Rauch predicts escalating conflict between offensive and defensive uses of AI in the near term, and says he opposes banning open models over cybersecurity fears since intelligence itself is what strengthens defense.

05 Open-model economics and frontier-lab risk

Rauch highlights the swing to 80% open-model token share on Vercel’s Gateway, crediting newly competitive open releases for tasks not requiring top-tier intelligence, while frontier models retain a role for high-stakes planning tasks. He warns frontier labs face commoditization and thin loyalty — comparing the dynamic to crypto speculation, complete with fake usage metrics and promotional subsidies — and questions why model labs are launching unrelated software products if their core intelligence advantage were durable. He declines to predict IPO timing, noting public markets reward predictable unit economics that AI model businesses currently lack.

06 Culture, hiring, and personal performance

Rauch says Vercel now expects much higher technical fluency even from sales roles, since agentic tools let salespeople “forward-deploy” technical work — citing a Mercedes F1 stack migration whose first iteration an agent turned around in minutes, work that used to take months. He still prioritizes strong human communication skills, arguing some interactions must remain maximally human even as agents absorb technical execution. Personally, he describes a back-to-basics performance philosophy (diet, walking, Brazilian Jiu Jitsu, no alcohol) over biohacking trends, and says he blurs work and family life, including with his six children. He closes by rejecting the idea that serverless is dying, instead describing a successor he calls “fluid compute” — elastic infrastructure spanning functions, sandboxes, and agents.

THE SIGNAL

  • Lambert’s Interconnects data shows Western open models win far more adoption than Chinese models of similar performance and size, even when the Chinese ones ship first, so Muse Spark Open could see outsized uptake despite ranking only 10th.
  • Lambert says other big open-model providers also use revenue gates like Meta’s, to set up revenue sharing on resold inference; this points to open-weight labs taxing inference providers’ margins (our read).
  • Meta’s Muse lineup on the index: Wonder Full 56, Wonder 52, Spark 1.3 48, Spark 1.2 40, Spark Open 39, so Meta open-sources a model about 17 points below its best and keeps the frontier closed.
  • Lambert floats Microsoft as a possible new frontier lab, citing strong scaling-science results, hyperscaler funding and direct access to OpenAI’s model IP, and sees Meta, Gemini and Microsoft as safer long-term bets because of their balance sheets.

01 Muse Spark Open’s debut and specs

Meta released Muse Spark Open, described in its model card as an 816B total-parameter, 42B active-parameter open-weights model with a 1 million token context window, built for coding and agentic work and natively multimodal. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index it scores 39, placing it above GLM 5.2 and near DeepSeek v4.1 Flash, but still behind GLM 5.3 and Kimi K3 — landing it 10th overall among open models globally, behind Chinese releases like MiMo V2.6 Pro, GLM 5.3, Kimi K3, and Step 5 Preview. Lambert notes the model underwent additional safety post-training, which some observers mistook for a broken release; he argues it’s more likely a safety-tuned version of the earlier Muse Spark 1.2 (a roughly one-point index drop), not Muse Spark 1.3, and recalls that Alexandr Wang had previously signaled Meta’s intent to open-source the 1.2-era model.

02 The license problem

Lambert details four restrictive terms in Muse Spark Open’s custom license: mandatory “Built with Muse” branding (a rule Meta and Kimi have tried before with little compliance), a revenue-gating clause requiring a separate Meta license above a revenue threshold, a binding acceptable-use policy, and ambiguous restrictions extending to model outputs and derivatives (raising synthetic-data legal questions). He calls this an unnecessary strategic risk for a lab trying to regain open-model adoption, though he notes licenses haven’t historically been decisive for AI adoption and many developers will likely ignore the terms except in egregious cases.

03 Meta’s closed models and lab tiering

Just before the open release, Meta also unveiled closed frontier models Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, which Lambert says land just behind Anthropic’s Opus 5.5 and roughly on par with OpenAI’s GPT-6-Astra and Google’s Gemini-4-Argon — making Meta the only US company fielding both frontier open and closed models (a strategy he compares to Alibaba’s Qwen, though more open-leaning). He ranks the field: Anthropic above OpenAI, above Meta (roughly tied with Gemini), above xAI (roughly level with the best Chinese open models), above the best US open models. Lambert attributes Anthropic’s lead to organizational consistency and vision, OpenAI’s to inconsistent execution offset by scientific leadership (o1-style inference-time scaling), and credits Meta’s recent momentum to Alexandr Wang’s high-ownership, high-compute-per-researcher structure despite managing over 200 direct reports — contrasted with Gemini’s reported bureaucratic friction and xAI’s talent-turnover risk. He remarks that misaligned incentives and layered bureaucracy are what typically kill labs.

04 Tier-three labs and unpredictability

Mistral’s newly announced Mistral Large 4 (index score 38, weights not yet released), along with Reflection, Nemotron, and similar “tier three” players, are described as increasingly unable to dent either the open or closed frontier, even as they retain enough performance to sustain standalone businesses — because frontier model-building costs now run into the billions per model, a bar only large firms can clear. Lambert adds that despite this scale of investment, labs still have a poor hit rate predicting real-world model success, citing Anthropic’s swing from a hit model to a reported dud (Opus 5) and back to a hit (Opus 5.5) as evidence that model quality remains fundamentally hard to forecast over the long run.

05 Valuation tension between Meta and Anthropic

Lambert closes on a financial observation: Meta is valued at under $2 trillion while Anthropic is approaching a similar valuation — despite Meta already possessing a highly profitable core business. He suggests one of the two valuations is due for a major correction and poses it as an open question what Anthropic’s public valuation might look like by November 2027 or 2028, warning that prolonged competition from Meta and others could pressure prospective AI IPO valuations.
