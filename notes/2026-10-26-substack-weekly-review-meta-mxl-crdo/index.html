Now some candid thoughts on the state of the market.

The volatility and the reactions to every headline are wearing people down. Prices can look very attractive, but there is always that concern that they could become a lot more attractive next week.

I still believe Iran and its impact on oil are driving a lot of the pressure. Higher energy costs create problems well beyond the businesses directly involved. Then you layer on concerns about AI financing and whether all this spending will generate an adequate return, and high-growth stocks become especially vulnerable.

My view is that a meaningful easing of the conflict would give the market more room to look past individual AI headlines. I could be wrong about how much that would change things, but it remains a big part of how I’m interpreting this selloff.

The earnings we have received so far have generally been very solid and constructive. But we’re also seeing that good results can be followed by another drop in the stock. We went through something similar in July, and it is frustrating when the business delivers what you were looking for and the market still sells it.

In the networking update, I talked about how strong execution can coexist with lower valuations. We can be right about a business growing and still underestimate how little the market will be willing to pay for that growth. This correction has been a pretty uncomfortable reminder of that.

The AI trade feels increasingly fragile to me.

There have always been concerns. For a long time, the market moved past them fairly quickly. Lately, those concerns seem to stick around longer, and each new headline has more potential to set things off again. I’m less comfortable assuming we’ll return to a market that consistently gives these businesses the benefit of the doubt.

I also think the market has become too focused on OpenAI and Anthropic as the scorecard for the entire AI opportunity. Their progress is very important, especially given the infrastructure commitments involved. But there is a much wider range of applications and businesses benefiting from and spending on AI.

That being said, I continue to see enormous potential here. And of the investment themes I know well, AI still offers some of the most attractive opportunities I can identify.

That is why I have slowly been adding to several of my AI positions this past week, and why I continue to add. At these prices, I want more exposure to opportunities where I believe the potential return justifies the risk. I know how quickly these stocks can move when sentiment improves, and I want to participate if that happens.

What has changed is how much of that exposure I want to keep through the next cycle of enthusiasm and fear.

I can want to own more at today’s prices and also intend to reduce more aggressively if we get a substantial rally. Those decisions fit together. The price I’m paying today helps determine whether I want to add. The price and expectations after a strong run will help determine how much I want to keep.

Some of these stocks have fallen to levels I genuinely didn’t expect to see without something much more serious breaking. I clearly underestimated how far valuations could compress. Many did. That is something I need to learn from. Either way, I have to account for the downside the market has shown it is capable of, even when I think the selling has gone too far.

For months, I have generally maintained a core position and traded around the edges. I still want to do that, but I want the part I manage more actively to become a larger share of these positions.

Where I might previously have thought about a position as roughly 70% core and 30% to trade around, I’m now thinking closer to 30% core and 70% that I’m willing to adjust as the opportunity changes. I’m talking about how I manage exposure within these AI positions.

I still want a core in my highest-conviction opportunities. I also want more flexibility to take money off the table when prices run, rather than feeling committed to carrying most of the position through whatever comes next.

There is a trade-off. I could reduce something that continues much higher. I could add before another leg down. This approach won’t remove those risks, and I don’t want to pretend that I’ll get the timing right every time. But it better reflects how I want to manage my capital after what we’ve seen.

At the same time, I’m continuing to look for steady compounders with earnings drivers beyond high-growth tech. The CAT article is an example of where that research is taking me. Caterpillar still has cyclical risk and exposure to AI infrastructure spending, but it also has a much broader business and an installed base that generates ongoing parts and service demand.

I want to build positions in businesses I would be happy owning for years, including after the AI trade recovers. That gives me more places to invest without needing every opportunity to depend on the same spending cycle or investor sentiment.

This is also why I wanted to make my portfolio visible.

I spend a lot of time writing about individual businesses. Showing my allocation adds context to that research. You can see where I’m willing to put capital, how much exposure I’m comfortable with and how that changes as prices and my thinking evolve.

I want this page to be a place where we can explore ideas and different ways of looking at the market. Sharing how I’m personally navigating it is part of that. It doesn’t establish that my approach will be the right one, and my decision to invest in something shouldn’t be the sole reason someone else does.

If you find the portfolio useful, it is there to help you understand my thinking more clearly. If you’re here for the research and have no interest in following my positioning, you can continue using the page exactly that way.

TLDR: Right now, I’m adding selectively to AI while planning to be more willing to reduce that exposure into strength. Alongside that, I’m building toward a broader portfolio that I can feel comfortable holding through different markets. That is how I’m choosing to navigate this, and I want you to understand the reasoning behind it.

Hopefully Anthropic showing an acceleration in September could give some fuel.

META Earnings Thoughts

A big part of why I want to own Meta is that AI could make the advertising business they already have much more valuable. They already reach billions of people, and businesses already pay to reach those people. If AI helps an advertiser get more sales from its budget, that business has a reason to spend more with Meta.

If I’m a business paying for advertising, what I care about is whether that spending produces a good return. If Meta gets better at finding the right customers for me, I can pay more for an ad and still come out ahead. That gives Meta room to increase what it earns while also making the service more valuable to the advertiser.

Management said improvements to its ad systems increased app-ad conversions by 9.6%. Meta also reported higher ad impressions and higher prices per ad. Better recommendations can keep people engaged, while better ad performance gives advertisers a reason to keep spending. AI is contributing to those improvements, although management also credited better macro conditions for some of the increase in pricing.

I’m also excited about where Meta is taking Muse. If I ask an agent to help me find something and buy it, that agent becomes involved in the decision and the transaction. Meta already helps businesses find customers through advertising. With Muse, it could help more of those interactions turn into completed purchases and develop new ways to earn from them.

And they are building the business side too. I can imagine a personal agent that understands what a customer wants working with a business agent that knows what the business can provide. Meta’s existing relationships through advertising and WhatsApp give it a strong starting point. More than 10 million people in the US and Canada have now set up Muse agents, with roughly a quarter using them on a given day. I think there is a lot of potential here as the product improves and people find more useful ways to use it.

A big part of why I want to own Meta is what the cash generation could look like as more of this infrastructure starts earning its keep. Right now, a huge amount of the cash coming in is going straight back out to fund the expansion. What I’m looking for is the business continuing to grow while Meta gets more use out of the capacity it has already paid for. If the pace of new spending then slows, or eventually comes down, Meta could retain a much larger share of the cash it generates.

And capex doesn’t have to fall outright for that to become attractive. Operating cash flow could grow faster than capex, leaving more cash after investment even while Meta continues expanding.

Some reported numbers:

  • Q3 revenue was $64.6B, up 26% YoY. Ad impressions increased 10%, while average price per ad increased 14%.
  • Reported operating profit was $13.0B, with EPS of $3.83. This included a $9.89B legal charge. Excluding that charge, operating profit was approximately $22.9B, up about 11% YoY. EPS excluding the charge and its tax effect was $7.40.
  • I calculate an operating margin of roughly 35.4% excluding the charge, compared with approximately 40% a year earlier.
  • Operating cash flow was $32.8B against $34.9B of capex, including finance-lease principal payments. That left negative $2.1B of FCF.
  • Q4 revenue guidance is $73–76B. Full-year capex guidance is now $135–145B. Meta raised the bottom from $130B and left the top unchanged.

There is also a separate $25B New Mexico judgment that Meta intends to appeal. That is different from the $9.89B charge in these results and remains a potentially significant financial obligation.

I don’t want to explain away the profit pressure as just a legal charge. Even after removing it, operating profit grew about 11% against 26% revenue growth. And FCF moved from slightly positive in Q2 to negative this quarter because infrastructure spending increased faster than operating cash flow.

My original thesis already allowed for heavy spending. I’m looking for Meta to keep growing while the infrastructure it has built supports more of that growth. If capex slows because demand disappoints, we would have to revisit the revenue assumptions too.

And lower capex wouldn’t immediately improve every number. Equipment still needs replacing, and depreciation can keep weighing on earnings after the cash has been spent. The most direct benefit would be to FCF. That could be meaningful even while the income statement continues absorbing the cost of the earlier build.

I found the analyst reaction interesting because even firms seeing stronger revenue reached different conclusions about what the stock should be worth. BofA raised its target from $810 to $845 while slightly lowering its 2027 adjusted EPS estimate from $34.24 to $34.00. Piper raised its revenue estimates but cut its target from $875 to $850 because it expects higher spending. That is a useful reminder that a higher price target doesn’t necessarily mean higher near-term earnings. Analysts are also making assumptions about how valuable the longer-term opportunity becomes.

As a shareholder, I’m still encouraged by the direction. The advertising business is improving, and I’m excited about how Muse could make Meta more useful to both consumers and businesses. I’m willing to own it through this spending period because I think those opportunities could support a larger business that eventually retains much more of the cash it generates. I don’t know exactly when that becomes visible in the financials. But that potential cash generation is a meaningful part of what I’m investing for, and I want to judge this spending against that opportunity rather than look at the size of the bill alone.

MXL

MXL had a very, very strong quarter.

The optical business is becoming large enough to make a meaningful difference to profitability. That was an important part of the thesis. MXL has already spent a lot developing these products, so if shipments grow without operating expenses increasing at the same pace, earnings can grow much faster than revenue. We are starting to see that happen.

MXL also raised its 2026 optical revenue outlook from $210–230M to $250–270M. That is an approximately 18% increase at the midpoint. I think that deserves attention because the outlook is improving before the larger 1.6T opportunity begins contributing.

With 1.6T, MXL can potentially earn more from each optical module. Management expects the selling price of its DSP to be roughly double the 800G version. It can also sell the receiver amplifier alongside it, giving MXL more content in the same module.

Initial 1.6T shipments are expected in the first half of 2027, with a stronger second half and a larger contribution in

  1. Management also expects 800G demand to continue as 1.6T ramps. So there is an opportunity to grow the existing

business while adding a new generation of products with more revenue per module.

I’m also interested in seeing how the AEC opportunity plays out and what it could mean for CRDO in the long run. MXL is supplying chips to cable manufacturers rather than selling the complete cable itself. That could help those manufacturers compete with Credo’s integrated offering, but it also means MXL earns the chip content rather than the full cable revenue. Initial revenue is expected in 2027, followed by a stronger ramp in 2028.

Let’s look at some numbers.

  • Q3 revenue was $218M, up 72% YoY and 29% QoQ.
  • Adjusted EPS was $0.62, up from $0.35 in Q2.
  • Adjusted gross margin reached 60%. Adjusted operating margin increased to 29.5%, from 22.3% in Q2.
  • Q4 revenue guidance is $240–260M, with adjusted gross margin of 59–62% and adjusted operating expenses of $65–70M.

Using those Q4 midpoints, I calculate roughly $84M of adjusted operating profit and a 33.5% operating margin. That would be approximately 30% operating-profit growth on about 15% revenue growth QoQ.

That is very, very impressive. More of the additional revenue is reaching operating profit, and the next-quarter guidance suggests that could continue.

There is still a cash cost to supporting the ramp. Operating cash flow was negative $13.6M in Q3, with wafer prepayments contributing to the outflow. MXL is committing money to secure supply before collecting the revenue from the finished products.

The longer-term risk I think we need to consider is the DSP business. Some CPO and linear optical designs reduce the need for standalone optical DSPs. As those designs gain adoption, optical demand could keep growing while MXL captures less revenue from each connection. That could influence how much investors are willing to pay for its future earnings.

The timing of that transition is important though. If broad CPO adoption takes longer than we think, MXL has more time to grow its DSP business and earn a return on the development spending it has already made. Its receiver amplifiers give it a way to participate as architectures change, although we still need to see how much of the DSP profit opportunity those products could replace.

In my September article, I was more optimistic about the earnings potential than consensus, but I didn’t want the upside to depend on the market continuing to pay an extremely high multiple. This quarter gives us more evidence on the earnings side of that argument.

MXL currently trades at roughly 14.8× the current consensus estimate of $4.30 in adjusted EPS for 2027. I think that looks inexpensive for the growth being projected, although those estimates still require earnings to more than double from 2026. If MXL delivers something close to that and can sustain those earnings, the investment case doesn’t need an extremely high multiple to work.

I think MXL had an amazing quarter, and I think there could be several more strong quarters ahead. Profitability is improving before the bigger 1.6T ramp arrives, and the higher optical outlook adds to my confidence. I’m encouraged by that progress while keeping the longer-term DSP risk in mind.
