SUMMARY

Overview

The expert, a former technical program manager at the subject company, discussed its plan to supply power to satellites through an in-orbit network. Satellites today rely mainly on onboard solar panels and batteries, which constrain available power and can require them to spend time charging rather than performing their missions.

The proposed system would collect solar energy, convert it into electricity, and beam energy as light to other satellites’ existing solar panels. The expert said the solar collection and receiving components are established technologies; the main innovation is delivering power safely and effectively between spacecraft.

Key Insights

The expert identified high-power laser systems that can operate in space as the central technical hurdle. Vacuum compatibility, heat removal, limited onboard power, and radiation protection also require engineering, but were described as more conventional challenges. A ground demonstration showed the team could combine existing technologies quickly and recorded a high received-power result. It did not establish that the system was ready for space operation. The expert characterized the first orbital effort as an incremental test, with the company’s satellite launched but no public confirmation of the power-beaming demonstration’s outcome. Checkout, regulatory approval, and a limited deployment opportunity can extend testing timelines. Existing satellite solar panels may need multiple laser wavelengths to generate meaningful power. The expert said developing suitable high-power beams across those wavelengths remains an important research challenge. The expert stressed that success is far from assured: proving a technology, building a useful product, and deploying a large constellation are distinct milestones. Space projects require substantial capital, and failures can significantly disrupt progress.
