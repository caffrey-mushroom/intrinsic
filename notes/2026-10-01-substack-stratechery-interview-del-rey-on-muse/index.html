An Interview with Jason Del Rey About Muse, Amazon, and Walmart

Thursday, October 1, 2026

Good morning,

This week’s Stratechery Interview is with The Aisle author Jason Del Rey. Del Rey has been covering the e-commerce space for years, including a decade at AllThingsD and Recode. In 2023 Del Rey published Winner Sells All: Amazon, Walmart, and the Battle for Our Wallets, tracing the multi-decade battle between Walmart and Amazon. Then, late last year, Del Rey went independent, starting his own e-commerce focused publication called The Aisle.

In this interview we cover Del Rey’s background, including a brief history of Recode, before diving into his area of expertise and one of my areas of fascination: Walmart and Amazon. I loved exploring the history of these companies, and you can make the case that the news of the last week — Amazon blocking Muse, while Walmart signed up — is actually just a continuation of a battle Del Rey has been on the front lines for. We also discuss agents generally, groceries, Google, and Shopify, and why Del Rey is optimistic The Aisle will be his last job.

Walmart

Well, you wrote a book in 2023 entitled Winner Sells All: Amazon, Walmart, and the Battle for Our Wallets. What was the overall story you were trying to tell, and why did you feel that was the right time to tell it?

JDR: I told myself I would only ever write a book if I felt like I was the best, or one of the best, people to tell it. Which seems obvious, but I don’t know that it always is to authors.

I really was one of the few — maybe the only — at the time diving deep into both companies. I started diving deep into Walmart once they acquired Jet.com and really did a big acqui-hire of the entrepreneur Marc Lore. I had followed him since I started covering e-commerce, and just thought it was fascinating to see them try to reinvent themselves.

But what I was trying to get at in the book was a real explanation of why they missed the opportunity for so many years and, “allowed Amazon to become Amazon”. It’s obviously more nuanced than that, they really just sort of passed on it.

What is this story? Actually, I think Walmart is super interesting. I’ve covered them, not a massive amount, but more than you would expect for a technology site, because I do think it’s super interesting. Both their many years of failures, and then also the way I think they’ve changed the business. But you’re the expert, walk me through the whole thing, I think Walmart looms very large going forward, so it’s very pertinent.

JDR: They do indeed. I found one of the earliest Walmart software engineers who was basically the leader of the skunkworks team back in the mid-1990s that was investing in e-commerce. He hadn’t talked to the press, I think, ever, so I was very excited to talk to him.

He basically told me they saw the future, they were experimenting — they were selling gift baskets online for Sam’s Club in the 1990s and other experiments. He went into the office of the then-CEO, David Glass), and basically said, “Listen, I don’t need a huge investment, I just need the other divisions, when I ask them for help, to not laugh in my face about what I’m doing”. And the story he told — David Glass has long since passed — was that Glass looked him in the eye and basically said, “This online thing is cool, but it’ll never be bigger than one year of sales at a Sam’s Club store”. This guy, Robert Davis, ended up leaving and going to work for Amazon for about a dozen years — not in super high-profile roles, but in critical internal roles.

So part of it was just not taking the opportunity seriously enough, but once they did, there were a lot of internal turf wars over who would get credit for sales. I think the internal incentives, until really the pandemic, for executives leading e-commerce versus physical stores were not aligned in a way that made them want to work together. Those two things for sure. And did they have the technical talent? Maybe not at certain parts of the 2000s, but in the early and mid-90s they really did.

How much of it was the assumption that, “We’re already the best in retail logistics in the world, this will be easy” — and it turned out the logistics for stores was way different than the logistics for e-commerce?

JDR: That’s absolutely right. I talked to a bunch of old-time Amazon folks and they kept saying, “We were waiting and waiting for Walmart to turn their stores into an e-commerce advantage, and they just never did”. It is difficult — even now today, real-time store inventory, someone ordering online for pickup and showing up when someone just walked out the door with the last one, there were all these complications. But Amazon was truthfully amazed for the better part of two decades that Walmart didn’t — until the pandemic forced them — turn the stores into the real advantage they could be for online shopping.

It’s interesting because — correct me if I’m wrong, you’re much deeper in the space than I am — my view is all the things you listed make total sense, they didn’t take it seriously, once they did, you had all the internal turf wars and those issues, and I think part of the Jet.com acquisition was “Let’s build an e-commerce entity completely independent from Walmart because it won’t work internally”, but it also feels to me — and I don’t know if this is right or wrong — that stores didn’t work for e-commerce because delivering and distributing goods to consumers individually versus to stores are similar but very different problems, that was my take for a long time. But to your point, today part of why Walmart is a great e-commerce experience is they are leveraging the stores. Did that only work because they built e-commerce on the side under Marc Lore and then glued that on? Or did COVID come along and save them from themselves, and they could have gotten there much earlier?

JDR: I mean, the answer is yes — there’s a little of all of that. Mark coming in — a lot of people criticized, and still criticize, that acquisition, $3.3 billion at the time, mainly to get Marc Lore and a few of his executives in. But what he did was change the narrative. He really did a good job changing the narrative, both on Wall Street and in hiring circles.

But he also brought the metabolism and the launch speed of new things — really, expanding the marketplace. Yes, following in the footsteps of Amazon, but expanding the marketplace that they would ship from their warehouses or their partner warehouses. I think that in combination with the pandemic forcing them to launch curbside and just focus on it and invest in it in a real way — I think there’s part of the answer in those different areas.

I think people don’t realize that Walmart has a marketplace like Amazon does, almost every item — well, not almost, but a lot of the items you find on Amazon that you feel you can only get on Amazon are actually also on Walmart, usually sold by the exact same sellers. It’s almost like that gave the foundation, and now you can layer on the stores, which are fundamentally limited. Walmart stores have an unbelievable number of SKUs, but it’s always going to be a limited number relative to what Amazon has, or what an entire marketplace is going to have, and then you get the experience of, “Walmart has everything, and some of the stuff you can get in an hour”, it’s a surprisingly large amount of stuff you can get in an hour. I tell people this, and I think the current advertising campaign for Walmart — “Who knew?” — is perfect.

JDR: Yeah.

People don’t know — this is actually an incredible e-commerce experience for a whole bunch of products.

JDR: Yeah. We in the Del Rey household we mainly use the curbside product, and it’s just very well done. Part of the problem I have with our local one — which is actually next to Teterboro Airport in New Jersey, which is funny enough where Walmart executives fly in, and it’s usually the first store they visit — is there’s honestly not enough parking spaces for all the pickup orders, because you also have their delivery network picking up in the same space.

Amazing.

JDR: I haven’t been there in a few months, so maybe they’ve fixed that. But no, it shocks a lot of people who live on the coasts. There’s a reason they’ve done very well on the market over the last five, six years, and part of it is that they’ve figured out the pickup business.

JDR: I mean, it’s a return hub now, I guess, and I think they’re trying to bring pricing down there. Honestly, I’ll be candid that in the last year or two I’ve not paid as much attention to Whole Foods as I did.

That’s the answer — you weren’t ready for my question because it doesn’t really matter. Well, actually, I want to — you mentioned grocery, which I think is really interesting. To go back to the Walmart question, how important was grocery for Walmart in terms of figuring this out and actually mounting a credible challenge to Amazon?

JDR: Hugely important, I think.

Amazon had it right, they were right to be worried, and they were right to realize they needed to get grocery, and here’s Walmart.

JDR: In my book I tell some stories about how Walmart used to own a UK grocer called Asda, the UK was very early to click-and-collect and online grocery delivery, so Walmart ended up importing a bunch of executives from that team to get online grocery off the ground here. They started with delivery, and it was like, “Oh, this is really expensive, we’re not sure this will work”, Walmart wasn’t a big fan of money-losing expensive things at the time. And I think, again, not to give too much credit to the pandemic, but it was a big factor, they then saw the need for grocery pickup.

JDR: Yes, absolutely, and I got lucky to spend some time with Doug for the book, and I thought he was pretty transparent and candid about where they were ahead of the curve, where they weren’t, and where they got a little bit lucky.

Actually, one more question on this, I think the grocery thing is fascinating. It’s like they’re out-Walmarting Walmart. Because Walmart, their retail store, as you said, is limited — but they’re also pretty good for a retail location in how many SKUs they have. Everyday Essentials is a very small number, and it turned out that was the key, it’s really interesting.

JDR: Yeah. If you look at the history, I spoke to Doug Herrington, who is now Amazon’s — he’s basically the guy who runs the retail business, CEO of Worldwide Amazon Stores or whatever they call it. Right.

JDR: You buy a TV every couple years, you buy a camera whatever, but this stuff you buy several times a week.

They’ve just been relentless, decade over decade, trying different paths. We’ll see if this is the end state or not, but investing in the warehousing network, plus trying again and again different ways to make the small incremental purchases make sense — it feels like they’ve gotten there in the last year or two.

What’s interesting about this Everyday Essentials, and you sort of drew this distinction between buying a TV — buying a TV is a fun experience, you do research, you look at all the reviews, it’s an entertainment experience beyond just being an entertainment device. And this applies to a lot of things you might buy. It applies to booking a trip — for most people, they’re not flying every single week, they want to go out there and plan, the anticipation is part of the experience. That’s one category that tech demos tend to focus on and think about. On the other hand, there’s buying milk and eggs and paper towels — things you just do that are not fun, they just need to be done and to your point, Amazon realized these are a continual touchpoint with consumers. If we don’t own that touchpoint, someone else is going to, and they can expand into these other areas.

Agents

Did I just explain the entire agent debate by talking about this specific issue?

JDR: (laughing) Maybe! That’s sort of how I’ve been thinking about it recently — trying to figure out the boundaries for where a personal shopping agent does and doesn’t make sense. Not in a VC’s life, or a super AI-pilled researcher in Silicon Valley’s life, but in an everyday person’s life.

Because it feels like the agent thing — there’s the chatbot angle, where it’s great for planning trips and all those sorts of things and at the end of the day the conversion is almost beside the point, because there’s so much else that goes into it. I’ve made the case that shopping is fun — people do it because it’s enjoyable, it’s not enjoyable to buy paper towels. But to your point, it’s probably underrated that the thing people buy the most is actually everyday essentials, and why not have your agent buy it? Particularly the whole CPG category is predicated on you getting attached to one brand and buying it until you die. Why not have your agent do it? It’s not like it’s fun to buy Old Spice deodorant.

JDR: Yeah, it’s interesting. We have a mutual industry friend in Michael Morton, the analyst over at MoffettNathanson. He had some report — I don’t know when exactly — looking at the Subscribe & Save penetration in Amazon’s business, it was actually a little smaller than you might think, which kind of complicates this discussion.

No, it’s great. It’s smaller than you think, and the Dash Button — which I thought was awesome — totally failed. Are there takeaways from that?

JDR: Well, I think about my own life as a consumer. My wife isn’t in tech or media, so I have, like, a normie living with me, which is helpful for what I cover. I’m sure she would love that’s what I’m saying she’s helpful for.

Oh that’s fine, my wife ends up as fodder for these takes all the time, it’s very useful.

JDR: I’ve thought long and hard about why we aren’t a Subscribe & Save family. For us, at least, little things come up — a trip here, something changes in our kids’ lives and we don’t need that thing as much. I just don’t want to end up with too much or not enough of something. And Amazon has made it, without AI, easy enough that I can get that thing later today or tomorrow anyway. So what are the takeaways for AI agents from that?

Well, no, just to double down on that point. I was in Taiwan for a long time, there’s no Subscribe & Save there, but e-commerce — everything was 24 hours, because it’s small and dense. There’s just a lot of advantage that comes from that, I think it’s underappreciated what delivery in the U.S. means when it’s very large and very spread out. So I moved back, and I’m like, “Oh, Subscribe & Save, that’s going to be great”, so I did it for a bunch of items, and I lasted maybe a month because it was too hard to calibrate. Suddenly I’m in my garage and I have like 13 cases of sparkling water, and I’m like, “This is super annoying”. I think, to your point, it’s because it’s too dumb. It’s not necessarily a counter to say AI agents are going to fail — it might be the case for why they’re going to be compelling.

JDR: Yeah, it might be. And I actually just published a newsletter looking back at Walmart and this little internal startup they had called Jetblack.

Yeah, I was going to ask you about that. I’ve never heard of that before.

JDR: Well, great, I’m glad I wrote about it. It was, I think, partially Marc Lore’s idea for a long time — a personal concierge service. They spun it up in New York, they started actually by targeting wealthy moms, which didn’t make a lot of sense for Walmart proper, but I think they saw consumers who would actually pay for such a service.

It was money-losing, but not more than it should have been. It was basically “text something and get it delivered to you that same day.” You know, “I need size three diapers”, to talk about something in the everyday essentials space, and it would show up later that day. They started building some technology around it, but this is late 2010s, so this is pre-everything we’re talking about today.

People loved the service — a small customer base in New York City — but they were losing gobs of money. Walmart essentially was like, “We’re not going to keep dumping money into this thing”, so I talk about this in my book, Marc Lore went out and found $300 million of outside capital from, I think, FedEx, Visa, and Microsoft, and they were going to spin it off, but they needed Walmart’s blessing, Walmart would have owned 50% of it. They thought it was a done deal, Lore and team, then at the last minute, Doug McMillon decided against it.

The view from the other side was that he was fearful of funding something that might end up being a real competitor down the line. He told me, when I asked him about it, that he wanted to keep the technology for internal use, and that eventually you’d see Jetblack bear fruit in services that Walmart would launch, that was about four years ago when I talked to him.

It all came up in my mind because last week Amazon blocks Muse, and then Walmart is one of the partners of Muse. I had to reach out to the former CEO of Jetblack, a woman named Jenny Fleiss, and I had to say, “You must be thinking about the what-ifs”, and she’s like, “Oh, absolutely, all the customers keep reaching out saying ‘what could have been'”. Obviously a lot could have changed between then and now.

I think though this is a broader point. In the dot-com era, a lot of the ideas were right, but they depended on everyone needing to be connected — not just connected at home, but connected on the go. Once everyone had phones and was always connected to the Internet, all these ideas that seemed ridiculous in the late 90s made sense. And that’s what I’m hearing about this Jetblack service — it made total sense, but it didn’t make sense until you had an AI agent so you could actually do it profitably and to your point, people complained about token costs or inference costs, they had people on the other side, right? It’s a lot cheaper to do it this way.

JDR: Right. And the prime example of “the 90s idea until the technology was there” — and there are many — is Instacart a great one. Until the mobile phone and the gig-worker economy, it didn’t work.

Which depends on the mobile phone, to your point.

JDR: Correct. And so the Webvan and Kozmo jokes of yesteryear — is this another one that we’ll be looking back on? I thought it was interesting to look back from now, but we’ll see.
