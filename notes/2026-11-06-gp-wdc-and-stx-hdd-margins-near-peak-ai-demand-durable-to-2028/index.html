SUMMARY

Overview

The expert discussed the outlook for hard disk drives (HDDs), especially nearline storage used by cloud providers and AI infrastructure. The central question was whether strong demand, constrained supply, and long-term customer agreements can sustain pricing and high margins. The discussion also covered NAND flash as an alternative, technology roadmaps, and the competitive positions of Seagate, Western Digital, and Toshiba.

Key Insights

AI-related storage demand appears durable through 2028, supported by existing customer commitments and the low cost of HDDs for retaining large data sets. The expert expects growth to slow rather than collapse if hyperscaler spending moderates. The expert views current gross margins as near a peak. Renewals, new industry capacity, and greater customer negotiating power could pressure prices from 2028 onward. Seagate has a real HAMR technology lead, while Western Digital benefits from established ePMR production, lower near-term technology risk, and in-house head and media manufacturing. Toshiba’s capacity expansion could change industry pricing if it arrives on schedule. Its ability to ramp depends partly on access to head components and technology. Faster HDD designs may help with warm data, but are unlikely to close the gap with SSDs for latency-sensitive uses. AI inference and retrieval workloads are more exposed to QLC NAND substitution than cold archives and bulk video.

Competitive Landscape

Company or technology

Position and factors discussed

Seagate

Leads in HAMR development and has internal drive-testing capability. Its reliance on external head suppliers is a potential vulnerability.

Western Digital

Competes effectively using proven ePMR and Ultra-SMR, with lower near-term technology risk. In- house heads and media may support future cost and margin advantages.

Toshiba

A smaller HDD competitor with potential to add significant capacity. Expansion depends on component supply, qualification, and possible arrangements involving TDK.

NAND / QLC SSDs

Offer advantages in speed, power, and some warm-data workloads, but remain substantially more expensive per terabyte than HDDs.
