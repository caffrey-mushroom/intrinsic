01 Data centers under pressure

Faber opens on whether community opposition and moratoriums threaten the AI buildout pace. Dell characterizes the increased community engagement — over siting, power sourcing, and water-efficient liquid cooling — as a positive development, and says Dell has a large project backlog with no material delays. He notes data centers and workload demand are now global, not just US-concentrated, and sees mitigation options like more efficient systems and modular data centers. Pressed twice on whether this threatens 2027 guidance, Dell says he sees no impediment and no change to expectations, declining to speculate on whether post-midterm politics will ease pressure.

02 Safety debate and government’s role

Faber frames the AI safety spectrum between Huang’s lighter-touch, lab-responsibility view and Gates’s characterization of AI as among the most dangerous technologies ever. Dell compares AI to fire — capable of great benefit or harm — and says labs are already thinking hard about safeguards, with a role for government, bounded permissions for AI agents, monitoring, and human oversight. He stresses open-source and smaller models give customers choice beyond frontier models. On US-China competition, Dell argues that opting out of building AI and data centers would hurt the US economy while benefiting rival economies, favoring staying ahead while finding a path to safe, powerful systems. He says he isn’t a policymaker but engages with them, and places himself generally in Huang’s camp while noting Dell builds infrastructure, not models — infrastructure itself has no awareness of its own use. Asked directly if he worries about existential AI risk, he predicts things will be fine a few years out and says humans are good at creating countermeasures to anticipated risks.

03 Pricing, supply and the bubble question

On GPU economics, Dell distinguishes smaller systems running on less powerful infrastructure from frontier training systems requiring the latest, costlier semiconductors and memory. He says demand for compute remains greater than supply, describes the industry as still early in its growth curve, and acknowledges any tech cycle mixes excess, opportunity, and skepticism. Asked directly if this is a bubble, Dell says there isn’t enough supply for it to be one, and that Dell prioritizes orders based on evidence customers will actually use the capacity rather than taking every speculative order. His key bubble indicator is watching rental prices for older chips like the H100 — a one-year-high print signals genuine compute scarcity, not pre-positioning. He flags the roughly four-year fab lead time as a structural constraint, implying the supply gap could persist for years, with memory in particular tightening further ahead.

04 Compute-to-intelligence shift and Dell’s own transformation

Dell elaborates on his public claim that only 10-15% of companies grasp the organizational rewiring AI requires. He says firms typically try to bolt new technology onto legacy processes rather than reimagining outcomes, data architecture, and simplified/standardized processes before applying AI. Dell says his own company has executed this transition over three years, reflected in improved operating leverage, with further changes expected ahead. Faber connects this to Dell’s headcount falling by 11,000 to roughly 97,000 even as revenue rose 19%, though Dell reframes the benefit as speed and capacity expansion rather than just headcount efficiency.

05 Financing and philanthropy

Faber cites figures showing Dell earned $4.1 billion in income last quarter but only $1 billion in free cash flow, with receivables rising as Dell finances customer purchases. Dell says most of this financing is short-term (three to four months) and gets handed off to banks and lenders. On philanthropy, Faber notes Dell and his wife have donated $6.25 billion directed to certain ZIP codes; Dell says the foundation he created in 1999 has focused on children in urban poverty for twenty-seven years and confirms more giving is planned both domestically and internationally (97% of children live outside the US), leaving open whether future giving extends to Invest America accounts.

01 Forge: origin and architecture

Forge is CoreWeave’s new unified platform for building and running agentic AI systems, announced at the Fully Connected conference. It stitches together previously separate assets — Weights & Biases models, Weave, inference/serverless, training, and OpenPipe — into one workflow following a loop of run, observe, curate, improve, evaluate. Goldberg says the idea emerged organically from two internal projects: CoreWeave’s own agentic research system (internally referenced as ARIA) and a speculative-decoding optimization effort for its inference service. In both cases, engineers — she references one named Aaron — found that manually stitching tools together across the loop left the human in the loop worn down, and that machine-learning engineers and developers were no longer cleanly separated roles. CoreWeave frames its advantage as full-stack, purpose-built-for-AI optimization, arguing that just as training jobs spanning thousands of GPUs required unified tooling, agentic inference workloads (always-on, running on large GPU fleets) demand the same approach. Forge is available to all users immediately, without the traditional sales and invoicing cycle — a notable shift for a company historically reliant on enterprise sales relationships, alongside a related self-serve sandboxes product that now includes GPU access.

02 Fragmentation risk in the agent ecosystem

Goldberg’s central cautionary argument, drawn from a fireside conversation with Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis, is that the AI industry risks repeating — or worsening — the standardization failures Kubernetes avoided. She argues Kubernetes succeeded in part because the community deliberately built shared APIs and best practices that prevented fragmentation; in today’s agentic AI world, by contrast, rapid model and chip churn means teams are forced to rewrite systems every few months with no equivalent investment in shared standards. She says she’d like to see frontier and open-weight models coexist within composable, flexible frameworks rather than each vendor building closed, bespoke stacks — an aspiration she presents as a hope, not a prediction.

03 Engineering identity in the agent era

Goldberg discusses an identity crisis she sees among software engineers now that much code is agent-generated: engineers feel less ownership and recognition for work when agents produce it, and worry about ideas being copied once agentized. She argues the professionals thriving most are domain experts — critical thinkers who can communicate well through prompts because of deep subject knowledge — but notes a looming problem: expertise is traditionally built through roughly 10,000 hours of hands-on repetition, a pathway increasingly bypassed as agents take over execution. She credits a CoreWeave inference-team engineer, Rita, with emphasizing that teams need to stay curious rather than lazy to keep developing expertise. Host Alex Volkov (who said on tape he joined CoreWeave via the Weights & Biases acquisition) shares his own view that he once expected to become redundant as an engineer but now believes expertise is more valuable than ever, since most people are effectively managing fleets of agents like a hybrid product-manager-and-engineering-manager role.

04 Infrastructure proof points and partnerships

Goldberg points to Caterpillar’s Physical AI VP, Brandon Hootman, as an example of enterprises choosing to build AI capability in-house on CoreWeave after an earlier attempt to partner externally failed, leveraging Caterpillar’s machine and site data. Volkov says CoreWeave was the first company to deploy Vera Rubin NVL72, citing a claimed 4.8x improvement in token throughput over Blackwell, and calls out Cognition’s Devin coding agent; Goldberg adds that the ecosystem grows when teams use tools others build rather than everyone building everything themselves.

05 Sentiment on AI and personal agent use

Both speakers discuss a broader shift in public and enterprise sentiment, with Goldberg noting that organizational demand for AI tools is now driven bottom-up by teams eager to experiment rather than top-down mandates, across customers like Caterpillar, MasterClass, and Canva. She acknowledges public concerns about security, data sharing, and job impact as valid, but frames platforms like CoreWeave’s as helping make AI adoption secure and accessible. On personal use, Goldberg jokes about running multiple AI assistants and expresses hope for future consolidation and efficiency rather than everyone building redundant personal agent stacks, noting that people increasingly become the limiting factor for their own agents rather than the other way around.

THE SIGNAL

  • Jones says he no longer holds the hyperscaler and AI tech stocks he was buying last May; his plan is near-term weakness, a year-end rally possibly helped by Anthropic’s listing, then likely being short for all of 2027.
  • Crowding-out framework: heavy hyperscaler debt issuance already pushed down corporate bond prices, and he sees the same supply now moving into equities through SpaceX, OpenAI, Anthropic and later unlocks, which he compares to 2000-02.
  • He puts 60/40 odds on a sovereign-debt breaking point; AI build-out debt near 3.6% of GDP would be 50% above railroads and triple highways, which is why he thinks this time differs from past can-kicking.
  • Oil long rationale: a gentle Iran fix would have happened before the midterms, so he sees a tougher post-midterm resolution hanging over oil, which supports upside (our read: a hedge against his equity-bearish calls).

01 The 1999 analogy and market positioning

Jones, speaking live to CNBC’s Andrew Ross Sorkin at Good Shepherd Services, reiterates a view he first offered a year earlier: the market still feels like 1999, bifurcated between companies embracing AI and those lagging. At a recent conference of roughly 200 people, Jones asked how many had been trading in 1999; only about 20% had been, underscoring how few current market participants have lived through a comparable cycle. He says he can’t see professionally much beyond December 31, so his near-term playbook is tactical: short-term weakness, strength into year-end tied to midterm-cycle seasonality (markets typically bottom late October/early November, then rally into Q4), followed by a much tougher 2027.

02 Why 2027 looks bearish

Jones’s bearish 2027 call rests on flow analysis: summing buybacks, M&A, and other historically supportive corporate flows, then subtracting IPOs, secondaries, and unlocks, yields the most negative flow picture in many years. He attributes this shift partly to massive new equity supply from AI-related names — citing SpaceX alongside OpenAI and Anthropic — which changes the math that has powered markets since 2009. He draws a parallel to 2000-2002, when post-IPO unlocks from 1998-99 issuance made shorting easiest, and expects diversification and selling pressure as AI-exposed insiders face limited visibility into future earnings given the rapidly evolving landscape.

03 AI valuations, safety, and regulation

On valuations, Jones says he thinks Anthropic will do well and that OpenAI’s trading will depend heavily on how Anthropic’s expected November event goes. He attributes recent multiple compression in tech to AI safety anxieties and administration rhetoric about accountability for AI mistakes — the risk that a model behaves unpredictably or is misused by a bad actor. Jones says his firm runs internal AI/neural-net systems and has for years, but flags interpretability as a persistent concern: outputs emerge from billions of permutations without clear explanation of why or how they were reached. He cites a figure that 85% of Americans want AI regulation and predicts it will arrive politically one way or another, drawing an analogy to unregulated immigration from 2020-2024 provoking political backlash. He separately praises Trump’s appointment of Jay Clayton, calling him smart and likely to prioritize humanity in navigating AI policy, calling it the best source of hope in this area.

04 Debt markets, oil, and the Fed

Jones warns of a brewing debt-market tipping point, noting corporate debt issuance is up 50% since 2022 and AI-related issuance could reach 3.6% of GDP — more than railroads, highways, or telecom infrastructure build-outs historically — competing with sovereign debt issuance in a crowded global market. He references a conversation with an unnamed Senate majority leader who said deficits won’t be fixed until the market forces politicians’ hand, comparing the eventual trigger to 2008/TARP. On rates, asked what he’d do in Kevin Warsh’s position, Jones says he’s glad he isn’t in it and expects Warsh to resist the Japan-style path of fiscal dominance and inflating away debt, which he says makes the road ahead more volatile. On Treasury Secretary Bessent’s proposed buyback tools, Jones calls him smart and market-savvy but compares his interventions to Bank of Japan yen interventions — useful short-term but unable to fix underlying structural deficit issues; current 100-basis-point rate increases alone add roughly $120 billion to the CBO deficit baseline, pushing a projected 2036 deficit from 6.5% to 8% of GDP. Separately, Jones says he’s bullish oil, seeing another $20-25 of upside amid unresolved Iran tensions, while expecting equity markets to dislike such a rally; he suggests Trump may have a post-midterm resolution plan for the Middle East situation.

05 Other risks and philanthropy

Asked about a reported pathogen situation in Russia, Jones says it’s not currently affecting his investment strategy but that his firm’s biosafety expert is watching it given insufficient information. Closing the conversation, Jones previews the Robinhood Foundation’s upcoming investor conference, naming guests including Dina Powell (whom he describes as now president at Meta), Stan Druckenmiller, Steve Cohen, David Einhorn, and Philippe Laffont. He highlights that New York’s poverty rate is at an all-time high alongside record wealth creation, and notes U.S. charitable giving as a share of household wealth has fallen from 0.5% to 0.38% since 2020 even as household wealth grew.

01 Why leave DeepMind for a new bet

Silver says he had a fantastic experience at DeepMind but concluded his conviction — that superintelligence emerges from systems that learn purely from experience, not LLM-based products — deserved a dedicated, elite lab rather than remaining a sideline within an organization focused on LLM products. He frames the choice as a market-positioning one: with tens or hundreds of thousands of researchers already pursuing the LLM pretraining/post-training/coding path, joining that crowd offered less expected impact than exploring an unoccupied approach that could change the whole field if it works. Espeholt, who worked with Silver as early as 2016 on scaling reinforcement learning and left DeepMind before Silver did, says he joined based on respect for Silver’s thinking and the team (including named former colleagues Wojciech and Chris), plus the appeal of building something ambitious from Europe. The company name reflects the idea that discoveries made without reliance on human language could be so profound they’re impossible to express in words — plus a tongue-in-cheek second meaning that the bet can’t fail.

02 Defining the approach

Silver describes the goal along two axes: depth (an agent that starts knowing nothing and can discover knowledge far beyond current human understanding) and breadth (the same algorithm working across simulated, digital, or physical streams of experience). He distinguishes this explicitly from current LLM-based scientific-discovery claims, arguing those still rely on heavy human-in-the-loop effort and are not autonomous discovery, and questions whether language-based reasoning is even the right vehicle for embodied, real-world learning.

03 Crucibles and simple rewards

Instead of handcrafted task curricula, Ineffable builds “crucibles” — simulated environments engineered for emergent complexity via physics engines or many interacting agents — paired with simple optimization objectives rather than rewards that encode exactly what science or mathematics should look like. Silver draws an analogy to evolution (simple fitness optimization producing all of life’s complexity) and to human history (simple coordination/trading goals cascading into arithmetic, tools, and science). He cites the game of Go — a simple 19x19 grid that becomes endlessly complex once a second agent is introduced — as evidence that simple dynamics plus multi-agent interaction can generate unbounded learning curves. Espeholt adds that crucibles span a spectrum from cheap, fast-iterating simulations to more realistic but computationally expensive ones, and the team is deliberately building across that range.

04 Removing human priors and the AlphaGo precedent

Silver frames the rejection of human data and pretraining as an active, pragmatic (not dogmatic) choice: leaning on human data constrains both the depth of possible discovery and the breadth of applicable domains, including many sensor types or embodied settings where human language data doesn’t fit well. He recounts that AlphaGo took two years of human-in-the-loop, human-data-based development to beat Lee Sedol, while the human-data-free AlphaZero surpassed it far more quickly — four hours to beat the best chess AI, roughly two days in Go — his core evidentiary basis for betting against LLM-style pretraining. Silver notes that weak, environment-agnostic priors (not strong trillion-item datasets) remain acceptable, and Espeholt says the goal is avoiding bias toward specific human artifacts, such as Von Neumann computer architecture, while letting agents discover their own solutions. Silver also argues human language itself may be a ceiling rather than a foundation for reasoning, since it embeds historical biases (he gives the example of agents that might “believe” in flat earth or disease-causing humors if trained only on language from centuries ago).

05 Simulation’s ceiling and alignment questions

On whether superintelligence can be built entirely in simulation, Silver argues anything Turing-complete can in principle represent computation, money and economics, language, and mathematics, making simulation sufficient for much of what’s needed — and faster and safer than real-world training. He agrees with a point attributed to Rich Sutton (raised by the host from a separate episode) that simulated learning won’t automatically transfer to real-world complexity, but counters that simulation can still generate the underlying algorithmic science needed, later validated and ported to higher-fidelity simulations and eventually the real world. Espeholt cites the team’s earlier MetNet weather-forecasting model as evidence that machine-learned approximations can sometimes outperform physics-based simulation, while acknowledging a ceiling exists for highly exact domains like quantum computation. On alignment, Silver says current English-language-based monitoring is reaching its limits as agents get more capable and may begin to decouple their stated reasoning from their actions, and proposes alternatives like shadow actions and superintelligent agents monitoring other superintelligent agents, while expressing general support for pacing the real-world frontier (citing the direction suggested by people like DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis and Anthropic’s Dario Amodei) even as Ineffable pushes simulation work as fast as possible.

06 Compute, team, and predictions

Espeholt details the compute stack: Ineffable is the first customer of a large Vera Rubin cluster on Google Cloud, chosen partly because its high-bandwidth, rack-as-single-machine architecture suits reinforcement learning’s fast iteration cycles better than LLM training. Compute splits across training, inference, and simulation, with ongoing engineering work (model-based RL, replay buffers) to make simulation efficient on GPUs not originally optimized for it; Espeholt says RL scaling laws are non-stationary and harder to characterize than LLM scaling laws but the team is actively pushing toward them as it moves to the bigger cluster. Espeholt traces his own lineage work — IMPALA (GPU-scalable RL at DeepMind) and SEED RL (TPU-rack-scale RL at Google Brain) — as foundational to techniques now being rediscovered by LLM-era RL teams. The company has deliberately kept its team to 20 people, prioritizing talent, kindness, mission alignment, and skill fit, yielding a high compute-per-person ratio; several co-founders previously worked together on AlphaStar. Silver closes with predictions made on the record: strong evidence of the approach’s validity within a year, and achievement of superintelligence within three years, accelerated from an earlier three-to-five-year estimate due to coding-agent-driven research acceleration.
