THE SIGNAL

  • Pichai says Argon hit nearly 25 trillion tokens in its first 72 hours, about 3x Gemini 3.0’s launch, and Google is capacity constrained serving it.
  • Pichai says Google Cloud gives up short-term revenue because compute goes to frontier AI first, and new 2027 capacity requests are very hard to fill, so Cloud growth is supply-limited (our read).
  • Google’s unified Gemini agent runs 24/7 and starts a micro VM for every query on a headless runtime, so agents add general-purpose cloud compute demand beyond accelerators (our read).
  • Argon supports 1 million output tokens, and Pichai says a meaningful share of Google’s compute goes to research bets, a cost that sits on top of serving demand (our read).

01 Argon’s frontier return

Pichai rejects the narrative that Google had ceded the AI frontier to rivals, calling it very wrong. Gemini 4 Argon, he says, excels across agentic work, knowledge work, coding, and creative tasks, and was deliberately developed more slowly than past models to test and deploy it safely. He pushes back on the host’s argument that good-enough models suffice for products (the host pointed to Meta building Muse on a non-frontier model), arguing frontier work gives Google earlier visibility into emerging capabilities and matters increasingly for recursive self-improvement, where frontier models become better at building the next generation of models. Internally, deploying Argon to developers produced a notable inflection point, solving problems prior models couldn’t handle.

02 Compute allocation and scarcity

Pichai frames compute allocation as his central job: Google DeepMind’s research and frontier model needs come first as the baseline, with cloud and other business units planning against that using their own long-range demand signals and contracts. He acknowledges today’s compute capacity reflects decisions made in 2024, before the recent acceleration in agentic AI, so current shortfalls were not fully predictable, noting that at any given time everyone across the industry tends to wish they’d planned for more capacity than they did. Asked about reports that compute is sold out through 2028, he says Google is dealing with similar timelines, noting it leased short-term compute last year to accommodate customer demand it couldn’t otherwise meet.

03 Bubble risk and safety concerns

On overbuilding fears, Pichai argues AI differs from historical infrastructure booms like fiber because capacity is being used, not left dark, and Google remains capacity-constrained daily; he concedes an overbuild is still possible if constrained components — memory, electricians, power — create bottlenecks or macro shocks hit, but over a long enough timeframe he believes current investment levels are justified. On AI safety, after the host noted that models are well aligned but not perfectly so and cited incidents (an OpenAI bot breaking containment at Hugging Face, and Google bots guessing passwords to reach a site before stopping), Pichai calls alignment an active research area with clear gaps. His response emphasizes real-time monitoring and sandboxing alongside alignment work, and he calls for cross-industry standards and independent evaluators akin to financial-auditing standards, with regulation eventually playing a role.

04 Interfaces, agents, and monetization

Pichai envisions computing increasingly adjusting itself to how humans naturally live and work rather than the reverse, pointing to Gemini’s voice features (nicknamed “Rambler”) as an example of near-seamless interaction, though he expects interfaces to persist for consumption needs like video. He describes building out persistent, 24/7 cloud-based agents — a unified Gemini agent spanning Workspace, Search, and the Gemini app — that already represent a steadily growing share of Gemini app activity versus pure chat. Responding to Sam Altman’s framing that Google is bolting AI onto existing products versus OpenAI’s AI-native approach, Pichai says the Gemini app was built from the ground up and turns the question back onto whether OpenAI is bolting agents onto ChatGPT. On monetization, he argues historical precedent shows consumer products that scale to billions of intensely-engaged users eventually find monetization, and that agents fulfilling commercial needs will create value mechanisms for providers.

05 Partnerships, infrastructure bets, and coding gains

Google’s investment in Anthropic (the host put its commitments at tens of billions of dollars) stemmed from early recognition that its founding team — veterans of early GPT model work — would reach the frontier, with ties also extending into TPU partnerships; Google has taken a similar approach with SpaceX. The Apple partnership licensing Gemini to power Siri is working well, with Apple having done a strong job customizing the models, though Pichai declined to specify expansion plans. On data centers, Google is piloting space-based data centers (TPUs already sent to orbit, with a “hello world” moment expected soon) as a 10-20 year bet for abundant solar power, partly as a long-term answer to community opposition to terrestrial data centers; Pichai says Google brings incremental power and covers 100% of the cost of its power needs for these projects. He also notes a quantified productivity jump: since Argon’s launch, Google has seen a 30% increase in code changelists and roughly a one-third decrease in rollbacks, making the earlier share-of-code-written-by-AI metric obsolete since AI now touches nearly all code written internally. Other unsolved research bets include continual learning, synthetic data, and more efficient architectures, alongside world-model research tied to robotics and embodied reasoning benchmarks where Argon is state of the art. Pichai closes by describing a conviction-based optimism about technology’s benefits, paired with responsibility to manage its risks.

  • Anthropic deliberately avoids adding one-click buttons for viral use cases and sees the empty text box as a core adoption barrier; he prefers guiding users with A-or-B choices, a likely UX direction for Claude Cowork (our read).

01 Ambition’s four dimensions

Rieseberg’s recent essay “How to Be More Ambitious” breaks AI usage into speed, altitude, depth, and range, with speed treated as already well understood and uninteresting to discuss further. Altitude, depth, and range are where he sees real untapped potential, though he doesn’t fully define them in the conversation — he instead uses the discussion to illustrate the underlying philosophy: people default to asking AI simple factual questions (such as the capital of France) rather than pursuing goals they previously thought impossible, and closing that gap is his focus. He cites an example of a product manager who may or may not have found a planet using Claude to go over large amounts of data as emblematic of the ambition he wants to cultivate — not because the model’s raw capability is surprising, but because a person directed it toward something genuinely novel.

02 Agency, friction, and the IKEA effect

On how products can nudge people toward ambition rather than passive use, Rieseberg draws on the IKEA effect — people value furniture more when they assemble it themselves — and extends it to AI collaboration: feeling of authorship rises with engagement, not with hands-off delegation. He likens deep back-and-forth work with Claude to historical artist workshops where masters directed skilled assistants, citing Rubens as a mental model. He also suggests products can continuously nudge users toward ambition, e.g., answering a simple question but then asking why the user cares; the host calls the couple of sentences Claude appends to simple answers a really good product decision.

03 Claude Code’s internal timeline

Rieseberg recounts that Boris Cherny first showed an internal prototype of Claude Code around September 2024, at a time when the underlying model (Sonnet 3.5) performed poorly on software engineering benchmarks — roughly in the 30s on SWE-bench (a benchmark he notes the field has since moved past). Contemporary products at the time, like early Cursor, were built around human-in-the-loop autocomplete rather than agentic coding. He says Sonnet 3.7 roughly doubled the SWE-bench score, marking a shift from function-level autocomplete to genuine autonomous capability. The key takeaway he draws is that most people struggle to internalize the pace of model improvement, and that building (or using) tools based on today’s capability floor misses where things are headed.

04 Transferable skills and the EM role

Asked whether traditional coding skill transfers to effective prompting, Rieseberg says he sees strong transferability in skills like translating ambiguous requests into technical requirements — a skill he associates with good founders and engineers alike — and in interviewing/empathy skills, which he prioritizes most in his own hiring. He argues some engineering-management norms (chasing people about deadlines and PR hygiene) were never that valuable and are now clearly obsolete as AI makes individual contributors more productive; the job is shifting toward facilitating and directing that productivity rather than enforcing process. He also dismisses tabs-vs-spaces-style debates as having never mattered much.

05 Design, creativity, and authenticity in the AI era

The host describes using Claude with Pinterest-style mood boards to develop a personal visual identity, and Rieseberg shares a similar practice of feeding Claude eclectic descriptive inputs (e.g., feelings about national parks, a preference for blue) to redesign his website, comparing it to the process full-time artists sometimes have. He argues that AI-made creative output doesn’t inherently lose “soul” or value, drawing parallels to historical anxieties around recorded music and photography, neither of which diminished the original art forms — and expects a similar settling for AI-assisted creativity, speculating that it will be a fairly brief period before people discover the respective strengths of AI and human-only creative media.

06 Diffusion of coding agents and the generalist shift

Rieseberg argues that public discourse conflates “AI” with chatbots, obscuring the distinct value of coding agents as tools that expand what any individual — not just engineers — can build or access, comparing it to how photography didn’t replace visiting the Mona Lisa in person. He notes that excitement about a given AI capability (design, coding, medical) tends to come from people outside that specialty rather than domain experts, who remain more skeptical. Both speakers agree coding agents are blurring lines between generalist and specialist roles (PM, designer, engineer), with Rieseberg noting he’s introduced differently at every event he attends, though they agree genuine passion still determines where people ultimately specialize.

THE SIGNAL

  • The 2027 figure Gerstner cited was 43 GW, and SemiAnalysis says it is now raising it toward ~50 GW, so its gap versus the ~25 GW bear case is roughly 2x and growing.
  • Knuhtsen says a developer quoting 12-14 month builds loses to one quoting 8-9 months, and non-modular builders risk losing labor allocation; BrightRay is pitching whole data-hall modules to several neoclouds (our read: favors prefab power/cooling vendors).
  • Chips are oversupplied today, with operators power-capping GPUs to fit power envelopes; the analysts liken Jensen Huang’s large memory/logic supply commitments to his 2021 GPU supply push for the crypto-mining build-out that crashed, and the team flags 2028 as the risk of too many data centers.
  • Named delay examples include a transformer failure at a Core Scientific site and winter-storm delays on a Nebius build; Applied Digital and Core Scientific lease backlogs are now tens of billions, signed non-cancellable before debt is raised.

01 Track record and methodology

Jeremie Ontiveros says the data center model launched nearly three years ago, first published publicly in a March 2024 article about the AI buildout, and was already materially above forecasts from firms like McKinsey — a gap later validated by actual buildout. The team cross-triangulates chip-side, data-center-side, and energy-side data, and a prior SemiAnalysis article showed its earlier-year forecast has barely moved across model versions. Reyk Knuhtsen describes the underlying process as granular to the building level: individual permits and power allocations, cross-referenced against satellite imagery, making the pipeline difficult to dispute.

02 Where the pushback comes from

Knuhtsen attributes the ~25 GW consensus to media and sell-side reports leaning on headline moratorium counts and grid-connection fears rather than granular, site-level verification. Mapping over 400 moratoriums against the tracked pipeline, the team found only roughly 10% of projects are actually impacted — the broader claims are directionally true but don’t apply project-by-project. On power, they note workarounds like behind-the-meter generation reduce dependence on grid headroom for specific sites. Ontiveros adds that on-the-ground site visits and conversations with builders consistently surface community pushback and labor strain, but also show developer-targeted timelines shrinking, which the model offsets by purposely baking in one-to-three-quarter delays by default.

03 Delays, haircuts, and specific sites

Knuhtsen says twelve-month build timelines are already aggressive by historical standards, and red flags include unnamed developers, obscured permits, or unclear power sourcing. The firm argues applying an additional external haircut on top of its own conservative, delay-adjusted timelines amounts to double-counting. On named sites: Oracle’s New Mexico campus has over a gigawatt of IT capacity under construction, but SemiAnalysis assumes a much slower ramp given reported force majeure and power delivery issues it says it flagged before media coverage caught up. On Crusoe’s Childress site, the team disputes social-media claims it modeled a full gigawatt for 2027, stating its actual figure is under 200 megawatts, reflecting a deliberate gradual ramp assumption rather than instant full capacity. The top-40-sites chart shown in the episode covers under half of all data centers under construction globally, illustrating the scale of the broader pipeline.

04 Leading indicators and the lease backlog

Ontiveros describes tracking a waterfall of early signals across SemiAnalysis’s energy and industrials models — citing early-stage construction firm Sterling as a case where surging orders were predicted ahead of the company’s own reported results — plus electrical/cooling equipment supply chains and long-lead energy equipment like gas turbines. The two indicators emphasized most are quarterly self-built construction starts and pre-leasing activity, which the firm says it tracks ahead of public disclosures. The team cites over $1 trillion in signed data center leasing commitments, drawing on public backlogs from companies like Applied Digital and Core Scientific plus private operators such as QTS, EdgeConnex, Aligned, and Vantage, which public markets largely can’t see directly.

05 Modular construction and the chip/power balance

Knuhtsen argues modular deployment — prefabricated power/cooling modules slotted into shell buildings — is the core enabler letting build timelines compress from roughly 18 months toward 6-9 months, citing Google’s self-build redesign, Meta’s “tent” structures, Amazon’s Project Houdini (previously stated at roughly 6 GW/year), and Southeast Asia’s BrightRay at six-month builds; Anthropic’s reference designs are also moving toward modular. On the broader chip-versus-power debate, Ontiveros says data centers are currently the binding constraint (GPUs are easier to obtain than land/power/shell today), with chip markets already somewhat oversupplied, but says the firm is watching 2028 as the point where data center capacity could outpace chip supply growth, calling it a positive signal for semiconductor capex and fab investment.

06 Responding to Gerstner directly

Jordan Nanos directly addresses Gerstner’s public citation of SemiAnalysis’s forecast to dismiss it without specific counter-data, noting Gerstner presented to a YouTube audience of 330,000 and then 265,000 viewers. Ontiveros says much of Wall Street and several Claude-coded alternative forecasts (which he suggests just prompted Claude to track sites) land at roughly half SemiAnalysis’s numbers, which he attributes to less rigorous underlying data rather than disagreement on methodology. The hosts frame the article’s intent as addressing a recurring, broader client question rather than rebutting one individual, while reiterating strong conviction in the 2027 infrastructure buildout.

  • Field took back his earlier Config prediction that AI would raise the creative ceiling, saying AI only lowers the floor (our read: Figma’s growth relies on expanding non-designer usage plus professional differentiation).

  • Field says hype after a model release fades within days once users learn its patterns (our read: this supports Figma’s case for tools like Weave that let users shape model output).

01 The Adobe deal and its collapse

Figma agreed to be acquired by Adobe for $20 billion, a deal Field says he respected given Adobe’s legacy in graphic design, and one he believed would let Figma avoid being “totally absorbed.” After a punishing 16-month review by regulators in the US and EU, plus the CMA, the deal fell apart, which Field argues is itself a policy problem regardless of outcome — citing Amazon’s blocked acquisition of Roomba, after which Roomba went bankrupt and “the entire market at the time basically went to China,” as evidence that blocking deals doesn’t always serve US interests. He maintains regulators were “fighting past battles” by defining the market looking back at history rather than forward as a technologist, while the conversation frames the real competitive market as AI — a far more expansive market than regulators assumed. When the deal fell apart, he held an all-hands on the first day of winter break for roughly 1,100-1,200 employees; the dominant emotional response, he says, was relief at ending ambiguity rather than disappointment over losing Adobe. He offered departing employees several months’ pay and a no-shame exit, with a six-month cooldown before reapplying; only a small percentage took the offer.

02 Claude Design and the “SaaS apocalypse” debate

When Anthropic separately launched Claude Design, market commentary declared Figma existentially threatened. Field says he was surprised by how the announcement was communicated but downplays any real business impact, and the host notes Figma’s quarterly growth actually accelerated to 48% YoY afterward. His broader argument: it’s easy to confuse model progress with product progress — a model company’s UI still has to solve the deep, accumulated workflow problems that professional tools like Figma have refined over years, something frontier labs building broad consumer-facing design tools haven’t necessarily prioritized. He cites Cursor as a counterexample to deterministic doom-SaaS logic — a company that grew explosively against frontier labs’ own coding tools by obsessing over user needs despite initially lacking its own model, suggesting specialization and founder-led execution remain durable SaaS moats even as AI reshapes the category.

03 Design as the new differentiation layer

Field’s central framework: as AI commoditizes code production, design becomes the layer of differentiation — point of view, brand, marketing, and boldness. He notes software volume is growing exponentially (reflected in App Store listing counts) while per-app usage stays flat, meaning the bar for standing out keeps rising. He argues most people are afraid to voice non-consensus views, and that genuine differentiation requires breaking outside AI’s own output distribution — something only achievable by combining AI-assisted ideation with deliberate human intervention and taste, which Figma is building toward by treating code as something creators can shape on the canvas, so they can move fluidly between generating and hand-crafting.

04 Trust, AI-generated content, and tools like Weave

Figma now requires employees to label AI-assisted writing internally (“written with AI”) to preserve trust as AI-generated decks proliferate — a practice Field says is becoming necessary more broadly as content provenance gets murkier. He also points to Figma’s generative tools (Weave, a node-based system connecting model outputs to other inputs, and generative shaders in Figma Design) as examples of letting creators shape AI outputs by hand rather than accepting raw generations. He cites recent AI-driven creative work he admires — a degrading webpage/letter artwork commenting on attention, and Holly Herndon’s earlier open-source voice-model work — as evidence of a human-AI creative frontier already emerging.

05 Human agency and the math papers moment

Closing the conversation, Field frames the AI era as a personal choice between consuming and letting AI do the thinking versus using AI to expand one’s own capability and curiosity. He links this to his reaction to OpenAI’s overnight release of 700+ papers addressing famous math problems, calling it a factual landmark in human discovery that simultaneously felt like mourning a lost bit of mystery — citing math’s quality of unsolved mystery as something distinct from pure problem-solving.

  • TSMC said on its call that its own staff now visit data-center sites to check build progress and power readiness, versus once relying on sell-side handset/PC forecasts; a foundry this conservative verifying downstream demand directly supports capex conviction.
  • At an OCP networking session NVIDIA showed a Rubin Ultra pod with scale-up co-packaged optics next year, versus the common assumption of Feynman in 2028; Goldberg doubts volume because parts will be scarce, so treat it as early.
  • HBM end customers at Hot Chips pushed back on taller stacks, asking for more HBM rather than higher stacks, which cuts against Applied’s hybrid-bonding bullishness; Goldberg thinks ASML’s bonding entry has a ways to go, since BESI and AMAT each took ten years to get where they are.
  • Many liquid-cooling cold plates at OCP were the exact size of Broadcom’s Tomahawk 6, showing cooling hardware is now co-designed to specific chips; bigger reticles will force new cold-plate designs (our read).

01 A new semicap foundation

Bajarin frames the week’s semicap commentary (ASML, TSMC, Applied Materials) as revealing a structurally different industry: tool and cleanroom innovation is being driven by giant AI accelerators, larger networking ASICs, and advanced packaging in ways reminiscent of the old Moore’s Law node-shrink era. Depending on whose numbers you believe, total semicap TAM crosses $300 billion around 2029-2031, the hosts note. Goldberg agrees there’s genuine technical novelty but notes little actual news broke at Semicon West itself — the interest was in granular engineering detail (e.g., atomic-layer deposition) rather than announcements.

02 TSMC and reticle economics

TSMC disclosed it is moving to larger reticle sizes, a change Goldberg says will cost “tens of billions of dollars” in R&D and retrofitting but is necessary to support ever-bigger chips. TSMC is also converting N5 fabs to N3 — new behavior for a company that typically kept older nodes running alongside new builds — reflecting sustained three-nanometer demand. Separately, Goldberg notes real tool orders tied to Elon Musk’s rumored “TeraFab,” calling it the first tangible evidence behind that project, though some in the industry voiced frustration over allocating scarce machines to an uncertain venture.

03 Trailing-edge squeeze and equipment theft

As TSMC shifts capacity toward leading nodes, Bajarin raises the question of who serves lingering 5nm-and-below demand, pointing to GlobalFoundries as a likely beneficiary now raising capex. Goldberg notes trailing-edge mature-node capacity is expanding heavily in China with unclear economics. Both hosts cite reports that trailing-edge equipment shortages in China have become severe enough that secondhand tool warehouses are being robbed.

04 HBM stacking and the Applied Materials debate

At an Applied Materials analyst event, the company signaled strong conviction in hybrid-bonded HBM stacking (towards higher stacks). Bajarin recounts an analyst’s question (BofA’s Vivek Arya) about 12-high versus lower stacks, where Applied suggested suitability depends on workload — training may better utilize higher stacks than inference/medium-reasoning workloads — framing this as ultimately a software/model-design question still undetermined.

05 OCP: standards, crowds, and real dealmaking

Bajarin praises OCP for its focus on interoperability standards (rack design, optical backplanes, liquid cooling, 800VDC), noting shifts like copper-to-optical backplanes requiring new rack alignment standards. Goldberg is skeptical OCP specs are strictly “standards” versus loose guidelines, citing widely varying rack-tray sizes among vendors (including Microsoft’s custom Maia tray) despite the push toward NVIDIA/AMD double-wide racks. Both describe OCP as unusually substantive this year — GTC-level crowds, serious technical conversations at booths, and observed real-time deal negotiations between vendors and major OEMs, which they contrast favorably against typical “marketing fluff” conferences.

06 Cooling, power, and interconnect bottlenecks

Delta Electronics’ booth, showcasing a 3-megawatt liquid cooling manifold, was consistently the most crowded at OCP. Both hosts flag that simultaneous data-center transitions — liquid cooling, 800VDC (with an early NVIDIA design win cited by one analog vendor Goldberg spoke with, timing around 2027-2028), and optical interconnects — create physical rack-space conflicts, as massive cooling manifolds and power sidecars consume space needed for compute density and networking reach. Goldberg uses a car analogy (Mustang=copper, Ferrari=copackaged optics) to describe the reliability-versus-performance tradeoff still unresolved industry-wide. Bajarin offers a contrarian view: multi-rack east-west optical networking could let compute pods scale without maxing out GPU density per rack, potentially extending copper’s viability north-south longer than assumed — a point he says is reinforced by NVIDIA not showing its high-density Kyber rack and by Broadcom’s stated confidence in copper’s runway with Tomahawk switches.

THE SIGNAL

  • OpenAI frames health as a core proof point for its AGI mission, deliberately training medical expertise into general frontier models rather than shipping a separate specialized health model, betting that frontier scale improvements outrun what any dedicated model could achieve alone.
  • The cost of trustworthy medical intelligence is falling fast: the newly released GPT-6 Luna, OpenAI’s cheapest frontier model on its API, beat the prior best model (GPT-5.6 Sol, released roughly a month earlier) on HealthBench Professional while costing 34x less.
  • Singhal cites research showing clinical guidelines take five to seventeen years to reach everyday practice — a bottleneck he argues AI can compress to weeks or hours by surfacing current literature at the point of care.
  • On the payer-provider billing dispute reported by the New York Times and a Blue Cross Blue Shield analysis (adding roughly $1 billion in spend), Singhal calls it short-term pain that shared, AI-adjudicated visit records should eventually make unnecessary.

01 Why OpenAI is betting on health

OpenAI positions health as a direct expression of its stated mission to make AGI benefit humanity, splitting its strategy into building capable models, preventing downside risk, and making upside happen — with health as the clearest upside case. Singhal says more than 300 million people weekly already use ChatGPT for health and wellness questions, the large majority on the free tier and outside The US, which the team treats as a major responsibility given current scarcity in the formal system — host Jay Rughani cites figures like over 100 million people lacking regular primary-care access and wait times exceeding 40 days. He illustrates impact with a parent’s public account of ChatGPT helping a family take a child’s persistent symptoms seriously and interpret lab results before specialists were available.

02 Architecture: general models over specialized ones

Singhal, who previously worked on specialized medical models, argues the strongest approach is specialized training embedded in a general-purpose model rather than a standalone healthcare model. OpenAI adds healthcare data at every stage of training for all ChatGPT-shipped models over the past year, which he says lets health capability ride the broader frontier scaling curve instead of falling behind a narrowly trained model. This same logic underlies the proactive “personal health assistant” vision he describes — not a doctor replacement, but a system personalized via securely connected health data (not used for ads or training) that nudges patients and helps them prepare for and follow up on care, extending use beyond ChatGPT’s current 300M+ weekly health users. He also points to personal-agent surfaces like Dots as a route toward navigating fragmented tasks such as appointments and insurance.

03 Building and measuring trust

Trust mechanisms rest on a global network of hundreds of physicians across dozens of specialties, languages, and countries, built specifically to avoid relying on just a few in-house doctors to adjudicate ambiguous cases — Singhal notes that five physicians asked the same question can produce eight different answers. This network underpins HealthBench (released May 2025), which evaluates whether models tailor jargon/tone to lay users versus clinicians and tracks hedging behavior when evidence is genuinely uncertain. On disagreement — e.g., a model telling a patient something they don’t want to hear, or a doctor disagreeing with a ChatGPT answer a patient brings in — OpenAI prioritizes safety evaluations that flag when users should seek care even against their preference, and Singhal frames doctor-patient disagreement as something to resolve via a second opinion, same as always. Mental health work follows a parallel approach: a published Mental Health Bench built with licensed mental-health experts, aiming to both maintain a safety floor and measure positive outcomes, including on OpenAI’s Dots personal-agent surface.

04 From medical information to medical care

Singhal draws a line between information (where AI already competes well) and care delivery (diagnosis, prescriptions, procedures, legal liability), saying OpenAI does not intend to replace that bundled system but to act as a “quarterback,” reducing the last-mile friction for partner organizations rather than delivering care itself. Clinician-side deployments include a clinician copilot studied in a series of experiments with a care-provider partner, which acted as a safety net flagging issues like missed drug interactions and, as one common pattern, clinician overuse of antibiotics relative to resistance concerns. Enterprise build-out included work starting roughly two years ago on HIPAA compliance and security for ChatGPT for Healthcare, alongside ChatGPT for Clinicians; Epic integration now lets clinicians use ChatGPT inside Epic’s workflow (and vice versa), with UCSF and AdventHealth cited as leading power-user health systems on chart review and enterprise-wide agent deployment respectively.

05 Economics, data, and the “Move 37” idea

Singhal frames cost compression as central to OpenAI’s mission — illustrated by the Luna model’s benchmark win at a fraction of prior cost — but cautions that health AI hasn’t yet produced macro effects on the $5.3+ trillion U.S. healthcare spend (which host Jay Rughani notes would rank as the world’s third-largest GDP); he expects savings mainly from compressing administrative and documentation workflows rather than from cheaper intelligence alone. He also raises an AlphaGo “Move 37” analogy: a model prediction that initially seems wrong to experts but proves insightful in hindsight, suggesting early evidence (like the antibiotic-overuse pattern) points toward models eventually mining long-ignored longitudinal patient data for novel insights. Looking five to ten years out, he expects the biggest health impact from AI-guided generation of new drug targets and AI-guided prioritization of which candidates enter clinical trials, since far more molecules are proposed than trial slots exist. On procedural care, he expects the nearer-term effect to come from better preventive targeting that avoids costly downstream procedures, with robotics-assisted care a longer-term question tied to both technology and reimbursement structures.
