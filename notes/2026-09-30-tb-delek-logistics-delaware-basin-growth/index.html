Agenda

  • Delek Logistics' (NYSE: DKL) volume growth durability, New Mexico water constraints and future capital deployment
  • Sour gas competitive advantage and three-stream commercial leverage
  • Growth economics, capital intensity and deleveraging

Key Insights

  • Delek's Howard County water assets are nearing terminal inventory limits, with volumes likely to follow proved developed producing declines, while production in Lea and Eddy counties is already outpacing existing infrastructure
  • New Mexico disposal permits are increasingly constrained, making produced water pipelines into Texas one of the few scalable capacity solutions, increasing the value of existing New Mexico infrastructure
  • Developing sour gas infrastructure requires 18-24 months for acid gas injection permitting and commissioning. Although sour gathering triples standard USD 0.30-0.40/Mcf sweet gas OPEX, sour treatment still yields the basin's highest margins
  • Long-term minimum volume commitments are becoming obsolete. Midstream operators now largely rely on acreage dedications, forcing them to absorb upfront drilling risks while capturing long-tail proved developed producing cash flows
  • Delek's USD 180m-190m 2026 capital budget has limited flexibility, as majority of spending is tied to existing dedications and long-lead New Mexico projects

00:24:33] You had previously highlighted that the additional operating complexity associated with the highly corrosive gas sour stream is conducive to higher OPEX. Relative to a standard sweet gas plant, how much higher would you expect maintenance and labour costs and overall OPEX to be for an AGI facility?

MT [00:25:08]: It's more just additive. It's not necessarily that you're comparing the two because what you're basically doing is you're gathering sour production in the field. You've got to go build out, so It's higher on OPEX, it's higher on CAPEX. Number one, you got to go build out a dedicated pipeline to take sour production. You cannot take a pipeline that gathers sweet production and then also use that to gather sour production. It just doesn't work. You've got to have a dedicated line, even if it's paralleling a sweet line, you've got to have a dedicated line for sour. Number one, you got to build that out. Number two, then you've got to have compressors that are anti-rated [sic] to handle sour production. These compressors are much more expensive than a standard compressor. The operation of it is not necessarily that much different, but they are much more expensive. They're very hard to come by right now. You can be in quite a long queue if you're trying to buy one. If you're renting one, you can maybe get that in quicker, but these types of compressors are getting harder to come by. You've got higher CAPEX in your gathering, higher CAPEX on your compression. Then once you get it to your facility and you have an amine unit and an AGI well, you're basically taking that sour production, you're stripping out the bad stuff.

On the back end of that process, you've got a sweet gas stream that goes to your existing plant as sweet gas and then, basically, your waste, the H2S and CO2 that you've stripped out of that then go downhole into your AGI. Their standard of operating is going to be very different. A lot of instances, these guys are having to be fully masked up with gas mask as they're going and handling this stuff. It's not so much that it's increasing your plant OPEX, but you're adding a new operating asset into your footprint. How to price that can be challenging. I'd say your typical variable OPEX, not including processing, but your typical gathering compression OPEX on a gas asset out there in the Delaware can typically be USD 0.30-0.40 an M. If you're having to then run an amine unit and an AGI on top of that, you can easily triple that, easily triple that. Now obviously, scale is going to matter. The more volume that you can push, the more you can get that OPEX down, but yes, it can add significant cost to your gathering and compression side and the treating side.
