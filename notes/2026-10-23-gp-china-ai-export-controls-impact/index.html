SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion assessed China’s draft revision to its technology export catalogue, released on October 16, 2026. The proposal covers 23 technologies spanning foundation models, AI agents, computing clusters, chips, and cybersecurity capabilities.

The expert argued that the proposal is not a blanket restriction on overseas AI expansion. Instead, it creates a combined framework for controlling advanced capabilities, sensitive data, intellectual property ownership, licensing, and offshore deployment. Regulators may examine where technology was developed, who controls it, and how domestic intellectual property is transferred to overseas entities.

API, SaaS, app, and on-device delivery are not automatically exempt. Compliance depends on the underlying technology, training data, cross-border data movement, and destination-market rules.

Key Insights

Overseas providers will likely adopt layered hybrid architectures that separate sensitive local processing, lightweight on-device workloads, public-data services, and advanced model tasks. Local joint ventures with cloud or traditional IT partners in Southeast Asia and the Middle East may reduce implementation and regulatory risks. The expert strongly expects smaller vendors to benefit from specialization. Most companies operate below the proposal’s sensitive-data and advanced-computing thresholds and can build niche APIs, applications, and data services. Overseas computing centers may offer attractive near-term economics due to scarce high-end accelerators, but the expert does not view this advantage as durable. Long-term competitiveness depends on electricity, cooling, and operating costs. Agent rules remain ambiguous. The expert believes controls are more likely aimed at models that autonomously plan and decompose tasks than at customized enterprise workflows with many predefined steps. Restrictions on open-source releases could raise migration costs for overseas users, although alternative global model ecosystems limit dependence on any single Chinese stack. The broader policy reflects both industrial competition and AI safety governance, particularly for powerful systems capable of cybersecurity misuse.

However, it is a misunderstanding to conclude that an API can therefore always be exported. At least, that is my interpretation. The draft actually defines two logical frameworks. One is what we call the data-attribute framework. For a model, an agent, or inference acceleration, the attributes of the data used in the backend matter. The second framework concerns advanced capabilities, such as reaching 10 to the 26th power in computation or reaching two trillion parameters.

These two frameworks must be considered together. You cannot simply say that an API is completely compliant and lawful and can therefore be sold overseas. It depends on what the API carries in the backend. An API is merely a technical architecture through which many things can be transmitted. It does not eliminate backend compliance obligations. You must examine whether the API involves transferring core technology, whether it involves cross- border transfers subject to data-security rules, and whether it satisfies local data-privacy and AI-regulatory requirements. EY: Agent technology has also been included in the catalogue. Agents involve multistep tool calls and workflow integration. WW: Yes, there may be room for discussion. The latest proposal establishes some basic quantitative thresholds for agent technology. A large model provides underlying understanding and analytical capabilities, while an agent is more concerned with concrete task execution. The proposal attempts to describe measurable indicators for relatively complex agent systems. For example, an agent might perform 20 reasoning steps or integrate 200 tools. However, many conditions also apply. A number alone does not establish where the restriction lies. The draft provides some preliminary indicators.

For example, it refers to task-decomposition accuracy reaching 80%, an average subtask completion rate exceeding 90%, and concurrency exceeding 60 tasks. Behind these are also factors such as consistency over time and in decision-making. These are actually difficult to assess during implementation. Suppose I deliver a customer project involving 20 steps. Do we assess whether every step reaches a specific standard? Customers will also request acceptance metrics, asking what decomposition accuracy is achieved and what the completion rate is for each subtask.

Some points are therefore unclear. If a customer contract expressly states that a 20-step process must meet particular indicators, that may trigger conditions governing whether the technology can be exported. If the indicators are omitted, however, what standards will be used for customer acceptance? This could create unusual transitional situations. For example, could a contract specify a metric just slightly below the regulatory threshold, both satisfying the rules and broadly meeting delivery requirements? At this stage, that is not entirely clear.

I am personally more concerned with the two different dimensions behind this. One is the inherent capability of an agent model to reason and analyze. Traditionally, or when agents first began gaining momentum around 2025, we often described an agent as a workflow. Under that workflow, for a particular enterprise scenario, especially a B2B

agent scenario, we examined the relevant vertical data, an ontology or graph built on that data, the constraints at each step in the workflow, how implementation reliability could be audited, and how the system could be integrated into the customer's services.

That is a relatively customized project in which capabilities are implemented step by step. The implementation process can be very long, involving dozens or even hundreds of steps, because it is a hard-coded workflow. This is what we call delivery of a customized agent for a customer.

The rules and their interpretation may need to become more detailed because, in addition to agent design, many institutions are now studying what we call the "agent model." An agent model does not require someone to write steps one through five, or even one through one hundred or two hundred, and then have the system execute them. At each step, it may select and evaluate tools.

An agent is assumed to be capable of understanding a task autonomously. After understanding it, the agent decomposes the task into a number of steps. This is a model-level capability, not a project-delivery metric for a particular customer. I do not know whether that distinction is clear, but the two are different.

In a B2B scenario, which is generally where processes become this complex, a company's existing process may already contain one hundred steps. The provider simply implements those one hundred steps sequentially. An agent can control them in stages and support a relatively stable AI-agent deployment.

By contrast, a model with autonomous decomposition capabilities can analyze the process itself. Give it a task, and the agent model independently determines the number of steps required. The assessment then looks at decomposition accuracy and the success rate of each task within each step.

My personal impression is that the agent indicators in this proposal are more oriented toward limiting the autonomous capabilities of agent models, although the text may not yet be entirely clear. A highly capable agent model may be restricted from export. How a B2B enterprise defines a customer delivery may be different. What lies behind AI governance? It reminds me of nuclear-arms controls during the Cold War between the United States and the Soviet Union. AI is not a nuclear weapon, but there is one person I follow closely: Henry Kissinger, who is also well known in China. Before his death, he wrote several books. One was developed with a senior Microsoft strategist and discussed artificial intelligence and the future of humanity. He explicitly compared nuclear weapons with artificial intelligence.

During US-Soviet nuclear-arms control, countries considered how to prevent nuclear proliferation, restrict nuclear capabilities, and control the safety risks of a very powerful weapon. Against this background, Kissinger argued that the safety risks of artificial intelligence could be greater than those posed by nuclear weapons. Nuclear capabilities require state-level investment and are difficult to obtain. AI also requires substantial funding, computing power, and data, but it is far more accessible. In the past, only a very small number of people had access to nuclear capabilities, whereas almost anyone can now access AI.

Like nuclear governance, in which nuclear weapons are subject to non-proliferation while civilian nuclear technology can be transferred through licensing for power generation, AI may also require differentiated governance.

When AI reaches a particular level of capability, such as models exceeding two trillion parameters or clusters containing more than 10,000 cards, it becomes extremely powerful. The question is whether that capability will improve people's livelihoods or be used to exploit vulnerabilities such as the recent global PHP vulnerability, compromising large numbers of civilian websites, industrial websites, and banking systems. Technological development in AI is also irreversible.

Each country wants to maintain its competitiveness and be the best, leading to commercial restrictions and technology export restrictions. That is the competitive dimension.

There is also a cooperative dimension from the perspective of AI governance.
