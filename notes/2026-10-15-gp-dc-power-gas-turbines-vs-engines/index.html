SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion examined how developers select on-site generation for large data centers, particularly gas turbines versus reciprocating engines. It covered project scale, land efficiency, deployment speed, permitting, gas infrastructure, financing, equipment supply, renewable alternatives, and stranded-asset risk.

Turbines were presented as the stronger option for large campuses because they offer greater power density and simpler physical layouts. Reciprocating engines provide modularity and potentially faster initial deployment, but become operationally complex at very large scales.

Key Insights

Turbines are generally preferred for projects approaching gigawatt scale, while engines may be more competitive below 100 MW or for phased developments. The expert expressed low confidence in gigawatt-scale campuses relying on numerous reciprocating engines because of space, maintenance, and infrastructure complexity. On-site generation can secure earlier power while grid interconnections remain delayed, even though it may cost materially more than utility service. Large, permanently off-grid campuses in remote locations face the greatest stranded-asset risk, particularly if cheaper grid- connected alternatives later become available. Permitting and gas laterals can delay projects even after generation equipment has been secured. Air permits may also restrict operating hours and undermine original economics. Developers can likely pass higher equipment costs to data center customers under current market conditions. Labor and engineering, procurement, and construction capacity may become the next major bottleneck as more projects advance. Grid export capability materially reduces risk by allowing an abandoned behind-the-meter plant to operate as a conventional merchant generator.
