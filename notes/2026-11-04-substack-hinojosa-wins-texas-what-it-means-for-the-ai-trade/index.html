Texas election & the AI trade

Gaetano

Nov 4

READ IN APP

If you’ve been watching the market since the close, you have been on a roller coaster.

The AI infra trade got especially hit hard.

Let’s look at why that was the reaction.

There’s a lot going on, but Texas is in focus here.

Now this gets interesting for the AI trade.

Hinojosa wants a pause on new data centers until lawmakers put protections in place for communities. And this is Texas we’re talking about.

~32% of the U.S. data-center capacity under construction, measured in megawatts (Baxtel).

That includes cloud, colocation, enterprise and crypto facilities, so it is a broader number than just AI, but still.

Almost a third of tracked construction. There is a lot riding on what happens here.

The debate is about power, water and who pays.

People see these enormous facilities going up and want to know what happens to their water supply, whether grid upgrades could end up in their electricity bills, and how much say they have in what gets built nearby.

The effect on bills depends on the actual utility and project arrangements. But you can see why people are asking.

I think that if AI demand stays strong while new supply slows, the capacity already operating could become more valuable.

The hyperscalers could get more work out of the facilities they already paid for, potentially charge more for new business, and spread the next round of spending over more time.

That could help the cash-flow story that the market is so focused on.

The catch is whether the companies can keep serving demand.

Getting more out of an existing facility is great but having no room for the next customer means lost or delayed revenue.

So the opportunity is in the capacity already available and the business it can support, not purely in spending less.

What changes after the election

Abbott had already told TCEQ (state environmental regulator) to pause data-center permits until ERCOT (Texas grid operator) finishes its review.

His September directive also calls for developers to pay electrical infrastructure costs, protect residential bills and community water supplies, and says he will work with lawmakers to eliminate financial incentives. Abbott has also called for more local say.

Quite a bit of this was moving before tonight.

Hinojosa’s plan includes union labor, local input, limits on NDAs, ending subsidies and protecting households from higher utility costs.

She also wants water contracts to become unenforceable if they threaten a community’s supply.

So there is still a path to building data centers in her proposal.

It comes with more conditions around how they get built and who pays.

There is no final timetable or approval process yet, and meeting that list of conditions would not itself guarantee a permit.

A November report described her position as pausing new permits rather than stopping projects already under construction.

A building already underway could still need another permit or a change to one it has so the adopted rules would decide how that works.

A few months followed by workable rules is one thing. Projects waiting much longer is another. Hinojosa adds conditions to what was already being discussed under Abbott, but how long projects wait could matter just as much as the eventual cost of those conditions.

The hyperscalers

For this specific setup, I think MSFT is the best positioned of the cloud companies. GOOGL and AMZN could benefit too, but I am more mixed on those because of what a delay could do to the next round of growth.

META is a different case because the payoff is mainly in its own advertising business. And ORCL looks more exposed to the delivery side of this. A lot depends on getting the next facilities earning.

Think about the amount of money these companies are putting into infrastructure.

The spending happens before the facility is fully earning. If they can keep growing revenue from what they already have while the next wave of construction slows, more of that revenue can catch up with the money already spent.

That is the supply-discipline idea.

Strong demand, less new capacity coming through, and more business going through the facilities already there. Better utilization and potentially better pricing, with spending spread over more time.

Microsoft gives us a useful example of the first part. It described engineering improvements that unlocked more CPU capacity and let it allocate more GPU capacity to customers, even with some third-party deliveries slipping.

More useful capacity from the same infrastructure. That is great.

The engineering work is creating the benefit there. The late capacity could still have served more demand. But it shows why getting more out of what they already own can matter so much when new capacity takes longer to arrive.

That is why I put MSFT first here. We have an actual example of them finding more capacity in the infrastructure they already have. They still need the new buildings, but this gives them a way to keep adding business while some of that capacity is late.

When we think about pricing, AWS says new GPU Capacity Block prices depend on supply and demand, while the price stays fixed once a block is purchased. Tighter supply could help new bookings.

This is part of why I think AMZN could benefit. More demand going through existing AWS capacity, potentially at better prices on new bookings, while some spending gets spread out. But it only works if they can keep serving that demand. If the next customer needs the building that is delayed, they lose or defer that revenue too.

Then look at Oracle. It says 75% of its Abilene capacity had been delivered as of September. More is still coming, including expected customer delivery at Shackelford beginning in the first half of 2027. So Oracle has capacity already coming through and more business dependent on the next deliveries.

When we look at Oracle’s 1 GW example we see that spending starts before service delivery, and revenue takes several more quarters to reach its full ramp. This is an illustrative, non-GAAP contract model, separate from a particular Texas site. The timing is the point.

Move a payment out and cash flow can look better this quarter. Move the customer’s delivery out too and the revenue gets pushed back. Depending on the agreements, some lease, financing or equipment costs could keep running.

That makes ORCL the harder one for me to call a beneficiary. Its existing capacity could still get more valuable, but delivering the next facilities is how it turns more of the business it has signed into revenue. I see more execution risk here than a simple benefit from slower spending.

Google gives us the other side. It expects near-term Cloud margin pressure as it uses more third-party compute to meet demand.

That is why I am more mixed on GOOGL for this specific update. It could get the same benefit from fuller facilities and less spending at once, but it is already using outside capacity to serve customers. If its own capacity takes longer, that extra cost could stick around longer too.

You are waiting for your own building, so you pay someone else to serve the customer. Less construction spending today can come with a bigger operating bill.

That is why a slower buildout could help the hyperscalers that can keep adding business to existing facilities, while hurting the ones that need the next building to serve their next customer.

Better cash flow for a quarter is only part of it. They still need to earn more from the investment over time.

META is a different setup

META mostly earns from advertising. Its AI infrastructure helps improve recommendations, engagement and the performance of its ad systems. It is not mainly trying to sell the next block of cloud capacity to an outside customer.

And Meta says those AI investments are already adding advertising revenue through better engagement, recommendations and monetization.

So for META, the opportunity is more about the pace of spending. If the ad business keeps benefiting from what they already built while the next wave of investment gets spread out, that could help cash flow.

The broader AI infrastructure trade

A pause in Texas does not by itself tell us that demand for AI has gone away obviously. It can change where the infrastructure gets built, when it gets built and what it costs.

If a project moves to another state, much of the demand for chips, networking, cooling and power equipment could move with it.

If the project is delayed, some orders or revenue could shift into later periods.

If it gets cancelled or becomes too expensive to justify, that is a bigger problem than timing.

This is also why the hyperscalers and the infrastructure suppliers could react differently to the same news.

The reaction in semis is going to be broader than just Texas though. We are seeing just how strong the opposition in communities is to Data centers, and many Democrats are going to push back on development. Regardless of what actually happens with the physical building, sentiment will drive this market in the near term.

And that sentiment is heavy for AI infra.
