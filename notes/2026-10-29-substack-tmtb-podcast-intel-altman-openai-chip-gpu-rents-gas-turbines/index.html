THE SIGNAL

  • Collison disclosed AI tokens are roughly 1% of Stripe’s OpEx, which he called too low and expects to reach 5, 10, then 20%; Altman agreed many companies will settle at tens of percent of OpEx.- Altman said DRAM makers historically spent about $20B of capex to earn about $19B, but now each may generate around $100B of free cash flow a year, and bankruptcy memories keep capex conservative, prolonging the shortage.- Altman argued local inference wastes scarce DRAM, since PC memory is used about 5% of the time versus high cloud utilization; (our read) this pushes back on the idea that on-device AI eases data-center memory demand.- Altman floated OpenAI possibly selling its own chip, which he said is strong on intelligence per watt; he called tape-out design a good task for AI and said only one company has mastered leading-edge lithography.

01 PHP vulnerabilities and the security overhang

Altman explains that “PHP week” emerged because models have been quietly improving at finding real-world vulnerabilities for a while, but only recently have people systematically pointed them at the vast surface area of existing code, which he expects to reveal far more serious vulnerabilities than anyone anticipated. Rather than just patching PHP, he and Collison discuss whether entire software architectures need rearchitecting — a shift Altman says would have seemed impossible two years ago but now looks achievable as startups build entirely new technology stacks using AI.

On the cyber capabilities question, Altman is self-critical about OpenAI’s public messaging, arguing the field has failed to explain why AI’s risks are worth the tradeoffs, likening weak industry messaging to a bad sales pitch. He defends OpenAI’s “iterative deployment” philosophy — pushing capable tools into the world early even when unpopular within the AI safety community — as the only workable path to avoid dangerous capability overhangs. On early results from the cyber initiative, he says the first days went better than his worst fears, with companies using models for rapid patching and defense, though he’s careful not to declare victory, and notes cyber experts are no longer confident their hardware-exploit threat models hold.

02 Sandboxing, alignment, and the case for swarms

Altman lays out a staged view of AI safety: sandboxing works to a point, then monitoring (including chain-of-thought and activation monitoring) works further, but beyond that, safety requires near-total confidence that a model is faithfully aligned with operators’ intent and values human control of the future indefinitely. He is explicit that a model dramatically smarter than the best security researcher raises containment questions he doesn’t have answers to.

As an alternative, Altman is drawn to agent teams with competing incentives as a promising safety direction, arguing they mirror the resilience properties of pluralistic, democratic societies rather than a single “brain.” He states he would much rather have a trillion AIs of slightly superhuman intelligence join society than one AI of a trillion IQ, presenting this as his own preference and an open research direction rather than a solved framework.

03 Compute, AI-driven research, and token budgets

Discussing test-time compute, Altman traces a rapid historical arc: a few years ago throwing more than roughly a dollar of compute at a problem yielded no benefit, but chain-of-thought reasoning unlocked productive use of massive parallel compute, enabling today’s agent teams to run thousands of copies simultaneously. He says the gap between elite AI users (advanced math, hacking) and typical corporate usage keeps widening because most organizations haven’t adopted swarm-style compute spending.

Internally, OpenAI avoids rewarding high compute usage — there are no token leaderboards, and topping one would not help a researcher’s career — instead giving researchers wide latitude unless usage looks abusive. On Navier-Stokes specifically, Altman estimates the proof cost around $10 million, with related math work totaling hundreds of millions of dollars, and predicts OpenAI could redo it a year after the original proof at roughly a hundredth of the cost, with smarter models and what it has learned about scaling agent teams (model size, copy count, rollout length). He acknowledges mathematicians’ unhappiness about AI solving open problems but frames it as ultimately good for the world, while admitting personal wistfulness and even sadness watching models rapidly solve problems that previously defined mathematicians’ careers.

On recursive self-improvement, Altman says OpenAI doesn’t yet have a codified method for AI-driven AI research beyond asking for concrete things like a modest compute-efficiency win — discovering something as foundational as the transformer architecture remains unsolved, and he expects to learn much more about optimal swarm usage in the coming months.

04 Compute shortage, chips, and robotics-built data centers

Altman argues the “invisible hand” is currently failing to resolve compute shortages because memory makers, scarred by historical boom-bust cycles, remain conservative on capex despite now-enormous profits. He says the solution requires new entrants and supply-chain innovation — citing new actuator makers for industrial robots and OpenAI’s own chip effort, which he says targets strong performance per watt of intelligence. He frames OpenAI’s robotics program as eventually enabling self-replicating data centers (robots building fabs, turbines, solar panels, and data centers), which he says gets far less attention than the usual picture of recursive self-improvement as AI doing AI research.

On cost targets, Altman takes $70 billion per gigawatt as an illustrative five-year total cost of ownership today (estimates vary, he notes) and says he’d like to see that fall toward $20 billion through cheaper energy, chips, and supply-chain margin compression, describing himself as pro-deflation.

05 Personal agents, agentic commerce, and pricing

Altman says personal agents (referencing OpenAI’s Dots and competitors) haven’t yet had their breakthrough “Macintosh moment” — people like them but aren’t yet dependent on them — though he believes that moment is close. Both he and Collison argue businesses resisting agentic interactions (preferring their own apps and forms) are mistaken, drawing a parallel to the iPhone app-store era when companies reluctantly ceded distribution control; Altman says he’d choose Amazon through ChatGPT almost every time regardless of interface, citing trust in fulfillment over UI.

On pricing, Altman says OpenAI nearly stopped pricing its next model in tokens, quoting a price per prompt and billing like AWS, but customer reaction was strongly negative; people he asked would rather pay more in a consistent unit than get an opaque price. He also distances OpenAI from “bench-maxing,” claiming the company is currently better than peers at avoiding benchmark gaming, while admitting it hasn’t always been.

  • Farley says Anthropic’s widely reported S-1 loss is mostly a non-cash GAAP mark on a convertible note issued to Amazon; netting cloud partners’ cut, he puts his implied gross margin (training excluded) nearer 82-83% than 88%.- Farley thinks Google Cloud’s growth includes TPUs sold to Anthropic through a Broadcom lease structure described in the S-1, and calls cloud clients extraordinarily concentrated in OpenAI and Anthropic.- Hou says SK hynix’s enormous latest quarterly profit still earns it a lower multiple, and ties that and CoreWeave’s and Nebius’s weakness despite surging rentals to market doubt that these earnings can last.- Farley says Oracle’s investor day hinted at favoring free cash flow over capex, and that a round of big spenders not raising capex, Microsoft included, would make it a different environment for AI hardware.

01 GPU rental surge and the crunch spread

Hou presents Silicon Data’s core chart tracking a token price index against GPU rental rates for H100, B200 and B300. The token index has drifted sideways-to-down since late May — which bears read as commoditization and deflation — while rental rates for the most advanced chips have risen sharply in recent weeks. Hou notes that within the token index, open and proprietary model sub-indices haven’t actually fallen much individually; the headline decline reflects substitution toward cheaper open models, even as enterprises (per Anthropic’s S-1) show strong adoption of pricier frontier models like Opus. He says he believes Anthropic disclosed slower Q3 revenue growth at a recent investor day, though he cites Ramp data suggesting a reacceleration tied to Opus’s popularity.

02 Hyperscaler capex and profitability signals

Farley and co-host Max Wiethe note Google and Microsoft both reported strong cloud growth but came in under capex expectations for the quarter, raising questions about whether the focus is shifting from capex to ROI. Wiethe relays a listener’s note from Alphabet’s call that Google held its capex guidance unchanged, still expecting a significant increase in 2027. Farley says Alphabet’s miss came in search, while its cloud business grew far faster and more profitably — framing why executives remain enthusiastic about AI investment. Hou argues capex and stock performance are linked: companies won’t keep raising capex if markets don’t reward the spend. Both agree visibility is poor — physical constraints like power and land, not demand, are described as the current bottleneck, with investment numbers described as reaching a scale equivalent to fractions of GDP, even top executives can’t reliably forecast years out.

03 Rates, credit spreads, and the Fed

Asked whether borrowers can bear higher rates after Fed Chair Warsh welcomed tightening financial conditions, Hou argues that modest rate moves are unlikely to deter AI investment on their own; the real risk is if the Fed concludes AI-driven demand is itself inflationary and reacts aggressively to slow it. On the surge in AI data-center bond yields (Farley cites CoreWeave at up to 15%), Hou attributes the widening credit spreads not to higher base rates but to the bond market’s persistent doubt that AI investments will deliver the outsized returns bulls expect.

04 Compute futures and the forward curve

Hou explains Silicon Data’s term curves (feeding CME’s planned compute futures, still pending CFTC approval) show rental forward rates for H100 and B200 shifting from a downward-sloping, backwardated structure roughly a year ago — the structure CoreWeave used to sell long-dated capacity at fixed prices — to an upward-sloping curve today, meaning clouds are now reluctant to lock in long contracts without being paid a premium. He compares this to airlines’ jet-fuel hedging dilemma and a Japanese memory maker that locked in forward sales and missed a subsequent price surge. He cautions the curve is not a simple straight line, the front still offers a discount for reserved capacity, and the long end is thinly traded, which Wiethe likens to the rarely traded 20-year Treasury.

05 Depreciation and chip residual value

Using a Silicon Data model, which Hou says NVIDIA also used in a roadshow, assuming an eight-year maximum physical chip lifespan with discounted observed and forward rental cash flows, Hou shows residual values for the chips on Silicon Data’s charts, the A100 included, sitting well above what a standard accelerated-depreciation schedule would imply, even years after release. He attributes this to strong AI demand adding to rather than replacing existing chip capacity: new chips haven’t pushed older ones toward obsolescence, though depreciation is still occurring, just more slowly than many expected.

06 Anthropic’s economics and the AI moat debate

Farley infers an 88% Q2 gross margin from Anthropic’s S-1, noting it excludes training costs, and cites an 8% Q2 operating margin, a separate measure, arguing this undercuts claims that frontier labs sell tokens below cost, while Wiethe cites Gavin Baker’s observation that half of Fortune 500 companies have adopted Anthropic at roughly 0.1% of their expenses, by Baker’s reading of the S-1. Hou agrees margins for serving tokens are strongly positive but stresses the real debate is forward-looking: whether Anthropic’s growth, which he notes its own S-1 warns won’t continue at this pace, and its competitive edge can be sustained as the market matures. On whether cheaper Chinese or open models threaten incumbents, Hou argues switching costs — enterprise onboarding, compliance, user experience, and agents becoming increasingly model-agnostic — create stickiness analogous to iPhone lock-in, while acknowledging he hasn’t personally used Chinese models and that economics could eventually push large-scale enterprises toward cheaper alternatives.

07 Semiconductor consolidation

The conversation closes on a report, relayed by Wiethe, that Marvell is in advanced talks to acquire optics maker Lumentum Holdings. Farley frames this as part of a broader consolidation trend in the semiconductor supply chain, where fewer suppliers for critical processes like EUV, etch, and deposition are gaining pricing power, while Wiethe notes Lumentum was trading near its highs rather than a depressed level, suggesting conviction in optics’ role in AI infrastructure.

01 iPhone Duo’s defect and PR fumble

Gurman describes investigating the crack issue after it surfaced on Twitter and Reddit: a bar inside the hinge gets stuck under the display and punctures the screen during normal opening and closing, not from deliberate stress tests. He says he crowdsourced evidence overnight before publishing, and Apple responded only 18 minutes after his story ran, having said nothing until then. He also relays a friend’s story of a day-one camera failure that Apple’s diagnostics misattributed to a replacement with a non-genuine part, with no replacement units available at the Apple Store and no follow-up call despite a promise — illustrating, in Gurman’s account, a broken resolution process for affected customers. He pushes back on influencers blaming consumers who treated their devices normally, calling that framing ridiculous and saying it feels like something Apple itself would want people to believe.

02 Software quirks versus overblown complaints

Gurman argues initial reviews overweighted problems — camera quality, Touch ID enrollment, and app compatibility — with app issues mostly resolved within the device’s first week as developers pushed updates and the camera and Touch ID concerns overblown (he notes the SDK reportedly arrived only one to two weeks before launch). The real unresolved issues, per Gurman, are software quirks: picture-in-picture FaceTime, which he believes works on the outer screen, doesn’t work on the inner screen, and pulling down a notification to reply hasn’t worked for him there either. He personally finds Touch ID a complete nonissue and says he uses the inner screen roughly 75% of the time, calling the Duo an absolute home-run product apart from the hardware defect and software bugs; he also recommends Apple’s cheaper standard case over the bulkier Folio.

03 MacBook Pro, Mac Studio, and design choices

Gurman confirms the new MacBook Pro adds touch, OLED, and Dynamic Island while keeping the existing M5 Pro/M5 Max chips, with M7 Pro/Max versions expected late next year; weight drops from 4.7 to 4 pounds. He is skeptical of the touch screen’s utility, calling it something Apple had no choice but to add as table-stakes spec, predicting it will be little used, while favoring the OLED panel’s visual quality. Top Mac Studio configurations are pricier still; Gurman notes MagSafe and ports returned with the 2021 MacBook Pro design (reversing 2016-era removals), and the new model adds a light-up Apple logo, which Gurman speculates may relate to the tandem-OLED panel’s thinness and brightness rather than pure nostalgia, while the hosts note the crowd at the event cheered.

04 Siri, AI strategy, and smart home gaps

Gurman says Apple’s Siri/Apple Intelligence brand is damaged and argues Apple should have treated the AI relaunch as a chance to rebrand its voice assistant rather than simply tacking the “AI” label onto Siri. The hosts extend this into a broader critique that Apple is no longer an innovator, pointing to its new Home collection partnering with LG for smart-home cameras instead of building a unified in-house platform, and a new Home display the hosts call purposeless (Gurman says he likes it for FaceTime). Gurman notes a future mobile/robotic home device is planned for next year or later at a higher price point, and that Apple’s next major hardware push, coming next year, is smart glasses competing against Meta’s Ray-Ban-branded line — raising the on-air question of whether buyers will trust Apple’s own eyewear design over Ray-Ban. He also addresses why Apple keeps selling wired headphones (nostalgia demand, low cost) despite the AirPods push.

05 Corporate moves: M&A shakeup and App Store economics

Gurman reports Apple moved its head of M&A, Adrian Perica, toward the services organization as a potential long-term successor to Eddy Cue, putting his deputy in charge of M&A, with Oliver Schusser as another internal contender. Separately, Gurman says hardware leadership under Ternus pushed for a more active M&A approach after years of only acquiring small, undisclosed teams, which he says raises — without confirming — Apple’s odds of buying Instinct, a deal put at over $30 billion on air. He also recalls his newsroom’s earlier reporting that Apple makes more money from App Store games than from the Mac, prompting the hosts to joke about Apple’s reluctance to take a cut of prediction-market or trading apps.

THE SIGNAL

  • Brookhart puts net new ARR at ~$10B a month in Q2 and ~$4B in Q3, and expects $8-10B in Q4 after Opus 5.5; Meta, probably over 10% of revenue, reportedly halved Claude Code seats to 30k.- In Brookhart’s illustration, a ~16% revenue share on Anthropic sold through Bedrock, Vertex and Foundry is worth about $10M per inference MW on top of ~$10M of compute rent, which he says is lifting cloud AI margins.- Per the S-1, Brookhart says, Anthropic is in 50% of the Fortune 500 at roughly 0.1% of those customers’ expenses; top-5% Claude Code users spend $18k a year, non-Claude Code users well under $75 a month.- Consumption is fragile: SemiAnalysis’s own Anthropic usage fell from 90%+ to 70% after rival releases before recovering with Opus 5.5; 80% of revenue comes from models under six months old.

01 Where the trillion dollars comes from

Patel opens by questioning the core assumption behind Anthropic’s ambitions: if the industry is racing toward a trillion dollars in revenue, someone else has to be generating equivalent value to pay for it, and that diffusion hasn’t clearly happened yet. Brookhart agrees the market remains extremely narrow — even with $120 billion of combined ARR across the top two labs, only the top 1% of enterprise customers spend meaningfully, concentrated heavily in coding. He argues growth to a trillion requires expansion into verticals like cybersecurity (where 8-figure enterprise contracts can scale quickly) and biology, plus a shift from augmentation toward actual labor replacement in white-collar work — something enterprise Q4 budgeting cycles haven’t yet tested at scale, since Anthropic’s ARR only began accelerating this year. Brookhart also notes the competitive field has widened well beyond two labs: open-source model ARR grew from roughly $1 billion in Q1 to over $10 billion by Q3, and well-funded labs at Google DeepMind and Meta are scaling training compute.

02 Tokenomics behind the margin flip

Brookhart, who co-leads SemiAnalysis’s “tokenomics” model, explains Anthropic’s unit economics: revenue per inference megawatt has risen from the mid-teens (in millions) a year ago to $65 million today, with projections of $100-120 million per megawatt on Opus 5.5 running on Blackwell chips, while compute costs remain just over $10 million per megawatt. Only about 30% of Anthropic’s total compute actually serves inference revenue; the rest goes to training and research — the part of the business Brookhart says delivers long-term defensibility. He notes Anthropic is managing training spend against inference margins well, achieving positive 11% non-GAAP EBIT margins faster than expected, though Brookhart says the company would ultimately prefer near-zero or negative margins if it meant funneling more compute into training toward AGI. Patel separately raises the “capability overhang” question — whether models today are already good enough that the smarter business move is to stop training and deploy into new verticals (law, medicine) rather than keep chasing the frontier — to which Brookhart responds that competitive and adversarial dynamics (e.g., cybersecurity, legal cases) keep demand for frontier intelligence persistent, even as open-source and non-frontier models increasingly handle lower-stakes tasks like email summarization.

03 The $80 billion loss and dilution

Patel flags the widely discussed $80 billion loss that alarmed observers; Brookhart explains it as mostly non-cash accounting charges from revaluing warrants and convertible notes (Patel notes Amazon was the other party), triggered by Anthropic’s valuation surge — not an operational loss. On dilution, Brookhart notes Anthropic has only burned $10 billion of cash against $120 billion-plus in cash and marketable securities on the balance sheet (likely another ~$100 billion post-IPO), and argues that creatively structured compute deals mean dilution concerns are secondary if an investor believes in the long-term AGI bull case.

04 Supplier-competitor tension and compute ownership

A recurring risk factor: AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud are simultaneously Anthropic’s suppliers and competitors (notably Google). Brookhart expects both Anthropic and OpenAI to shift toward owning or renting-to-own compute via SPVs rather than pure leasing — Anthropic already buys TPUs (3.5-4+ gigawatts) directly through such vehicles. He also notes 48% of Anthropic’s business flows through cloud marketplaces (Bedrock, Vertex, Foundry), which functions as a de facto sales channel and is boosting cloud margins broadly; AWS specifically had a strong Q2 partly on Anthropic’s Bedrock growth. Brookhart sees little strategic urgency for Amazon to build its own frontier model, unlike Microsoft, which he says can now monetize OpenAI models essentially without paying OpenAI under their new agreement.

05 Neo clouds, compute gluts, and data center backlash

On neo clouds, Brookhart pushes back on Elon Musk’s “extinction event” framing, noting Musk has his own incentive to say so. He distinguishes well-backed players like CoreWeave and Nebius (which have hyperscaler, NVIDIA, or Broadcom backstops) from weaker, undercapitalized operators — hyperscalers account for 65% of net new compute capacity. On US data center backlash and permitting fights, Brookhart says the market will find ways to get sites built, though hyperscalers could respond to oversupply by leaving shells empty rather than buying chips. Separately, he raises the possibility of a 2028 compute glut as efficiency gains reduce megawatt requirements even as revenue per megawatt rises, questioning whether 2028 revenue will justify trillions of dollars in CapEx.

06 OpenAI comparison and the road ahead

Brookhart contrasts Anthropic’s enterprise-heavy, pure-consumption model (he cites 900% net revenue retention, unlike software peers’ high logo retention) with OpenAI, which he says was 60% consumer at the end of Q1 and only recently flipped to enterprise-majority revenue and reportedly posted roughly -100% EBIT margins versus Anthropic’s +11%. He suggests OpenAI delayed its own IPO partly to let a stronger Q3 (enterprise grew 100% quarter-over-quarter) build a better growth story. Looking ahead to Anthropic’s roadshow, Brookhart expects management to explain the Q3 slowdown (Meta’s internal shift, distillation enforcement), tout new verticals like a forthcoming cybersecurity SKU, and balance AGI messaging with conservative near-term guidance to leave room for post-IPO beats.

THE SIGNAL

  • Brough says the largest turbine OEMs now earn more profit on new units than on aftermarket, a first for the industry; if prices settle at a level still about double what OEMs expected five years ago, new-equipment margins stay structurally higher (our read).- Turbines in the 20-35MW range now sell for $1,900-2,500/kW, up from about $400-500/kW not long ago, so his expectation that prices will come down over time implies a significant decline from today, a risk for post-2028 pricing (our read).- His 2026 order forecast rose from about 101GW in June 2025 to as much as 132GW; GE Vernova’s ~116GW of firm orders plus deposit-backed reservations roughly equals Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi combined.- Brough knows of one to three unannounced jet-engine converters beyond FTAI and Nova Power, which could mean more competition for FTAI (our read); orders for 40MW+ units like the LM6000 and LM9000 are emerging.

01 Backlog and delivery timelines

Brough, tracking both order activity and declared backlog, now projects 126-132GW of turbine orders in 2026, toward the upper end of his prior forecast range. He estimates GE Vernova carries about 53GW of firm orders plus ~63GW of slot reservations, Siemens Energy roughly 70GW, and Mitsubishi Power around 35GW. Anyone placing a reservation today should expect delivery in 2030, 2031, or 2032, with some OEM product lines reportedly sold out five years flat, according to Brough’s conversations with OEM contacts.

02 Capacity expansion and the shortfall Brough sees

All three major OEMs are investing in capacity growth: Brough cites GE moving from 20GW to 30GW annual capacity by 2030, Mitsubishi nearly doubling, and Siemens rising from 16GW to 23GW. Smaller but established players — Baker Hughes and Solar Turbines — are also roughly doubling capacity, and Doosan is entering the market. Still, no competitor besides the big three approaches double-digit gigawatt capacity; everyone else remains under 5GW. Brough frames this as deliberate caution — OEMs remain wary of a potential AI-demand bubble and are avoiding overbuilding — even as he puts North American data center capacity demand alone at about 150-160GW by 2030. He agrees with Kann that if the data center boom continues, the big three — about 75GW a year of global capacity between them, which also serves the grid and other sectors — will not nearly meet the market’s need by the end of the decade.

03 The non-traditional supplier landscape

Kann and Brough map several categories of entrants trying to fill the gap. In the bucket Kann fills with established generator makers like Cummins and Generac, Brough calls the number of sub-3MW units being bought for data centers incredible — some sites plan 500-800 small units, which he calls a logistics nightmare likely to be the first orders cancelled if demand softens, though he concedes the counterargument that small modular units offer easier N+1/N+2 redundancy for bridge or off-grid power. Popular turbine sizes purchased today range from 7MW to over 100MW, with heavy order activity in the 14-17MW range (Siemens SGT-400, Baker Hughes NovaLT16, Solar’s Titan 130) and 25-35MW aeroderivatives (LM2500, Siemens SGT-A35).

De novo turbine OEMs — Boom Supersonic and Parallax — attempt ground-up designs; Brough is skeptical, questioning whether Parallax’s 10MW unit will become real hardware, calling turbine design from scratch potentially a 10-20 year undertaking. He recalls a former boss at General Electric calling a gas turbine a symphony in metal, reflecting how he views the extreme design complexity facing new entrants. He’s more optimistic about supercritical CO2 cycle technology, viewing it as a lower technical lift since it borrows from steam turbine technology, though he expects only a small market impact. He is bullish on jet-engine-to-stationary-power conversions: companies like FTAI and Nova Power retrofit used aircraft engines, addressing two technical challenges — replacing the thrust fan with rotating torque output and redesigning combustion for non-liquid fuel — while ProEnergy goes further, reusing only the external casing and re-engineering all internal components. Brough views tens of thousands of retiring aerospace engines as a viable long-term supply source. He’s less convinced by novel technologies like Mainspring Energy’s linear generators and Bloom Energy’s fuel cells, citing footprint inefficiency — a Mainspring set delivering 2.5MW requires ~6,000 square feet where 30-100MW of turbines could otherwise fit.

04 Pricing dynamics and the 2030 outlook

Beyond the immediate price spike, Brough expects per-kW turbine pricing to eventually settle down — still about double what OEMs expected five years ago — which he frames as a durable, favorable shift for OEM economics long after the current surge subsides. Looking to 2030 and beyond, Brough forecasts the overall gas turbine market begins diminishing around 2028, driven by renewables’ continued cost advantage (he states solar photovoltaic capacity additions are roughly ten times gas turbine additions each year and calls renewables the lowest-cost electricity source globally). Even in his worst-case scenario, however, he projects order activity a decade out remains higher than 2023 levels, suggesting a durable but moderating market rather than a collapse.
