On November 12, 2026, Nvidia announced that its founder, Jensen Huang, had resigned from his positions as President, CEO, and a member of the Board of Directors, effective immediately. CFO Colette Kress was appointed Interim CEO. While the market initially focused on potential market share shifts between GPUs and ASICs, we believe the more important implications lie in three areas: 1) a possible decline in risk appetite in corporate governance; 2) a potential contraction in financing for the AI ecosystem; and 3) a possible reshaping of value distribution across the supply chain.

Why Jensen Huang Is Difficult to Replace

The Key Decision-Maker Who Shaped the AI Era

  • From launching CUDA in 2006 and developing dedicated GPUs for data centers, to personally delivering the first DGX-1 system to OpenAI in 2016, and later supporting Neoclouds and Neolabs, Huang has played a central role as a founder in many of the pivotal developments that shaped the AI industry over the past two decades. CUDA generated little tangible return for years after its launch and faced skepticism from Wall Street, but Huang continued to invest, ultimately helping establish Nvidia’s ecosystem advantage in AI computing.
  • Its CPU business offers another example. The Grace project encountered significant challenges midway through development and ultimately adopted Arm’s off-the-shelf Neoverse V2 cores. Its performance still lagged x86 competitors, and it was primarily sold in bundles with GPUs, reaching cumulative shipments of nearly 2.5 million units. Nvidia nevertheless continued investing: the next-generation Vera adopts its internally developed Olympus cores, entered mass production in 2026, and is being offered externally as a standalone CPU for the first time. Meta, OCI, CoreWeave, and others have planned deployments, while Dell, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro are also expected to launch standalone servers powered by Vera. We believe Vera is now on a more solid development path. This kind of long-term investment, which spans multiple product generations and tolerates setbacks along the way, is often sustained only by a founder with the willingness and ability to stay the course.

A Major Backer of Neolabs and Neoclouds

  • Nvidia has invested in nearly all major LLMs and Neolabs, with OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Mistral, Thinking Machines, Reflection AI, Poolside, and others among its investees. In 2025, Nvidia participated in approximately 67 venture capital deals. We believe Nvidia is likely one of the most important backers of the Neolabs ecosystem overall. Its role is even more pronounced in Neoclouds: without Huang, it would have been difficult for today’s Neocloud ecosystem to take shape. Nvidia began investing in CoreWeave in 2023, signed a $6.3 billion idle-capacity buyback agreement in September 2025, and injected another $2 billion in January 2026. It also invested in Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe, Nscale, and others. In July 2026, Nvidia introduced a standardized program to lease back idle GPUs at fixed prices in exchange for a share of cloud services revenue, helping Neoclouds secure financing. Nvidia expects the installed capacity of its Neocloud partners to increase from approximately 3 GW at the end of 2025 to 8 GW by the end of 2026.
  • These investments and partnership arrangements have used substantial balance-sheet resources. As of July 26, 2026, Nvidia’s equity investments had reached $99 billion, with commitments of approximately $36 billion to certain AI cloud service partners. The company had also discussed providing guarantees for the lease of an OpenAI data center in Ohio, with the initially discussed amount reaching approximately $250 billion. According to reports, CFO Kress repeatedly opposed transactions proposed by Huang, including this OpenAI guarantee. Nvidia ultimately divided the guarantee arrangement into two phases in August, with the first phase capped at approximately $105 billion. This is a key reason behind our view that financing across the AI ecosystem may begin to contract.

The Final Decision-Maker in Cross-Divisional Collaboration

  • Nvidia has an exceptionally flat organizational structure. Huang directly manages more than 60 senior executives and rarely holds one-on-one meetings. He often makes the final call on cross-divisional resource allocation and technology roadmap disputes. As a founder, he is also willing to take responsibility for the potential failures of aggressive decisions—something professional managers may find difficult to replicate.
  • Tensions between more aggressive and more conservative groups have long existed within Nvidia. Many of the company’s supply chain issues in recent years have been related to the rapid advancement of new technologies. A single Blackwell system required seven chips to be designed from scratch and brought into mass production. In August 2024, reports of a design flaw and low yields led to changes to the GPU photomask, delaying product shipments by approximately three months. Huang later publicly admitted that it was “100% Nvidia’s fault.” Early deployments of the GB200 NVL72 also encountered overheating and interconnect issues, prompting multiple revisions to rack designs.
  • CPO (Co-Packaged Optics) was another project that Huang personally pushed forward. Debate over the technology roadmap among CPO, NPO, and pluggable optical transceivers remains ongoing. The latest Spectrum-6 switches are still offered in both pluggable and CPO versions. Although Spectrum-X Photonics entered mass production in 2026, yields for CPO optical engines remain relatively low. We believe Huang was uniquely positioned to coordinate this extensive collaboration across the former Mellanox networking team, the chip division, and the systems division. Following the management transition, the pace of high-risk technology roadmaps such as CPO may slow. As noted in our CPO report published earlier, “Deep|Optics: Hybrid Bonding and Testing Remain Key Bottlenecks to CPO Mass Production,” progress toward CPO mass production remains slow.

Betting on the Next Billion-Dollar Markets Yet to Take Shape

  • Autonomous driving and robotics are among Nvidia’s most prominent long-term investments. Huang has described these fields—which have yet to reach scale but could eventually grow into billion-dollar markets—as “zero-billion-dollar markets.” Nvidia’s platforms span DRIVE Thor, the DRIVE Hyperion Robotaxi platform, and the Alpamayo autonomous driving model, as well as the Isaac GR00T humanoid robotics platform, the Cosmos world model, and the Omniverse simulation platform. We estimate that Nvidia invests several billion dollars annually in these areas, while their current revenue contribution remains limited. However, automakers and Robotaxi operators such as Uber, Mercedes-Benz, and Toyota, along with numerous robotics companies, have already planned products around this full-stack platform. If the next leader faces pressure to protect margins, these investments—which are unlikely to generate meaningful returns in the near term—may be among the first areas to face cuts. Customers relying on Nvidia’s platform may also face greater uncertainty around product roadmaps.

A Leading Advocate for Token Demand

  • Huang is one of the most influential advocates for increasing token usage, and Nvidia has led by example internally. During GTC in March 2026, he proposed that engineers should receive a token budget equivalent to approximately half of their base salary, in addition to their compensation. He said he would be “deeply concerned” if an engineer earning $500,000 a year used less than $250,000 worth of tokens annually. When asked whether Nvidia spends approximately $2 billion each year purchasing tokens for its engineering teams, he replied, “We are working toward that.” In our view, aside from OpenAI and Anthropic themselves, Nvidia may have one of the largest corporate token budgets globally, and possibly the largest. If the new management team prioritizes expense control, internal token budgets and external efforts to promote the token economy may both lose momentum, removing an important role model for enterprise inference demand.

The Supply Chain Integrator

  • Huang’s support for the supply chain goes well beyond a conventional customer relationship. He often personally negotiates key terms with critical suppliers, reflecting a classic “founder mode.” Nvidia has worked with TSMC for more than 30 years. From its early, large-scale adoption of CoWoS and HBM in data center GPUs in 2016 to the transition to CoWoS-L for Blackwell and Rubin, Nvidia’s demand has been a major driver of advanced packaging capacity expansion. In 2026, Huang further linked capacity assurance with equity investments. In March, Nvidia invested $2 billion each in Coherent and Lumentum, alongside purchase commitments worth tens of billions of dollars and future capacity assurances. Later that month, it invested another $2 billion in Marvell, and in May it invested in Corning through warrants. In July and October, Nvidia signed packaging agreements with Amkor worth $1.5 billion and $1.3 billion, respectively, and prepaid approximately $1.1 billion to KYEC to secure US testing capacity. In October, it also signed capacity expansion agreements with SPIL, Unimicron, Ibiden, and Samsung Electro-Mechanics, covering package substrates, advanced packaging, and optical engines.
  • As of July 26, 2026, Nvidia’s supply and capacity commitments had increased to $279 billion from $119 billion in the previous quarter, primarily to secure memory and manufacturing capacity. Huang’s annual meetings with senior executives from Taiwan’s supply chain around Computex have also been an important barometer of industry confidence in capacity expansion. By comparison, cloud service providers developing their own ASICs still lag Nvidia in supply chain coordination: there are often two to three management layers between their CEOs and the leaders of their ASIC teams. It was not until late 2025 that Google and Amazon moved the heads of AI infrastructure and custom silicon to report directly to their CEOs. We believe that over the past three years, Nvidia’s founder-led credibility and long-term commitments have given Taiwan’s supply chain companies the confidence to expand capacity ahead of demand and adopt new materials and architectures. This is also one of the most difficult aspects for professional managers to take over.

Near-Term Impact (Within 12 Months)

In the near term, compute market share is unlikely to shift rapidly following the management transition, as key capacity has already been booked. Capital allocation and expense controls, however, may tighten first. The market is closely watching AMD, Broadcom, and Taiwan-based AI ASIC design service providers. From a supply chain perspective, HBM, advanced packaging, and high-end ABF package substrate capacity are currently close to full utilization, and much of the additional capacity already has customer commitments. Combined with the 2027 ramp of Rubin-generation products and existing compute financing arrangements for OpenAI and Neoclouds that are closely tied to Nvidia, meaningful market share shifts may take at least 12 months to emerge. By contrast, changes in financial governance may become visible sooner. With the CFO immediately stepping in as Interim CEO, new equity investments in Neolabs and Neoclouds, lease credit guarantees, high-risk R&D projects, and internal token budgets are likely to become more conservative over the next one to two quarters.

Medium- to Long-Term Impact (Beyond 12 Months)

The AI Ecosystem’s Financing Support May Contract

  • Nvidia generates tens of billions of dollars in free cash flow each quarter. In the past, the company supported ecosystem expansion through substantial investments in Neoclouds and advance purchases of upstream capacity. If professional managers move first to tighten new credit guarantees and long-term prepayments, the benefits of existing financing support and capacity subsidies may gradually fade over the following year. This could weigh on downstream Neocloud expansion, slow its growth, and bring forward a cooling in the broader AI capital expenditure cycle.

Accelerating ASIC Substitution

  • One reason customers have been willing to accept Nvidia’s gross margins of as much as 72%–75% is that Huang’s aggressive product strategy has continuously driven improvements in compute performance. If professional managers take control and the pace of technology development becomes more conservative, the total cost of ownership (TCO) advantage of AI ASICs may widen further. TSMC’s leading-edge process and advanced packaging capacity could also gradually shift toward Google and Broadcom.

Slower Hardware Specification Upgrades and Greater Supply Chain Pricing Pressure

  • The valuation re-rating of Taiwan’s supply chain over the past three years reflected Huang’s willingness to take on product delay and technology iteration risks, while introducing new materials and architectures with each product generation. Under a management model led by professional executives, product iteration may slow, specifications may become more standardized, and price comparisons and negotiations among multiple suppliers may become more common. With custom ASICs developed by CSPs primarily aimed at reducing total costs, we believe that, while wafer foundries and ASIC design services may remain resilient, much of the downstream system assembly and electromechanical components segments could face a narrower value pool and valuation pressure.

Conclusion

Overall, Huang’s departure may mark the end of a phase in which high-risk investments across the AI supply chain were driven by a founder. Even if total compute demand continues to grow and ASICs gradually take a larger share of shipments, the industry’s focus may shift from pursuing peak compute performance regardless of cost toward standardized specifications and TCO optimization. As a result, gross margins and valuations across many hardware supply chain segments may face long-term downward pressure. In this new environment, we believe wafer foundries with leading-edge process advantages and ASIC design service providers benefiting from the in-house ASIC trend are better positioned to sustain their long-term competitiveness.
