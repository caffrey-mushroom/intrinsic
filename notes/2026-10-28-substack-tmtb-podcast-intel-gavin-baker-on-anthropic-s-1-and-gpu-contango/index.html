THE SIGNAL

  • Bear case Baker credits to an analyst’s post: Anthropic devoting 74% of its compute to training, so any drop in training intensity could release a large block of capacity onto the inference market and weaken the tight compute-pricing thesis.- Cursor used to be a major demand driver for inference clouds, and SpaceX has since moved Cursor’s compute in-house, yet Baker says the open-weight ecosystem still sped up and diversified, which suggests less single-customer concentration risk (our read).- Baker puts compute contracted before May 2026 at roughly $10-15 per watt, now deep in the money; he says CoreWeave is actively chasing more spot exposure and fewer big offtakes, a possible repricing lever (our read).- Baker reads the blank October ARR line in the S-1 as a sign that management expects a strong number; he notes OpenAI accelerated over the summer and Anthropic clearly reaccelerated in October.

01 Anthropic’s S-1 and enterprise diffusion

Baker, speaking as an investor at Atreides Management, describes the S-1 as a clear positive surprise, pointing to the $10B cumulative burn, profitability, and cash generation as better than feared. He cites an annualized revenue figure he reads as roughly $67B off the reported Q3 run-rate. Despite this scale, he argues enterprise AI penetration remains very early — possibly earlier-stage than he’d previously believed — given how small AI spend is relative to total enterprise budgets even among large adopters. Hosts Coogan and Hays push back with the commonly cited Ramp data suggesting a small number of companies drive most AI spend; Baker counters that the S-1’s own numbers (250 accounts over $10M, by his reading; a host cited ~11-12K over $100K) show a genuinely diversified customer base, a claim he treats as more authoritative than secondary data sources. He also notes open-weight model providers — Fireworks, Together, Baseten, Cognition — have been growing faster than either OpenAI or Anthropic since summer, a trend that held through Anthropic’s recent reacceleration; Atreides led Fireworks’ last funding round, which he says was hard to price because the company was growing so fast.

02 Compute pricing and the CoreWeave contrast

Baker’s central market claim is that GPU/compute pricing has moved into contango: spot prices are rising, but forward prices for clusters delivered in 2027 are rising even faster, meaning buyers pay substantially more to lock in future capacity than current capacity — a shift he attributes to remarks at GTC by the founder of Silicon Data, a firm Atreides has invested in and which he calls the most authoritative source. He says portfolio companies that secured Blackwell clusters for mid-2026 are shocked by the pricing demanded for a comparable cluster in September 2027, despite broad awareness that new supply is coming online. He contrasts CoreWeave — which a host described as reliable “diamond tier” infrastructure, and which Baker says locked in long-term compute contracts now priced well under market — with SpaceX’s more spot-market-exposed strategy. Separately, Baker cites CFO Sarah Friar saying her own company’s long-term compute commitments could now be resold at a 3-5x premium. Baker speculates Elon Musk reasoned from first principles toward shorter, spot-friendly contracts with fast payback rather than copying any peer. He also flags NVIDIA’s new DSX pod (announced at GTC Berlin) — a preassembled, rack-based deployment — as a meaningful innovation because it could shrink the typical three-to-six-month GPU “energizing” lag, freeing up working capital that would otherwise sit idle.

03 AI agents, biology, and commodities

Discussing personal AI agent use, Baker says Grok Bot has been the most impactful AI product for him and his firm, describing roughly 17 custom Grok Bots he has built, after earlier stitching tools together with Claude Code. On biology, he notes AI-assisted drug repurposing has already identified uses for decades-old compounds to treat rare diseases, though he concedes commercialization timelines remain long and rates the AI industry’s PR standing at roughly negative five out of 100. On commodities/energy, Baker says his firm tracks natural resources and energy closely but hasn’t made it a large portfolio allocation, aside from a private investment in a company developing a cleaner chemical process for rare-earth refining, an area he says DARPA has also pursued.

04 Financing the AI buildout

Baker identifies an underappreciated capital unlock: U.S. mutual funds are capped on how much of a fund can sit in illiquid assets, so SpaceX and Anthropic going public will free up significant mutual-fund buying power for the broader private AI ecosystem once lockups expire. He also points to sovereign wealth funds — particularly UAE-based MGX and Mubadala — as already heavily engaged, noting Atreides and Valor co-led a Crusoe round with Mubadala. Looking to 2027, Baker expects accelerating hyperscaler and neocloud revenue growth, converted into high-margin operating cash flow, to finance a larger share of the infrastructure buildout than before — suggesting peak reliance on external financing may be near. On public sentiment, a host argues that as OpenAI and Anthropic shares reach millions of holders (rather than maybe 50,000 people today), AI-related sentiment on platforms like X may become less polarized, and Baker praises Musk’s, Michael Dell’s, and Brad Gerstner’s practice of distributing company equity broadly as preferable to routing wealth through foundations.

THE SIGNAL

  • Asked if agents let Shopify raise its take rate, Lütke said he would rather charge a lower percentage on more volume, managing to gross profit dollars, not margin; this caps agentic take-rate upside (our read).- Agentic order volume started this year from a low base, so its growth rates mean little, Lütke said, but agents increasingly drive considered, high order value project purchases, which points to AOV upside before volume does (our read).- Lütke says more Shopify stores are opening than ever, and skewing younger; Shopify is working so AIs can sign people up, with costs amortized across its billion-dollar merchants.- As a Meta board member, Lütke called Meta’s ad engine the greatest boon to small businesses ever, with Google’s slightly less so; agents threaten paid discovery unless trust-compatible formats emerge (our read).

01 Shopify vs. Amazon on agents

Lütke says he learned of Amazon blocking Muse only after Shopify had already announced it was welcoming the agent, calling the contrast cheeky but not new. He describes Amazon as representing a digitalized department store built on inventory depth rather than storytelling, while Shopify represents boutique-style entrepreneurship. He attributes Shopify’s openness to agents partly to having no advertising business to protect and to a deliberate choice never to build a traditional three-sided marketplace, since marketplaces force an operator to choose between buyers and sellers; Shopify instead sides with merchants throughout the stack. He notes Shopify has also announced partnerships with other agents, describing the company as serving small businesses wherever they sell — social channels, point-of-sale, craft fairs — not just its own storefronts.

02 Early agentic shopping behavior

Lütke describes a shift toward project-based purchasing through agents — examples include outfitting a home gym or sourcing everything for a weekend 3D-printing project with kids, where an agent plans, prices, and places orders, in some cases connecting directly to a home device so it can automatically reorder supplies like filament. He says agents today mostly help people do existing tasks better, with a next phase expected where completely new, digitally native possibilities emerge that weren’t possible before.

03 Sci-fi, optimism, and AI backlash

Lütke discusses rereading Neal Stephenson’s Cryptonomicon and marveling at its predictive accuracy on cryptocurrency, while arguing most sci-fi reveals more about the era it was written in than the future it depicts — citing a multi-decade dystopian stretch (the Hunger Games era) that he says contributed to tech-industry pessimism. He says his own estimate of AI catastrophic risk is zero, framing concern as overstated relative to the scale of economic value still locked in computation. On the broader AI backlash, Lütke — speaking specifically as a non-representative, Shopify-vantage observer — expects personal AI usage to erode pessimism over time, and contrasts U.S. early-adoption enthusiasm with a more cynical European stance (he notes Muse isn’t available in Europe) and a Canadian middle ground, crediting Toronto’s AI research legacy, including Geoffrey Hinton and the University of Toronto.

04 Working with Meta and Zuckerberg

As a Meta board member, Lütke praises Zuckerberg as an exceptional entrepreneur and credits Meta’s decision to open-source the Llama models with setting an industry norm that shaped his overall optimism about AI competition. He also references the public setback of Llama 4 and praises how quickly Zuckerberg rebuilt the training team afterward, while noting his own relief that Shopify, unlike Meta, isn’t a 70,000-person organization — a scale he says he isn’t sure he could manage.

05 Founder leadership and the AI memo

Lütke frames founder-led companies as having an irreplaceable asset: the ability to cut bureaucracy and direct the company without committee review, citing his 2025 internal memo on reflexive AI use as an example no professional manager could have sent. He confirms the memo’s two components — AI use factored into performance reviews and justifying headcount requests against AI capability — are still operating, now without active enforcement. He argues diversity among companies requires some firms to explicitly repel employees who are AI-skeptic, since such employees won’t become top performers at an AI-intensive firm, and that Shopify deliberately leans into this filter rather than trying to appeal to everyone.

06 Monetization, advertising, and competitive risk

On how agent startups will make money, Lütke says he’s unconcerned, expecting value exchange to take forms ranging from subscription fees to transaction-based models, and advises founders to focus on providing undeniable value rather than fixating on margin points — competing, in his framing, with the everyday convenience people already pay for. On advertising, he says the model will evolve but acknowledges a structural tension: an agent acting on a user’s behalf cannot also be paid to steer purchases without breaching the trust it’s meant to serve, and Shopify is in active conversations about new, socially acceptable discovery and advertising mechanisms, potentially using blockchain primitives. Asked about Shopify’s own risk in the agent era, Lütke says the threat isn’t impediments to merchant sign-up but the possibility that a rival could simply build a better Shopify, comparing the moment to incumbents being disrupted during Shopify’s own 2004 founding era.

01 IPO framing and efficiency story

The host opens by contextualizing Anthropic’s S-1 filing, noting trailing-twelve-month revenue per employee rose from $1.4M to $6.6M year-over-year, and describing a sharp swing toward profitability over the past year. He attributes this to two reinforcing “loops”—an improvement loop (better Claude → more use cases → more feedback → better Claude) and an investment loop (more revenue → more investment → more capability)—and notes over 50% of customers spending $100K+ annually use the Claude Platform, positioning Anthropic as a hyperscaler as much as a model lab.

02 Opus 5.5 launch and anti-distillation

Angela Jiang says the team focused on cleaning up Opus’s convoluted writing style and improving intelligence-per-dollar via pricing cuts. A less-publicized effort involved hardening the API against distillation after a public paper on stealing reasoning traces prompted responsible disclosure. Anthropic shipped preserved thinking blocks across turns, new compaction features (to avoid breaking customers’ existing compaction strategies), and protections against multi-account abuse, working directly with open-source harness maintainers. The Anthropic guests stress the motivation is safety—distilled models run without Anthropic’s safeguard classifiers—not revenue protection, since distillers pay Anthropic anyway. Jiang says measured distillation difficulty increased significantly post-patch.

03 Safety infrastructure behind every launch

Lesse describes model launches now requiring extensive safety-policy work: data retention decisions (Opus 5.5 launched without the data retention requirements that Fable has), cyber and life-sciences verification programs granting relaxed safeguards to vetted researchers, and a unified “trust grant” authorization system threading access control through the entire stack—including a built-in single-button global model-access kill switch, partly motivated by a prior incident where a model was rolled back. Jiang frames this as probabilistic product management: the team builds a portfolio of hedges for low-probability-but-nonzero scenarios (like unexpected government use cases) rather than committing to fixed roadmaps.

04 Claude Managed Agents and the brain/hands architecture

Lesse explains Claude Managed Agents sit above the raw Messages API, offering a pre-built harness so developers skip infrastructure setup. Architecturally, the agent’s reasoning runs on a durable server while execution runs in disposable sandboxes—designed first for latency and reliability (a sandbox dying doesn’t kill the whole agent) but, as one product manager insisted early on, its most important property is security: credentials live in a separate vault, agents only see placeholders, and a proxy injects real credentials on egress, so the agent never sits alongside the credentials. Egress allow-lists further contain untrusted code. Lesse distinguishes sandbox-breakout incidents reported in training/research contexts (deliberately adversarial environments) from production workloads, where she argues the brain/hands split makes that kind of breakout far less of a concern. Memory and “dreaming” (a recursive managed-agent-on-managed-agent system generating memories) are described as still active but lower-priority than earlier in the year, with safety work absorbing more team energy; a coming update will expose the underlying multi-agent primitives for developers to build custom strategies.

05 Scaling the platform: Rust and CPU scarcity

Lesse says CPU — not GPU — has become a binding constraint, spanning product services, sandboxing fleets, and surging CI workloads industry-wide. The team’s response included fully rewriting the Python Messages API monolith in Rust, a decision made by a team with no dedicated Rust developers; Claude wrote the implementation while humans designed the distributed-systems architecture and verified outputs via shadow traffic against the old system. Lesse ballparks the results at 40-50% CPU savings and a 10-20% improvement in time-to-first-token, achieved as a disciplined “port” that deliberately avoided scope creep, though follow-on work may let the API avoid resending full conversation state for long-running sessions.

06 Pacing, routing, and model fallback UX

Asked how a new frontier model release eleven days after public pacing commentary isn’t contradictory, Lesse says each release reflects passing safety checks across three dimensions — architecture/infrastructure security, online safeguards, and offline classification. Hosts probe a related UX problem: safety classifiers sometimes fall back mid-response from a frontier model to a less capable one, creating disjointed experiences (e.g., a cybersecurity-related query getting cut off after extensive reasoning). Jiang compares this to the broader model-routing problem, arguing perfect routing is impossible given incomplete task information, and favors a more unified, consistent-persona approach over disjointed multi-model fallbacks; Lesse notes the API offers both server-side and client-side (SDK) fallback-handling options today.

07 Internal culture: slop, DevRel, and multi-agent advice

Lesse describes internal debate over AI-generated “slop” — the team’s rule is Claude-assisted output is fine but humans remain responsible for quality, while discouraging over-fixation on stylistic tics like em-dashes. On DevRel, Lesse credits a host’s three-pillar framework (product, content, community) for structuring Anthropic’s platform DevRel team, hired in part to establish brand and voice distinct from Claude Code and research narratives, citing hires like Lance (technical, teaching-style content) and CJ (cookbooks and community). Closing advice for Opus 5.5: Lesse highlights its cost-efficiency enabling experimentation previously limited by token cost; Jiang recommends multi-agent setups with asynchronous, non-blocking sub-agents sharing uniform context as the key design principle.

01 From silicon patch to patent

Mahajan, a 34.5-year Intel veteran who moved from reliability and thermal engineering into pathfinding in 2000, says the EMIB concept emerged from a self-directed study: plotting how many wires could escape a die’s edge per package layer across earlier process nodes, and finding a steady roughly 10% generation-over-generation improvement. Questioning why not more, he reasoned that silicon lithography could print far finer wires than organic package substrates. In 2007, after an architect asked whether dies could be split and stitched (essentially today’s fan-out wafer-level packaging), Mahajan showed colleagues his idea; one urged him to patent it, and another suggested cushioning the embedded silicon with a soft pad to manage thermal-expansion mismatch with the organic package—becoming the first EMIB patent, something Mahajan describes as seeming almost like science fiction at the time.

02 The long build to production

Getting from concept to manufacturing required multiple enabling technologies to mature in parallel, per Mahajan: a passive silicon process stacking four thick, 2-micron metal layers versus the typical one or two, die thinning and precise placement of roughly 5,000 bumps in a tiny area, embedding processes borrowed from Intel’s bump-less-buildup-layer work, controlled solder joints mixing fine- and coarse-pitch bumps, and signal-integrity design for unboosted six-millimeter passive wires. The team grew from 7 people in 2013 to roughly 150 or more by the first half of 2014. Intel built initial parts in 2012, refined manufacturing through 2013, and by mid-2014 was confident enough to announce the technology publicly that August.

03 Why HBM came first

Three application spaces wanted fine-pitch interconnect in that era: FPGAs needing local memory, mobile devices optimizing competing Wide I/O 2, and GPUs wanting high-bandwidth, high-capacity DRAM access. Intel’s internal analysis concluded HBM represented the most demanding performance requirements, so solving it there would translate to the other markets—Intel started with four HBM stacks and scaled attach counts over time.

04 EMIB versus CoWoS

Mahajan contrasts EMIB with CoWoS, which he describes as dies sitting on a silicon interposer, on three dimensions: the interposer base must grow with total die area, shrinking die yield per wafer as packages push to larger reticle sizes; interposer-to-package TSV pitch requires careful reliability design and adds signal length; and CoWoS imposes roughly uniform fine-pitch requirements across the full die rather than localizing it. EMIB instead uses much less silicon area via discrete bridges that can be manufactured, thinned, and diced independently of the assembly process, which he says removes that base-size limit—Mahajan notes packages today carry on the order of 16 to 20 bridges, and that EMIB is scalable to panel-level processing. He describes the underlying philosophy as building interconnects only in the locations where they’re actually required. Mahajan says yield challenges centered mainly on the embedding step and on managing mixed coarse/fine-pitch attach, not on dicing the bridges themselves.

05 Variants, exclusivity, and what’s next

Intel has since introduced EMIB-T (through-silicon vias for direct top-down power delivery and multiple controllable power rails), EMIB-M (embedded MIM capacitors for local voltage-droop management), and demonstrated an active bridge with repeater circuits in a paper published last year to boost signals over longer bridge lengths. Mahajan says Intel Foundry is the only foundry packaging EMIB today, while remaining open to packaging dies fabricated elsewhere, provided specs meet its assembly requirements. Looking ahead, Mahajan points to industry-wide packaging gaps in power-delivery efficiency, cooling, manufacturability of mixed silicon/metal/organic composites, and materials/EDA co-design—work his team described in a Nature journal paper earlier this year—alongside growing use of AI to accelerate design optimization, yield-loss analysis, and predictive digital twins. He also describes Intel’s active work on topside co-packaged optics, attaching photonic ICs to boost package-to-package bandwidth density, calling the ecosystem still maturing but progressing toward true co-packaged optics beyond today’s near-package transceivers and pluggables.

01 Micron’s roadmap and the China subtext

Micron held an unusually transparent technology webinar detailing its HBM roadmap, including what Bajarin describes as a move to vertical stacking of HBM (vHBM, or iHBM), and its decision not to pursue 4F-squared scaling, which Scott DeBoer, Micron’s lead technologist, said the company had studied for years without finding a workable path. Bajarin frames this as partly a message to CXMT, which released a competing memory structure announcement the prior week, with Micron countering by showing it has a far deeper roadmap, capital base, and tooling to execute. Both hosts read an implicit “this is not a commodity” message throughout: by segmenting its roadmap into AI-specific product derivatives tied to real end-market use cases, Micron is signaling it expects premium ASPs for memory engineered specifically for AI workloads, a sharper and more customer-oriented pitch than typical industry capacity-signaling.

02 Lam Research and tightening WFE supply

Lam Research reported an in-line quarter and guide, yet the stock sold off despite what the hosts frame as genuine supply-side constraints — cleanroom and materials shortages — rather than any softening in demand, on both equipment supply and customers’ inability to take delivery due to unbuilt cleanrooms. Lam raised its next-year wafer fab equipment (WFE) market forecast to roughly $200 billion, up from about $150 billion this year, a number the hosts note was not even under discussion earlier in the year. Management notably declined to quantify what revenue would have been absent constraints — mirroring Jensen Huang’s similar non-answer on NVIDIA’s own supply calls — which Goldberg calls the smart move.

03 Meta’s vertical infrastructure edge

Bajarin, drawing on conversations with NVIDIA’s Ian Buck and Meta staff around the Prometheus announcement, argues Meta’s ownership of gigawatt-class training infrastructure is a structural advantage: it enables frequent creation and distillation of multiple model variants (treating models like “products”) at a cost basis he contrasts with OpenAI and Anthropic, which he says run on other companies’ infrastructure even with good deals from Google or Amazon. Goldberg adds that a separate hyperscaler contact told him Meta may be the largest AI data-center operator by gigawatts, reinforced by Meta’s founding role in OCP. Both hosts agree this repositions Meta alongside Google as a genuinely vertical frontier-model player, a shift from earlier narratives questioning its AI org. On monetization, Goldberg argues Meta’s real lever is engagement, monetized as ARPU across its properties, and says OpenAI and Anthropic still need to figure out monetization beyond subscriptions.

04 Compute demand from personal agents

Bajarin runs rough math suggesting that if half the internet’s users (2.5 billion people) used personal AI agents, that alone could add a large amount of new compute demand just to run the agents, with per-person annual serving costs estimated at $50–$100. He argues owning its infrastructure may let Meta keep these costs a little lower than rivals that don’t own theirs, while Google will eventually need to replicate the same monetization-via-engagement approach.

05 NVIDIA’s supply lock-up and Amazon’s warehoused GPUs

At GTC Berlin, NVIDIA disclosed new multi-billion-dollar substrate purchase commitments, extending a pattern of locking down capacity across lasers, packaging, foundry, and memory — a strategy Goldberg says leaves no competitor besides Broadcom able to match NVIDIA’s balance-sheet-driven crowding-out of supply. Separately, reports that Amazon has GPUs sitting idle in warehouses were cited by bears as evidence of channel stuffing or weak demand; Bajarin, citing data-center-builder contacts, calls this standard “staged integration” practice, where components are pre-ordered and staged for up to six months pending installation timelines that can’t match real-time demand the way iPhone supply chains can. Goldberg adds nuance, noting reports suggest Andy Jassy himself pointed to electricity shortages — not demand — as the actual constraint, and both hosts flag a broader risk that idiosyncratic supply snags (PCB shortages, gas turbines, electrician shortages, financing issues) could compound and eventually cause a company to miss a quarter, a risk the Street would have looked through six months ago but whose reception a year from now is uncertain.

06 Rising AI-enabled cybersecurity threats

The hosts close on a warning that CrowdStrike and banks are reporting phishing and login-probing activity at scale from unknown sources, which Bajarin says validates concerns he raised back in June. He cites a CTO conversation describing agentic security as about to receive a “blank check” in enterprise budgets, since companies are only now realizing — via live vulnerability testing — how exposed ungoverned AI agents leave them. Goldberg draws an analogy to early-2000s “script kiddies” weaponizing open-source hacking tools, arguing frontier AI models will remain critical both as attack tools and, necessarily, as the defense needed to counter them, framing cybersecurity (after coding) as poised to become AI’s next major use case.
