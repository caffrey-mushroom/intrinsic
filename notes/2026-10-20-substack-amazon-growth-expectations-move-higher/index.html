原标题：Amazon’s Growth Expectations Are Moving Higher

#cloud #ai-demand

Substack · 2026-10-20

Amazon’s latest analyst update gives us a more aggressive picture of cloud growth and the spending behind it.

I wanted to unpack what that means for Amazon shareholders and the AI infrastructure businesses I own, then also discuss Meta’s Prometheus disclosure and Nokia’s earnings this week.

Amazon

In my October 4 hyperscaler report, I explained why I’ve been building exposure to Amazon. AWS and Trainium were part of that, alongside the opportunity for robotics and fulfillment improvements to lift retail margins. Morgan Stanley’s latest note adds to the AWS side of the argument.

(Read my hyperscaler report)

They raised their Amazon target from $335 to $350 and named it a top pick. They now expect AWS revenue growth of 47% in 2027, up from 41%, followed by 42% in 2028, up from 37%. Compounded, that would more than double annual AWS revenue between 2026 and 2028.

I think that’s a pretty damn big expectation for a business already generating $42.2B in quarterly sales. AWS contributed $16.6B of Amazon’s $27.5B in operating income in Q2. Faster growth there can have a much larger effect on earnings than Amazon’s overall revenue growth would suggest.

Morgan Stanley also expects AWS operating margins of 42% in 2027 and 44% in 2028, with Amazon EPS reaching roughly $16.50 in 2028. Their capex forecasts are $355B in 2027 and $365B in 2028. That is $720B across two years, and there’s clear signal across the AI infrastructure trade. These are analyst estimates, rather than spending guidance from Amazon of course. We will hear more at their next call.

For the suppliers, a buildout of that size would support demand for accelerators and memory, along with the networking and power infrastructure needed to put them to work. I see it as an encouraging demand signal for the businesses I cover. The spending also reaches buildings and land, and higher component prices can absorb part of the increase. Amazon attributed its latest increase in 2026 cash-capex guidance, from $200B to $220B, to higher memory costs.

As an Amazon shareholder, I care about how quickly that spending produces paying workloads and attractive returns. Over the twelve months through June, they generated $161.4B in operating cash flow and spent $169B on net property and equipment purchases, leaving free cash flow at negative $7.6B. The business is generating substantial cash, and the expansion is absorbing all of it and more.

The earnings cost builds as the equipment enters service. AWS depreciation and amortization reached roughly $8.1B in Q2, up from $4.8B a year earlier. Morgan Stanley’s case requires revenue to grow quickly enough to support expanding margins despite those costs. I’m constructive on the opportunity because AWS already has a large, profitable customer base. I’d want the next results to show new capacity converting into revenue, with a credible path toward more cash remaining for shareholders.

Amazon also announced TorchNeuron, with a public beta scheduled for October 20. It helps developers using PyTorch, a widely used AI development framework, run their work on Amazon’s Trainium chips. Reducing the engineering work needed to switch hardware could help AWS win more workloads. It’s great that they’re improving the software around their chips. Wider Trainium adoption could give AWS more control over computing costs, with some savings potentially going to customers through lower prices.

Meta

Meta’s Prometheus disclosure shows how much networking is needed when a training cluster spreads across buildings. The first phase of the Ohio supercluster is complete and has already trained Muse models, including Muse Wonder. Hundreds of thousands of GPUs are deployed today, with roughly 1 million planned at full scale. Wild stuff.

The network connecting its building-level clusters spans distances from 2 kilometers to more than 100 kilometers. Meta describes millions of optical connections across the installation, including 800G-ZR links beyond roughly 10 kilometers. Those longer connections use coherent optics, which help transmit high-speed data reliably over greater distances.

Meta names NVIDIA hardware, Arista switches and Broadcom networking silicon in its description. For the optical businesses I own, the useful readthrough is the product demand created by this architecture. Coherent and Lumentum supply optical components and modules for high-speed connections, including 800G coherent products. Ciena, Nokia, and Adtran’s products help address the longer-distance connections between buildings and sites. These aren’t confirmed suppliers though.

The opportunity expands with the physical footprint of a cluster. More processing capacity distributed across multiple buildings creates more traffic that has to travel between them. That can support demand for both short-reach optics within buildings and coherent optics across longer distances. It gives these suppliers several places to compete as campuses grow.

As a Meta shareholder, the completed infrastructure has already produced models available through paid developer and coding products. I see that as useful execution progress. How much recurring usage they attract, and what it costs to serve it, will determine the financial return on the buildout. I think adoption of agentic will be lumpy but trend up over time as people figure out more use cases and it hits critical mass.

Nokia

The networking requirements Meta describes help explain why I’ve been spending so much time on optical systems. In my recent optics coverage, I discussed Nokia’s coherent technology and its role in connecting AI infrastructure. That gives us a useful way to look at BofA’s latest commentary.

BofA reiterated its Buy rating and target on October 19. The target converts to approximately $18 using today’s exchange rate. It’s pretty wild to see such an aggressive price target. There aren’t many optical companies with price targets that are ~2x their current value.

Their valuation applies 30× estimated 2028 EBIT to Optical Networks and IP Networks, versus 10× for the remaining businesses. I find that more informative than treating every dollar of Nokia’s earnings as equally valuable. My own models don’t do that either. As optical and IP become larger contributors, the mix could support a higher valuation for the overall business, provided the growth and margins come through.

(Read the BofA coverage)

Nokia reported Q2 AI & Cloud order intake of about $3.14B. They expected around half to become revenue over the following twelve months. At that update, the new San Jose fab remained on track to begin ramping later in Q4.

Going into October 22, I’m returning to the questions in my July Nokia debrief around Optical performance, IP execution, component costs and the San Jose ramp. I want to see how much AI demand is becoming revenue. The manufacturing exposure I discussed in subsequent optics updates becomes more valuable as Nokia ships products and improves its earnings mix.

(Read my July Nokia debrief)

Management already set an important expectation for this quarter. They guided to 3–7% sequential sales growth, with comparable operating profit largely flat QoQ because of software revenue timing. They also expected a meaningful improvement in Q4. I’d assess the report against that outlook. Flat operating profit alone would be consistent with what they told us in July.

I’d find strong optical/IP sales, continued AI & Cloud orders and confidence in the Q4 profit improvement encouraging. A capacity delay or weaker conversion of orders into sales would make the timing of the earnings ramp less convincing. With BofA assigning 30× 2028 EBIT to optical and IP, the stock needs that part of the business to deliver.

The price action going into this earnings look quite similar to last earnings. I believe that the overall sustained move will have to do more with the overall semis market unless we get some king of surprise.

A selfish part of my would like to see the 7’s for my long term port.
