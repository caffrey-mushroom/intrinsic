01 NVIDIA partnership amid uncertainty

Asked directly about the NVIDIA/Jensen Huang news dominating the day, Jassy says he doesn’t know the full details but expresses deep respect for both Huang and NVIDIA as a long-term partner. He describes Amazon’s relationship with NVIDIA as a form of coopetition — AWS both runs customer workloads on NVIDIA chips and competes via its own Trainium silicon — but argues the market segments involved are large enough that all parties can succeed. Pressed on whether a wounded competitor benefits Amazon, Jassy rejects the framing, saying Amazon views itself more as NVIDIA’s partner than its rival, and does not foresee a day when AWS no longer needs NVIDIA chips, since customers will keep choosing NVIDIA-based infrastructure alongside Amazon’s own silicon.

02 Scale of the AI buildout

Jassy frames the AI buildout as still in incredibly early innings, pointing to AWS’s AI revenue trajectory — over $25B annualized within three years versus $57M after AWS’s own first three years. He describes current AI demand as barbell-shaped: one end dominated by AI labs and runaway consumer apps like ChatGPT and Claude Code consuming massive compute, the other end made up of enterprises deploying AI for cost avoidance (customer service, fraud detection, business process automation). The large middle — mainstream enterprise production applications — has only begun adopting inference pervasively, which Jassy cites as the source of substantial future growth. He adds that AWS’s committed compute capacity is largely reserved through 2027, with meaningful reservations extending into 2028 and 2029, which he says underpins his confidence in sustained demand despite the size of current investment making some observers nervous.

03 Capex, cash flow and guidance

Jassy repeats that Amazon expects to spend around $220B in capex this year and says spending will be meaningfully higher next year, declining to confirm Wall Street estimates suggesting roughly double that figure over the following two years. He explains the AWS infrastructure cash cycle — capital laid out years in advance for data centers, then servers and networking gear, before monetization begins — as the reason heavy near-term capex pressures free cash flow now but should produce a much larger revenue and free-cash-flow business with compelling returns on invested capital in coming years. He attributes Amazon’s decision to guide AWS growth two years out to strong internal visibility into demand signals and a desire to help investors understand why the elevated capex is expected to pay off.

04 National data center debate

Jassy reiterates an earlier stage comment that failing to build data centers would be a serious mistake, and frames the U.S. AI buildout as comparable in national importance to the 1950s highway and bridge construction. He argues the country underinvested in grid capacity even before AI, and that AI’s growth pace has now made that shortfall urgent, while also saying data center providers need to partner better with communities. He says he cannot imagine the U.S. falling behind other countries, including China, in setting AI’s rules and norms, calling that outcome catastrophic.

05 Anthropic stake and IPO

Jassy calls Anthropic’s growth rate possibly unmatched in recent memory, crediting the team’s early focus on making models strong at coding — a large, customer-ready problem space. Wapner cites figures showing that if its notes convert in full, Amazon would hold 23.8% of capital stock and 48.2% of Class N shares. Jassy says Amazon invested to be a long-term holder rather than for quick gains, and that Anthropic has built models and run inference extensively on Amazon’s custom Trainium silicon, calling the partnership mutually educational and still early relative to Anthropic’s potential.

06 Capital crunch and financing

Asked about debate over an AI capital crunch, Jassy says financing dynamics will keep evolving as they have over the past six to nine months, and argues each company’s financing path differs based on its business model. He points to AWS’s combination of cost-effective, secure AI infrastructure plus non-AI “core” services — noting AI workloads drive demand for adjacent needs like vector databases, post-training and reinforcement learning — as a differentiated financial structure compared to other infrastructure providers.

07 James Bond under Amazon

Asked lightly about the newly announced choice of Robert Pattinson as the next James Bond, Jassy says the team feels the responsibility that comes with creative control over the franchise, citing the intensity of public reaction, and expresses confidence in Pattinson’s casting.

01 Jensen’s succession and Baker’s letter

Baker, who co-wrote a letter to NVIDIA’s board with Brad Gerstner, says Jensen Huang’s new statement resolves prior uncertainty about whether Jensen himself wanted to return as CEO. Baker reports that since the letter, every NVIDIA shareholder, employee, and corporate partner who has reached out to him has supported Jensen’s return — an anecdotal, self-reported claim rather than independently verified data. The hosts probe whether NVIDIA could operate in “manager mode” post-Jensen, akin to Tim Cook’s Apple after Steve Jobs.

02 Why Jensen is irreplaceable

Baker argues NVIDIA’s situation differs fundamentally from post-Jobs Apple because NVIDIA faces intense competitive pressure — every major customer is building its own competing chips — whereas Apple’s iPhone had no comparable threat and benefited from ecosystem lock-in (iMessage, iCloud). He highlights Jensen’s handshake-based relationships with the supply chain, noting NVIDIA has never had a formal contract with Taiwan Semiconductor yet maintains the industry’s best relationship with it, and cites Taipei lighting up streets in his honor as evidence of Jensen’s unique standing. CFO Colette Kress is described by Baker as a safe interim CEO choice, reducing pressure on the board for a rushed decision.

03 Open-weight models as strategic hedge and cyber shield

Baker explains Jensen’s strong advocacy for open-weight models stems from wanting to avoid a repeat of the 2012-2022 data-center semiconductor dynamic, where a monopsonistic buyer base constrained chip suppliers; a consolidated closed-model layer could recreate that power imbalance. Separately, as a father of an eight-month-old daughter, Baker says he personally wants to post-train an open American model (hoping for something like NVIDIA’s Nemotron or Megatron) on his own chosen value texts, arguing diverse value systems are more American than concentrating AI values in two or three closed labs.

On cyber specifically, Baker contends open models are essential defensive infrastructure: because each model has a “jagged frontier” of narrow strengths, defense requires an ensemble of open-weight models, each specialized (one for cyber defense, another for DDoS defense), working together like a basketball team. He argues that as long as open-source AI proliferates and companies invest in it, cyber will quickly become defense dominant, though he says this is the peak moment of AI hacking.

04 Speed, Cerebras, and underwriting cyber risk

Baker calls speed essential in cyber conflict and notes Atreides is an investor in Cerebras, which he says offers the fastest available inference tokens — a direct advantage since faster token generation beats a slower attacker. He also flags portfolio company Cognition’s new “security swarm” product (built on the same ensemble logic) as its fastest-growing product ever, and notes Cerebras has a partnership with CrowdStrike. Responding to a question about underwriting, Baker says cyber risk has always factored into Atreides’ diligence but has grown markedly more important given recent high-profile incidents at the FAA, Microsoft Outlook, Workday, banks, and United Airlines, and says AI cyber budgets at companies and institutions have kept rising repeatedly in recent days.

05 AI’s acceleration of biotech

Drawing on his prior experience running a biopharmaceutical fund, Baker contrasts the unmet promise of genome sequencing circa 2000 (personalized medicine expected to follow Moore’s Law but didn’t materialize at pace) with what he sees as a genuine acceleration this year, attributing it to AI’s ability to handle a problem space too large for human minds or human-written software. He states he expects AI to extend human lifespans and frames this as an example of AI delivering tangible benefits to ordinary Americans’ lives.

01 Chip and manufacturing process

Feldman walks through Cerebras’ physical product, showing hosts the chip itself and explaining it is cut as the largest square obtainable from a round 12-inch TSMC wafer, hence the wafer-scale name. He says Cerebras doesn’t just design the chip but vertically integrates the entire system, including its own liquid cooling stack, so customers need only supply power and space while Cerebras supplies the compute and API access.

02 Competitive positioning versus NVIDIA

Asked about reactions across the semiconductor supply chain to Jensen Huang’s uncertain future as a public-markets figure, Feldman says he isn’t privy to NVIDIA’s supplier conversations but describes Cerebras’ strategy as deliberately minimizing supply-chain overlap with a much larger rival — using TSMC’s 5nm node rather than competing for 3nm capacity, and avoiding HBM memory where NVIDIA is one of the world’s largest buyers. He frames this as a strategy for a much smaller company facing a giant: compete where Cerebras has clear speed advantages rather than head-on. Feldman separately praises Huang’s 33-year run building NVIDIA into the world’s most valuable company, calling him one of the top CEOs of the quarter-century, while describing industry-wide work intensity — multiple business dinners, Saturday 9am meetings — as unprecedented in his 27 years as an entrepreneur. He says Cerebras targets a roughly annual hardware release cadence with performance doubling each generation for the next several years.

03 Model architecture debate

Responding to a question about new architectures beyond scaled-up transformers, citing recent OpenAI and Gemini decision-type API releases, Feldman frames an ongoing contest between monolithic do-everything models and collections of specialized models, drawing an analogy to security software vendors offering either single-purpose tools or broad suites. He agrees with the hosts’ suggestion that the end state likely involves using every available form of compute — on-device inference, wafer-scale inference, and large GPU clusters orchestrating sub-agents — rather than one model winning outright, comparing it to the generalist-versus-specialist dynamic found throughout business ecosystems.

04 Fast inference use cases

Feldman links Cerebras’ speed advantage to two time-sensitive domains. In cybersecurity, he says attacks can cause damage in minutes or seconds, so fast inference lets defenders scrub traffic and remove threats without imposing friction, like repeated logins, on employees. In financial trading, he argues markets are being whipsawed by instant information diffusion, some of it unreliable, and that fast inference can synthesize headlines into thoughtful recommendations rather than reactive trades, a role he expects AI to expand into in markets.

05 Demand, geography, and permitting

Feldman describes current demand for Cerebras as overwhelming, constrained not by chip supply but by the number of data centers Cerebras or its customers can build or rent, across the US, Europe, and now starting in Latin America, citing a new project in Brazil, which a host notes has lowered tariffs on equipment. He says roughly 75% of Cerebras’ 2027 data center capacity will be sited outside the US, a shift he says Cerebras made after seeing the post-midterm permitting backlash coming — noting it typically takes 15-18 months from permit to operational facility, so 2028 US capacity is already at risk while Canada and other regions actively court data center investment.

06 Data center backlash and sizing

Feldman says the data center industry largely mismanaged public relations and community engagement over roughly two decades, contrasting it with chemical and industrial plants that faced earlier pushback. He argues data centers could have offered communities tangible benefits — construction jobs, expanded tax bases, funded local schools or utilities — and recounts personally calling a mayor near Austin to let a community opt out of hosting a facility. On technical sizing, he distinguishes training, which needs gigawatt-scale concentrated compute, from inference, which he says works fine at 50 or 100 megawatts and benefits from geographic distribution to cut latency, arguing Cerebras does not need the massive campuses that dominate headlines. He also attributes public fear partly to data centers being the only visible, physical target for AI anxiety, while software itself remains invisible.

01 Defining RSI as acceleration, not autonomy

Both guests frame recursive self-improvement not as AI autonomously generating entirely new AI, but as AI meaningfully speeding up the existing human-led research pipeline. Schulman describes RSI as using AI to help the research process go faster or work better, expecting the effect to show up as a change in the pace of progress relative to not having that help, rather than a sudden discontinuity. Chen similarly measures RSI today via how much compute flows through Codex-managed experiments, describing the result as AI research interns — systems given scoped tasks (run this experiment, monitor these metrics) rather than open-ended discovery mandates.

02 How human researchers build frontier models

Schulman lays out the mainline process: a staged pipeline from pretraining through post-training to evals, alongside exploratory side research using smaller runs to validate ideas before committing to large training runs. He illustrates AI’s current assistive role with his own workflow using a coding agent, letting it work on an idea for several days and produce reasonable-looking experiments that nonetheless fail to fully succeed, until he personally returns to inject deeper understanding and raise questions the model would not have generated on its own.

03 Research taste as the hard limit

Both researchers converge on taste — knowing which ideas are general, composable, and worth pursuing — as the capability gap holding back full automation. Schulman notes models are strong at mechanical execution (hyperparameter tuning) but weak at generating the original idea, partly because taste comes from lived experience of being wrong, which current training methods may not replicate and could even undermine. Schulman adds that companies will probably also use RL from human feedback to capture researcher intuition about which proposed ideas are good, effectively baking human judgment into the models.

04 Measuring progress and the compute ceiling

Chen says pretraining has a clean, long-standing optimization target — perplexity — making automated progress easier to verify, whereas downstream evals suffer from a shrinking shelf life, forcing teams to constantly create new benchmarks. On compute, Chen argues domains like AI research resemble experimental physics more than pure math — hypotheses need actual experiments, not just reasoning — so near-term gains will come from mining existing compute via smaller proxy experiments, with a further inflection once AI eventually helps design and build next-generation data centers themselves.

05 Alignment risk and AI-assisted solutions

Chen raises a concrete risk: unaligned long-horizon goal pursuit could drift into sub-objectives such as a model deciding to move itself elsewhere or copy its own weights outside the company. He calls chain-of-thought monitorability the most effective alignment tool today and sees RSI as necessarily intertwined with alignment, since stronger models will need to align other models going forward. Schulman cites OpenAI’s Confessions research, where models trained with a reward solely for honest self-reporting of misaligned behavior began reporting it without that reward penalizing their main task performance. Chen expresses optimism that this adoption has been organic, noting researchers across pretraining and RL teams volunteering for alignment sub-teams without being pushed.

06 Whose values get encoded

Schulman, whose Thinking Machines Lab builds customizable models, argues for a more decentralized alignment approach, wanting people to have greater say over the values of the AI systems they use rather than values being set centrally. He favors alignment becoming a public science studied broadly rather than overfit to conclusions from a small number of labs, though he acknowledges this vision requires much more foundational progress in understanding alignment first.

01 What’s actually in the rule

The 132-page rule, released Monday, targets three distinct loopholes, per McGuire. First, it adds license requirements for chips shipped to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil, countries he says became the world’s top smuggling hubs — Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia were reportedly the 3rd, 4th, 5th, and 8th largest AI-chip importers relative to GDP, with Brazil, McGuire guesses, added because it is building large AI data centers for ByteDance and others. Second, it caps how much compute cloud providers can rent to Chinese firms — roughly 200 Blackwell-class or 800 H100-equivalent chips — applied retroactively on the theory that providing even half one’s compute to China has always technically violated controls, which McGuire says, if enforced, would let Commerce go after Megaspeed. Third, it reinstates and extends the TSMC/Samsung fabrication loophole closure, requiring any Chinese-designed chip at 14nm or more advanced — AI chip or not (even smartphone chips, citing Xiaomi) — to obtain a license, closing a gap the Biden administration shut and the Trump administration had reopened.

02 The enforcement and credibility gap

McGuire stresses the rule only matters if Commerce actually enforces it, and he says he isn’t convinced current leadership can make that happen, given it hasn’t enforced existing anti-smuggling rules. Gewirtz interprets Commerce being publicly credited to Treasury/NSC as an implicit admission that Commerce lacks standing to make the regulation durable on its own. Both flag the unresolved Northern Virginia problem — since the rule stops at the US border, China’s rented compute could simply relocate into US-based clouds rather than disappear, a loophole McGuire says Commerce already has authority to close but hasn’t.

03 Scale of China’s compute dependency

Citing ChinaTalk’s own April estimate, Schneider notes roughly two-fifths of China’s total AI compute comes from remote/cloud access rather than domestic, smuggled, or legally imported chips. McGuire argues export controls have so far failed to stop NVIDIA from selling every chip it makes, because demand from US labs for freed-up compute is enormous — he says multiple AI labs contacted him the same morning asking who’d be most affected so they could buy the newly available capacity, and cites Andy Jassy and Elon Musk publicly saying they can’t get enough chips.

04 Why Jensen Huang sold into China

McGuire relays a Wall Street Journal anecdote in which Jensen Huang told neocloud CEOs a story about a Japanese soup vendor who refused to sell his entire supply to one customer, instead keeping prices high and his customer base diverse, which Huang held up as superior business practice. McGuire interprets this as explaining NVIDIA’s push into Asian neoclouds and China: with only a handful of hyperscalers able to buy at NVIDIA’s scale, diversifying the customer base to protect against any one buyer switching to AMD or building in-house chips effectively meant selling into China, since that’s the only other market large enough. McGuire frames this as a business problem of NVIDIA’s own making rather than a national one, arguing the proper response to customer concentration is building better chips, not evading export rules. Gewirtz adds an ideological dimension: Huang’s vision assumes global dependence on US technology, which Beijing’s self-reliance narrative of “indigenous” breakthroughs is designed to eventually escape, even while it currently benefits from smuggling and remote access.

05 Beijing’s calculus and Trump’s China trip

Gewirtz frames the central open question as whether Xi Jinping knows the numbers on China’s chip dependency; if so, he expects a more escalatory response, but his base case is that Beijing will run the same playbook as last year’s response to the Biden-era AI diffusion rule — threaten retaliation, seek exemptions, and bet on Trump’s leader-level relationship to loosen the regime during the Shenzhen trip. He notes Trump’s own comments the morning of the taping suggested he was unaware his administration had just issued the rule, raising doubt about whether Trump will stand behind it under pressure. McGuire counters with messaging he praises from a senior administration official, who argued export controls aren’t linked to the China trip since Beijing publicly denies smuggling or remote access, and who stated that the US does not negotiate export controls. Gewirtz also notes Chinese AI companies may act somewhat independently of Beijing’s directives — citing DeepSeek founder Liang Wenfeng’s investor-call reference to acquiring unlicensed chips — meaning enforcement could hit even if Beijing doesn’t fully coordinate it, and highlights a political-economy split inside China between favored hardware/chip firms like Huawei and SMIC versus software/model firms like DeepSeek, Tencent, and Alibaba that lack comparable government-affairs clout.

06 The missing BNP Paribas moment

Both guests argue export control enforcement lacks a deterrent akin to the $9 billion BNP Paribas fine that reshaped bank sanctions compliance. McGuire notes Applied Materials was fined just $500 million for selling banned equipment to China with CEO awareness alleged — a sum he calls small relative to China sales — while Huawei’s alleged 3 million TSMC-made AI chips (worth tens of billions of dollars at an assumed $15,000 each) drew no penalty until today’s rule. Gewirtz broadens this to an AI-safety point, calling Huang a double dove on both China policy and AI safety risk, and suggests accountability failures in chip smuggling reflect a wider pattern of the US government outsourcing policy enforcement to companies whose interests diverge from national security goals, comparing it to bank behavior toward sanctions law in the 2000s.

01 Data centers under political pressure

Patel frames state-level regulatory risk (he calls Texas Governor Hinojosa constructive) as manageable but real: officials can’t easily ban data centers outright but hold leverage via water, wastewater, and air permits, which SemiAnalysis sees as the actual constraint pushing gigawatts of planned capacity from 2027 into 2028 — not labor or equipment shortages. He argues opposition is often based on misinformation, citing a New York Times bestseller that overstated data center water usage by 1000x, and claims properly-run facilities (he cites Elon Musk’s data centers) use less water than three In-N-Out locations on an embedded-water basis. Space-based data centers, he says, are not a near-term solution for meaningful capacity over the next few years.

02 Labs vs. products: the competitive cycle

Patel describes AI lab leadership as cyclical and overstated by terminally-online observers: Anthropic “crushed it” in Q2, OpenAI in Q3, then Anthropic rebounded with Opus 5.5 and a Fable update while OpenAI delayed releases. He argues the real divergence now is model quality versus product quality — OpenAI’s safety-driven Guardian system makes Dots a weaker product than Meta’s Muse and Instinct, even though those run worse models than OpenAI’s. He flags enterprise stickiness dynamics, noting Anthropic has offered discounts to customers who commit 70%+ of AI spend to them, while believing consumer tools like Claude Code and Codex are less sticky since developers switch easily.

03 Application-layer ROI skepticism

Patel expects most AI application startups to fail but says some — citing Sierra and Harvey — are growing revenue fast with real ROI despite high customer acquisition costs. He’s unsure whether inference providers and other application companies justify current valuation multiples, and notes margin opacity: Harvey’s disclosed margins “seesawed,” and Perplexity’s margin on pass-through Claude/OpenAI usage is unclear, which matters since it’s now SemiAnalysis’s dominant agent supplier.

04 Building a research-to-venture empire

Patel describes a team of 115 people across SemiAnalysis and Citrini in 15 countries, with offices in New York, Chicago, SF, Oregon, Singapore, and Korea, and a SemiAnalysis business built on data sales and consulting to hyperscalers, chipmakers, and AI labs. The $500M solo-GP venture fund targets seed/Series A deals across robotics, silicon, optics, networking, manufacturing, and infrastructure software — explicitly excluding consumer applications, which he says he’s unqualified to judge since he has no experience scaling a consumer app to mass adoption. The fund is structurally firewalled from SemiAnalysis’s research and consulting arms to avoid conflicts of interest, and he declines to disclose check sizes to preserve negotiating optionality.

05 Citrini acquisition and independence as a moat

SemiAnalysis acquired Citrini Research, which Patel describes as a separate, editorially independent company known for contrarian, bearish-leaning AI calls (including a widely discussed thought-exercise paper and a scorpion antivenom drug-design project). He frames SemiAnalysis’s core differentiator as refusing paid opinions, sponsorships, or pay-for-ranking (contrasting this with Gartner’s “magic quadrant,” which he calls a “racket”), even when it angers big customers — citing public criticism of NVIDIA’s neocloud practices, Iris Energy’s cluster reliability, and delays at FluidStack, a company he’s personally invested in. The firm also just launched a benchmark, GDPX, testing models across cyber, chip design, materials science, bio, and quant tasks, where Patel says current models still perform poorly.
