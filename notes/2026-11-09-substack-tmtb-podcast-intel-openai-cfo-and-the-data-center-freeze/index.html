THE SIGNAL

  • Ads launched early 2026 and reached a $1B run rate by August, under 200 days; Friar claims ChatGPT handles nearly 20% of Google’s query volume and calls ads a high-gross-margin tailwind for 2027.- Revenue is now split 50/50 consumer/enterprise after enterprise doubled QoQ in Q3; with total growth at 70%, enterprise likely drove most of the quarter’s growth (our read).- Friar says the $1.25T pre-money is almost 50% above the March round; she also floated consumer hardware as a possible next growth option next year.- OpenAI is moving into hyperscaler resale channels, with AWS launched in the summer and GCP announced last week; Friar says enterprise agents come first partly because limited compute is being steered to the highest-value uses.

01 Midterms and the data-center backlash

CNBC’s Sarah Eisen opens by noting that Democrats campaigning against data center construction flipped seats in Texas, Ohio, South Carolina and Georgia in the midterms, framing it as a voter rebuke of the AI buildout. Friar responds that demand for AI is real and that her job is managing through scenarios, pointing to the 70% QoQ growth as evidence usage is genuine rather than hype. She describes a community-engagement playbook — commitments not to raise electricity bills, grid investment with utilities, water use framed as roughly two Olympic pools per data center with most water serving on-site workers, and a 300-foot berm in Georgia to control noise — alongside local investments like funded preschool lunches (a reported 70% lift in kids eating meals) and a small-business AI summit. When Eisen presses that the policy rollout looks reactive given its timing right after the election, Friar insists the community approach predates the vote and reflects grassroots engagement rather than reacting to cable-news framing.

02 Growth, jobs, and enterprise traction

Friar cites OpenAI’s Q3 figures as the core evidence of demand: 70% sequential growth, enterprise revenue doubling, and 1.2 billion people weekly across ChatGPT, including 300 million for health/wellness and 200 million for personal finance. On jobs, she references that morning’s jobs report (productivity up, unemployment down, wages up) as consistent with OpenAI’s internal economic research, claiming roughly 40% of ChatGPT usage falls outside a user’s formal job description, and that 90% of tasks on her own finance team are now agentic. She cites Cisco deploying OpenAI technology to speed its roadmap by 20% and save a large number of coding hours monthly, plus JPMorgan Chase adopting the tools for cyber use cases.

03 Cyber, safety, and the Navier-Stokes model

Friar frames cybersecurity as a strategic enterprise priority, noting the newly launched GPT-6 Cyber and warning that Chinese models are becoming increasingly cyber-capable, which she says requires arming defenders with the best tools. As an example, she says OpenAI found and helped fix two major PHP vulnerabilities in August before public disclosure, protecting what she estimates as 70% of the internet. On safety more broadly, she says OpenAI has paced the frontier by holding back a model that solved the Navier-Stokes Millennium Math problem (relevant to things like artificial heart fluid dynamics) until internal and regulatory confidence in its safety is established — an assertion made without further detail on timeline or specific risks identified.

04 Products: agents, ads, and chips

Friar describes the proactive agentic assistant OpenAI launched at Dev Day: Uber started it with about 1,000 employees and is now rolling it out to 30,000. Asked how this compares to Meta’s Muse, she says OpenAI deliberately started agentic deployment in the enterprise because the tasks are harder, the security bar is higher, and compute constraints favor starting where value is maximized — later extending it to the 1.2 billion-person consumer base. She also discusses OpenAI’s first-party chip program, announced over the summer, as a way to serve customers at lower cost and a long-term competitive advantage tied to the AGI mission.

05 Fundraising, valuation, and the Anthropic comparison

Pressed on reports of a lower valuation and smaller raise than expected, Friar firmly denies this, reiterating the $1.25 trillion pre-money figure and calling it evidence of Q3 momentum carrying into 2027; Eisen notes this compares to an earlier reported $1.4 trillion target. Friar says investors were impressed by the 70% growth rate at OpenAI’s scale despite a rough October for the public AI trade and an ugly prior trading day. Asked whether Anthropic’s S-1 — which Eisen cites as showing 85% gross margins and 45% compute margins — creates pressure, Friar declines to share OpenAI’s own margins, instead stressing OpenAI’s broader platform (consumer plus enterprise, multiple frontier modalities) and new hyperscaler partnerships with AWS and Google Cloud. On IPO timing, she repeatedly frames fundraising as opportunistic, says a public offering, which she ties to spreading AI’s economic value broadly, is in OpenAI’s future while today is about managing the business, and says the midterm results have not changed OpenAI’s plans. Asked by Eisen about a reported sale of data-center loans that included one tied to Stargate’s New Mexico buildout, Friar does not directly address the report, instead asserting that capital remains available for the strongest, most exciting stories.

01 The $4 trillion wipeout

Jason opens by noting NVIDIA, SpaceX, Tesla, AMD, Micron and related stocks suffered what he calls the worst single-day dollar loss in stock market history, part of a near-$4 trillion market cap shed across the week. He links this directly to uncertainty over whether data centers will proceed, says it casts a cloud over Anthropic’s IPO, and cites a Wall Street Journal report that a valuation haircut could now be baked in, putting Anthropic’s expected IPO valuation well below what Gavin Baker had projected on the show.

02 Texas as a bellwether

Sacks frames Democrat Gina Hinojosa’s defeat of longtime Texas governor Abbott as the night’s biggest shock, attributing it not to culture-war issues (which she reportedly declined to engage on in a New York Times interview) but to economic populism around data centers — the perception that they raise utility bills and consume water while well-connected companies profit. Chamath adds color from a viral video of SpaceX employees Riley and Katie presenting a six-gigawatt Cochise County, Arizona data-center plan (promising 40% lower electricity costs, no water use, 20% lower property taxes) to a hostile audience, concluding the backlash reflects real anger at seemingly gratuitous tax breaks for the most powerful companies rather than genuine engagement with the facts.

03 Hyperscalers vs. Neoclouds vs. foundational models

Chamath lays out a three-tier game theory: hyperscalers (Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Google, SpaceX) hold existing capacity and can choose to keep renting it out, or redirect incremental compute to advantage their own products (e.g., Google feeding capacity to Gemini to let it catch up against faster-iterating rivals like Anthropic and OpenAI, which he says are on a 60-90 day improvement loop). The riskiest path, in Chamath’s telling, runs through the mutual 90-day out he describes in the SpaceX-Anthropic deal: if SpaceX used it, he says, it could reprice Anthropic’s compute at spot rates he thinks could double or triple, forcing Anthropic to capitulate and exposing how far other hyperscalers are from optimized economic yield. He says Neoclouds face potential destruction absent aggressive compute purchases now, while Google’s reported takeover of a Crusoe data center is cited as an early real-world instance of hyperscalers taking over renters’ positions.

04 Regulation, nationalization, and the Second Amendment framing

Friedberg raises the possibility that some on the political left may seek to ban AI development, deployment and use outright, estimating this is more likely than Jason’s suggested 10%. Sacks states with certainty that Democrats will push major federal AI legislation, predicting a regime modeled on COVID-era social media censorship pressure — a federal department reviewing and approving frontier models before release — which he frames as a surveillance and censorship apparatus powered by superintelligence. Friedberg draws an analogy to the Second Amendment, arguing the real power question is no longer who has guns but who has AI capability, and warns of a scenario where the state gets access to superior models while citizens get a lesser version. Sacks separately predicts the practical outcome will be a trade-off in which government gains more regulatory power while a duopoly of closed-model companies gains regulatory-capture-style moats. Chamath pushes back, arguing any broad legislation will mainly entrench the handful of largest hyperscalers’ power via supply constraints, which he doesn’t think serves politicians’ interests either.

05 Permitting freeze and offshoring compute

Sacks separates the regulatory fight into federal (AI safety/censorship) versus state/local (infrastructure permitting) tracks. He disputes optimistic readings of Hinojosa’s position, noting she has said no new permits will be issued until new legislation passes, and cites a semi analysis point that even under-construction projects may lack permits (e.g., air-pollution permits) needed to actually open — risking stranded, half-finished projects. His base case: fully-permitted projects complete in 2027, but new major projects stall indefinitely given that both parties ran campaign ads attacking politicians who approved data centers, removing political incentive to approve anything for a long time. Jason counters that Democrats, now in a strong negotiating position, are more likely to extract concessions (free electricity, schools, tax revenue) than ban projects outright, since data centers can simply relocate to Canada, Mexico, Malaysia, Singapore, or the Middle East — making the U.S. a potential net importer of tokens rather than exporter, costing high-paying construction jobs and tax revenue. He cites Launch, his venture firm, spending a modest sum on subscriptions (Grok and Claude the main ones) for a 20-person team, estimating $2 million in value from them and predicting 500 to 1,000 times today’s need for compute as agentic tools spread, plus a coming trend of $20,000 desktop Mac Studio rigs running local models to avoid being throttled.

01 The widening-versus-narrowing debate

Opening the episode, the three hosts (Josh, Matt, Vincent) split on whether the Q3 2026 results widened or narrowed the AppLovin-Unity gap. Josh and Vincent argue the gap is widening in absolute dollar terms — Axon grew 55% year-over-year versus Vector’s 40% sequential growth off a much smaller base — while Matt counters that Unity is “off the mat” and newly credible as a structurally sound number two, a shift from a year ago when AppLovin was closer to a monopoly. Josh notes Unity’s Strategic Grow segment includes legacy assets Tapjoy and Aura, which he assumes are flat, muddying simple comparisons with Vector. The hosts also touch on election spending, arguing political dollars likely boosted Google/Meta/TikTok more than AppLovin, though secondary effects (brands shifting budget to cheaper inventory) may have had a small impact.

02 Growth drivers: models, targeting, and self-serve

AppLovin’s management attributes its reacceleration to model improvements that sharpen conversion prediction, since the hosts note a large share of impressions generate zero advertiser revenue. Josh illustrates this with a simplified example: better targeting can deliver the same $200 revenue from half the impressions, effectively doubling the usable CPM and unlocking previously unbid inventory — a dynamic Matt frames as “value capture” that benefits both platforms if executed well. Separately, Josh admits his June thesis that AppLovin’s self-serve product would unlock major new demand hasn’t materialized yet; management reportedly acknowledged the slower-than-expected adoption on the earnings call, partly because Vector may be absorbing some of that self-serve demand instead. The group also discusses three gaming growth levers for AppLovin: expanding wallet share within existing advertisers, pushing into midcore/hardcore genres beyond its casual-game strength, and refining still-coarse targeting — alongside new CPM-based billing products that let developers advertise earlier in a game’s lifecycle (e.g., soft launch), which Vincent calls a meaningful new use case.

03 Unity Vector’s acceleration and its limits

Vector’s 40% sequential growth — compared to AppLovin’s best post-Axon-2 quarter of 24.2% — surprised the panel. They attribute it partly to a FOMO effect after Unity’s strong Q2 earnings, which reportedly prompted advertisers to test Vector in August-September. Matt recalls Unity saying 90% of Vector’s quarterly growth came from existing customers, which he reads as advertisers moving to the new model, versus AppLovin’s near-universal market penetration. Matt flags Unity’s margin profile as weaker: stock-based compensation runs around 25% of EBITDA (versus a more controlled figure at AppLovin), and the low/no-margin Create (engine) segment drags on profitability. Runtime data adoption is rising — 90% opt-in for new projects and 30% of top 1,000 spenders sharing data — though Unity has stated it isn’t directly feeding Vector.

04 Buyout speculation and competitive endgame

On the reported Silver Lake/PIF interest in Unity, Matt is skeptical a traditional leveraged buyout (like the EA deal, which cost a lot more than Unity would and carried heavy debt) is feasible given Unity’s thin free cash flow, arguing any deal would need an outsized equity contribution. Vincent flags potential synergy and neutrality concerns — Unity exited publishing by selling Supersonic to TripleDot, and an EA/PIF tie-up could compromise its position as a neutral tool provider. Matt offers a hypothetical “crazy Matt scenario” where Unity makes its engine free and funds itself via a Vector-linked revenue share, though Josh calls this unlikely given lingering backlash from Unity’s prior runtime-fee controversy. Separately, the hosts note both companies may be benefiting from public-market visibility (versus a comparator like Mintegral) that amplifies investor and advertiser attention each earnings cycle.

05 Publisher impacts and inventory lock-in

Discussing whether rapid growth reflects a growing pie or a fight for share, Vincent argues it’s mostly share being taken from other networks, while Matt adds some incremental dollars likely come from other media categories shifting into gaming, plus a wave of “vibe-coded” apps generating new but largely unmonetized spend. The panel reiterates that AppLovin’s Max platform remains the primary competitive chokepoint: switching mediation requires a UA (user acquisition) replacement as strong as Axon, which Vincent argues doesn’t currently exist outside Vector. Unity’s ad quality tool is discussed as a minor wedge — offering more granular control than Max — but one the panel says wouldn’t meaningfully hurt publishers if removed.

06 Meta, Google, and closing predictions

On whether Meta or Google could challenge AppLovin on iOS, the hosts largely agree with a prior mobiledevmemo argument that Meta lacks sufficient incentive, given larger opportunities in its own owned-and-operated inventory (including a push into long-form video to compete with YouTube) and the modest revenue upside from matching Max. Looking to 2027, Josh predicts AppLovin remains uncaught as “the apex predator,” calling any real competitive threat a multiyear story. Matt predicts Unity won’t undergo a heavily leveraged buyout and that one of the two companies will miss EBITDA margin expectations within four quarters due to competitive spending pressure. Vincent predicts a third credible competitor emerges within roughly two quarters, built around reaching Day-28 (D28) targeting parity with Axon and Vector.

01 Elections reject data centers

Thompson and Sharp open by cataloguing the midterm results: by their tally, 43 of 44 local ballot outcomes went against AI data-center construction, and in several places officeholders who supported data centers were recalled — a signal Thompson says is arguably more consequential because it disciplines future, non-ballot decisions by mayors and county boards. The most consequential single result, per Thompson, is Texas electing Gina Hinojosa, who promised to halt new data center construction until state lawmakers, who meet only once every two years, can act. Texas mattered because it was the presumed backstop for all US data center growth — projected to host 25% of US data center capacity by 2030 — given its own power grid, abundant natural gas, and historically friendly government; all three assumptions are now in question, though roughly 130 Texas projects already under construction should still come online through 2027-2028.

02 Why backlash happened

Thompson argues tech leaders misread the opposition as a factual dispute (water usage, electricity costs) when it is actually about sentiment and deep distrust of big tech generally — comparable to how “fake news” narratives succeed by feeding pre-existing confirmation bias rather than creating false beliefs. He contends AI has terrible marketing: the industry fails to show normal people concrete life improvements and instead frames humans as replaceable utilitarian inputs. Sharp ties the backlash to wealth piling up at a few big tech firms, and Thompson draws a parallel to the shameless Amazon HQ2 bidding war, arguing corporations trained communities to expect extraction rather than partnership, and will now be forced into a more transactional, pay-for-privilege model going forward. He cites Loudoun County, Virginia as the counter-example — lower property taxes and power prices after three decades of data center clustering — but notes that payoff took thirty years, while near-term costs like rising electricity prices are what voters react to now.

03 Bottleneck losers and winners

With US construction constrained, Thompson sees three adaptations: data centers moving abroad (he cites a reported $15 billion Google data center project in Malaysia), continued buildout in space, and intensified reliance on existing hyperscaler footprints. He argues hyperscalers are structurally advantaged because they already hold land/power/community relationships, generate enormous cash from existing businesses (he cites Amazon’s roughly $200 billion capex, funded by a massive cash-generating base) enabling cheaper borrowing, and can issue equity backed by profitable existing businesses — unlike Anthropic or OpenAI, whose equity value depends entirely on whether those companies ultimately succeed. He floats that Google or Amazon could end up effectively owning a large stake in Anthropic over time. On NVIDIA, Thompson makes a counterintuitive point: scarcity of power/data-center capacity raises the value of the most efficient “tokens per watt” machine, strengthening NVIDIA’s relative position even though constrained buildout hurts its chip sales in the short term, but a future of abundant compute would erode its margins as customers turn to custom ASICs — which is partly why NVIDIA courted China, a market not power-constrained. He adds that if US buildout stays bottlenecked, China’s eventual chip progress plus larger domestic buildout capacity could let it accumulate far more compute than the US over time, similar to manufacturing dynamics — though Sharp, who hosts Sharp China, says he has never been less concerned about the US-China competition.

04 Space data centers and CapEx logic

Revisiting an earlier Stratechery piece, Thompson describes space-based compute not as giant orbital warehouses but individual server racks (roughly the size of a Starlink satellite) with large radiators for cooling, enabled by existing Starlink laser-communication infrastructure and suited to latency-insensitive inference workloads. He likens SpaceX’s logic to shipping-industry economics, where firms operate at accounting losses but continue because marginal costs (fuel, crew, fees) are covered even though depreciation isn’t — meaning SpaceX can treat Starship launch capacity as near-free since the capability already exists and needs utilization, making ventures like cellular service, and potentially space data centers, sensible from an asset-utilization standpoint even if not from a from-scratch cost calculation. He separately notes that if the AI capex bubble eventually bursts, surplus power infrastructure built along the way could deliver long-term economic value independent of the AI buildout itself, given decades of US “power poverty” since the oil crisis era.

05 Google’s Facebook parallel and Reddit’s decline

Thompson structures his reassessment of Alphabet — which, as Sharp read out from a news report, fell more than 13%, erasing over $500 billion in market value after search and ad sales grew at their slowest pace in five quarters — around a direct analogy to Meta’s Stories and Reels: a loved new format initially monetizes poorly (ads remain in the old surface) before the company builds measurement tools and fully monetizes it. He argues Google’s AI Mode is following the same build-first-monetize-later sequence, and sees it as legitimate self-disruption, describing it as the “Google Capital Company” thesis — Google using search as a cash-generating base, akin to Berkshire’s See’s Candies funding railroads, to fund infrastructure, chips, and AI investment as the long-term business. He flags Thomas Kurian’s new spot on the earnings call, which he calls a promotion, as evidence of Google prioritizing capital/infrastructure over short-term search optics. Separately, Reddit CEO Steve Huffman’s earnings-call comments — arguing that people still want Reddit but Google is no longer sending them there, in line with Reddit’s push to become a direct destination — are read by Thompson as an honest acknowledgment that Google-driven traffic is permanently declining; Reddit stock fell about 30%, and Thompson argues the company must shift toward logged-in users and deeper ad targeting since its core value (a small base of active posters) doesn’t depend on the lurking majority that Google used to funnel in.

06 The group-chat era of software

Prompted by a listener email, Thompson extends his “end of the internet” thesis into an optimistic framing he calls the group chat era: AI enables small-scale, highly tailored software (e.g., a vibe-coded app for a school lunch menu) that doesn’t scale to mass markets but restores welfare-enhancing inefficiency and status-based incentives once associated with small communities. He contrasts this with sclerotic, scale-optimized incumbents (app stores, Google Search) incapable of serving niche needs, and highlights Google’s new Play Store feature letting developers publish apps just for friends and family as an early infrastructure response, contrasting it with the rigmarole of Apple’s TestFlight. He closes with career advice that AI is best aimed at offloading one’s weaknesses rather than automating core strengths, and argues disruption periods like this create outsized opportunity for small, previously-impossible one-person businesses — citing Substack’s success in proving larger-than-expected markets for independent writers.
