  1. CoreWeave CEO Mike Intrator称公司2027年剩余产能超额认购10倍，2028年产能也超额认购数倍，全球运营超过53个数据中心。
  2. 高收益债变贵后，CoreWeave转向预付款和押金融资，已落实约90亿美元，并将发行利率约7%至7.5%的高投资级资产层债务，DDTL已偿还超过40亿美元。
  3. 公司当前在运基础设施1.7GW，年底增至1.85GW，下季度调整后营业利润率指引为16%至17%。
  4. 四年前装机、2027年到期的H100正以较原价高20%至50%的价格续约，Intrator认为这回应了算力资产寿命短于折旧期的质疑。
  5. Intrator取消了自己的10b5-1减持计划，但公司ATM计划仍保留，此前空头称上市以来内部人士减持达33亿美元。
  6. 《芯片战争》作者Chris Miller认为英伟达与华盛顿的关系已逆转，国会将同时调查英伟达和特朗普政府，限制对华AI芯片销售已成为两党共识。
  7. Miller指出走私芯片和东南亚托管对英伟达收入仅占几个百分点，并提示为中国客户服务的海外数据中心如甲骨文（客户包括字节跳动）可能成为下一个审查重点。
  8. 据The Information报道，Alice Huang家族围绕上海世纪利通自2023年以来促成至少75亿美元、约24万颗英伟达A和H系列芯片进口，该公司成立数月即获得优选云合作伙伴资格。
  9. D.A. Davidson的Gil Luria称英伟达市值已蒸发超过5000亿美元，基本计入违规认定和黄仁勋离任两种不利情景，最大风险是新加坡和台湾经销渠道被切断。

THE SIGNAL

  • Intrator ties the 2027 H100 re-leases (20-50% above original rates) to GPUs he says are already fully paid off, with DDTLs repaid by over $4B, so renewal revenue should carry little debt service (our read).- Intrator says CoreWeave trades in the emerging two-way compute market, which he calls necessary for price transparency; observable spot prices could back up GPU residual values and asset-level debt ratings (our read).- The 10b5-1 cancellation is conditional: Intrator says his plan stays inactive until the equity price changes materially, so founder selling could return in a rally (our read).- Intrator says builds will speed up or slow down depending on whether deals clear component cost, cost of capital and price; IG asset debt at 7-7.5% versus the 9.75% 2031 bonds at 81.7 shapes deployment pace (our read).

01 Demand and the overwhelming narrative

Intrator opens by directly addressing skepticism that AI infrastructure demand is cooling, asserting that demand for this kind of compute infrastructure is running far ahead of what the world is able to build, a point he says he has been making for years. He reiterates the company is 10x oversubscribed on the capacity it has left in 2027 and multiples oversubscribed on 2028 capacity. On U.S. political risk — anchors raise the idea that a “blue wave” narrative and a Texas governor-elect’s stance on data centers could slow buildout — Intrator says CoreWeave sees no impact on its ability to deliver U.S. data centers needed for its backlog, noting the Texas governor-elect’s position was that facilities already permitted and under construction would continue. He notes the company operates in excess of 53 data centers globally, describing a worldwide groundswell of demand beyond the U.S.

02 Financing shift: from bonds to prepayments

Anchors note CoreWeave’s public high-yield debt had traded near levels they describe as a danger zone, but Intrator says the company pivoted. He says CoreWeave is about to launch a new asset-level debt issuance already rated high investment grade, expected to price at 7-7.5%, which would lower the company’s weighted average cost of capital. He frames high-yield debt as just one corner of the company’s financing toolkit, saying that once it got expensive the company turned instead to prepayments and deposits, lining up what he calls $9 billion of them. He adds that delayed draw term loans (DDTLs) have already been paid down by more than $4 billion.

03 Margins, interest expense, and capital tools

Pressed on whether interest expense will keep outpacing adjusted operating income, Intrator says adjusted operating income is growing far faster than interest expense and, in his view, will cover the debt by a large amount; the company is guiding to an adjusted operating income margin of 16-17% next quarter. CoreWeave has 1.7 gigawatts of infrastructure in the ground now, growing to 1.85 gigawatts by year-end. Intrator says the company treats equity issuance, convertible notes, prepayments, and debt as interchangeable levers, choosing whichever is cheapest at the time — currently prepayments.

04 Pricing power and chip economics

Asked about the price increases an anchor cites, 25% in July and another 10% later in the quarter, Intrator says customers accept higher prices because the revenue they generate from the compute supports the higher cost, pointing to Anthropic’s disclosed financials as evidence, and saying this pricing trend is being seen across the industry. On aging hardware, he highlights that H100s installed four years ago and coming off contract in 2027 are being recontracted at a 20-50% premium versus original pricing, which he says should resolve the debate over whether compute assets outlast their depreciable life. On NVIDIA, Intrator calls it an extraordinary company with deep, multi-level integration between the two firms’ teams on technology, infrastructure and networking, declining to offer an opinion on Jensen Huang beyond noting a process is underway.

05 Build plans, bond buybacks, and insider selling

On whether 2028 capacity builds are slipping, Intrator insists plans are not delayed, saying CoreWeave deliberately lets infrastructure “season” closer to delivery before marketing it, to maximize price, margin and customer quality. Asked why CoreWeave doesn’t buy back its own bonds (trading around 81.7 on one series) given high yields, Intrator admits he has considered it, but says he can deploy capital internally at a higher rate of return than buying back debt, he says. Finally, addressing bear claims of $3.3 billion in insider selling since IPO ($2.4 billion this year), Intrator reveals he has canceled his own 10b5-1 trading plan, saying future sales from him would just be routine RSU-related tax sales; he notes the corporate ATM program remains in place and will be used opportunistically if capital is needed.

THE SIGNAL

  • Miller says NVIDIA’s ties to Washington, positive with the Trump administration but adversarial with Congress for 18 months, have flipped: Congress will now investigate both, so NVIDIA needs a big reset there.- Hosts expect rules blocking Chinese labs from renting compute in Malaysia, Singapore or the US, and possibly curbing US data vendors’ sales of training data to Chinese labs (our read: neocloud and data-vendor risk).- Miller asks whether the second-order effects are negative for China: tougher controls could cut chip smuggling through Southeast Asia and Chinese access to data centers there that train and deploy Chinese models.

01 The scandal’s trajectory

Miller says scandals like this tend to run long, with new details likely to surface as investigations deepen. He states the damage to NVIDIA is already clear, but so is damage to the Trump administration, which allegedly sat on the matter for roughly a year without disclosure — something he calls puzzling. The hosts press on why NVIDIA didn’t get ahead of the story, noting the company hired crisis-PR firm Heller, implying it anticipated public disclosure and could have controlled the narrative by self-reporting sooner. Allegations regarding individuals are treated throughout as unconfirmed reporting, not established fact.

02 Washington turns adversarial

Miller argues NVIDIA’s relationship with Washington has been cordial with the Trump administration but strained with Congress for roughly eighteen months, and that balance has now flipped, with Congress taking the lead in investigating both NVIDIA and the administration’s enforcement failures. He cites the Banks-Warren legislative alignment as evidence that restricting AI chip sales to China has become one of the few genuinely bipartisan causes in Washington. The hosts contrast this with NVIDIA’s standing on the West Coast, where it has distributed large capital commitments — citing figures like $100 billion, $5 billion, and $2 billion allocations to various labs and inference providers — buying goodwill that doesn’t translate in Washington, where, per the hosts, the company has accumulated many political enemies.

03 Why the revenue math doesn’t justify the risk

Miller and the hosts discuss how Chinese labs such as Baidu already use GPUs hosted in Southeast Asian data centers remotely for training and inference, alongside advanced chips physically in China. Miller notes this exposure — smuggled chips plus Southeast Asian hosting — represents only a couple percentage points of NVIDIA revenue, prompting the hosts to call the China/Southeast Asia strategy an unforced error given how much American demand alone could absorb at full price. They contrast NVIDIA’s posture with what they regard as Apple’s more restrained approach to the AI race, suggesting NVIDIA could have maintained trillion-dollar status by simply refocusing on the U.S. market.

04 Governments decide what’s strategic

Miller’s central argument is that NVIDIA uniquely resisted the premise, accepted by most chip companies, that governments would shape the industry’s future — instead telling Washington its chips weren’t strategically important. He calls Intel a more complicated case, while the hosts point to Google’s unsuccessful push into Chinese search and speculate that Google and Amazon avoid selling chips to China. Miller frames the alleged spying operation as effectively settling the debate, forcing NVIDIA to confront that both Washington and Beijing already treat its chips as strategically vital.

05 What’s next and what’s overlooked

Miller expects tougher enforcement controls on NVIDIA and pressure on the Trump administration to apply existing rules, with uncertainty over whether China actually gained valuable information from the incident. He flags that stalled H200 shipments in Hong Kong customs hint at internal Chinese ambivalence about deploying the chips, suggesting China’s system is not monolithic on AI urgency. Asked what’s being overlooked, Miller points to second-order effects on data centers outside China serving Chinese customers — naming Oracle, which has ByteDance as a major client — as the next area likely to draw scrutiny as the investigation continues.

THE SIGNAL

  • Luria says the $500B-plus drawdown already prices in both bad scenarios, wrongdoing findings and a Huang exit, after only a couple of trading days, and calls NVIDIA attractively valued beforehand; his base case is still very fast growth.- The main earnings risk is non-China Asia: Luria calls sales there, via resellers such as Singapore’s and Taiwan’s, a meaningful part of the business that findings could cut off if the compute served Chinese firms.- Century Litong both sold chips and placed some in its own data-center space, so the exposure covers compute-as-a-service as well as hardware (our read).- The distributor-inventory channel: Yang says that as export rules widened to China-specific chips, preferred cloud partners could still get allocations of stock already in distributor warehouses, so distributor inventory routing is a likely focus for investigators (our read).

01 The Huang-Huang connection

Jing Yang reports that Jensen Huang and Alice Huang (unrelated despite sharing a surname) have known each other far longer than publicly visible footage from 2024 and 2025 in Taipei suggested. The Information traces the relationship to the 2010s through Alice Huang’s husband, Kun Wei, then a Shanghai private-equity investor who was involved in a series of Chinese gaming-company acquisitions during the period when NVIDIA was still primarily a gaming-GPU supplier, before its AI pivot. Yang reports Alice Huang has reportedly been identified by the US government as a Chinese intelligence asset, though Yang says The Information could not establish how much Jensen Huang or the broader company understood about Alice Huang’s and Century Litong’s activities.

02 The $7.5 billion chip network

According to The Information’s reporting, Alice Huang, her sister, and her husband built a group of companies centered on Shanghai-based Century Litong, which has facilitated at least $7.5 billion in NVIDIA chip imports since 2023 — roughly 240,000 chips of the A- and H-generation lines, some reportedly subject to US export restrictions. Century Litong, despite appearing as an unremarkable AI-boom-era startup, reportedly earned NVIDIA’s “preferred cloud partner” credential — which grants preferential chip allocation and typically requires demonstrated training competence and revenue thresholds — within just months of its 2023 founding, a timeline Yang says The Information could not explain. Yang reports that this status let the company get allocations of chips already sitting in distributor warehouses in the short window as the US widened export controls in 2023, and that Alice Huang reportedly used her closeness to Jensen Huang — including shared birthday celebrations and photos — as a sales pitch to customers despite having no prior chip-industry experience.

03 Market reaction and regulatory stakes

Gil Luria of D.A. Davidson frames the story as introducing substantial new risk to NVIDIA on two fronts: potential findings of wrongdoing in China sales, and a possible Jensen Huang departure. He notes NVIDIA shares have lost more than $500 billion in value following disclosure of a board investigation into whether Huang was targeted by a foreign intelligence operation, calling the drop “appropriate” given the newly introduced bad-outcome scenarios now priced in. Luria’s base case is that NVIDIA did nothing wrong and Huang remains CEO, in which case the company continues growing rapidly; but he flags that Congressional testimony and investigations are coming regardless, especially given what he calls a Democratic Congress. His bigger worry is whether investigators find that NVIDIA knowingly sold chips or compute into channels ultimately serving Chinese buyers in ways that may have violated export rules — a finding that could force NVIDIA to curtail sales through Singapore and Taiwan resellers across broad parts of Asia.

04 Succession scenario

Luria argues a Huang exit without accompanying regulatory wrongdoing findings would be the less damaging outcome, given NVIDIA’s structural strength, and urges the board to immediately vet internal and external succession candidates rather than wait. He floats Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan as a hypothetical choice, praising his turnaround of Intel and his ecosystem and Asia relationships, while cautioning this is purely his own speculation and not based on knowledge of NVIDIA’s actual deliberations. He adds that NVIDIA’s relationships with hyperscaler customers like Microsoft, Amazon, and Google, plus suppliers like TSMC and ASML, should outlast any single leadership change provided succession is handled cleanly.
