THE SIGNAL

  • Su said demand exceeds supply and declined to size unconstrained upside, so the over-80% 2027 figure is supply-capped (our read).- Server CPU is a second AI growth leg: Su dates real agentic workloads to the past three to six months, with customers asking monthly for more CPU over 12-18 months (our read: Venice upside).- Data-center revenue rising 50% sequentially costs only about one point of gross margin (56% to 55%), implying Helios/MI455 is less dilutive than feared (our read); Su sees margins holding in 2027 plus opex leverage.

01 Results and demand acceleration

Su says AMD grew over 40% year over year in a third quarter that beat its own expectations, with data center extremely strong, and says the company is seeing even stronger demand looking forward. She guides data-center revenue to grow 50% sequentially into Q4, and frames this moment as an early stage of an adoption cycle for AI and what she terms super intelligence, requiring CPUs, GPUs and accelerators together — AMD’s specialty, in her telling.

02 Raising the long-term target

Asked by Jim Cramer to revise the 60% data-center compound annual growth rate AMD gave at its November 2025 Analyst Day, Su describes very strong data-center growth this year and next, and says AMD’s business will grow over 80% next year. She attributes the upgrade to accelerating AI demand, customers validating AMD’s newest products, the Helios platform launch in Q3, and a surge in agentic workloads over recent weeks, citing Meta’s Muse product adoption as a concrete signal. She says the majority of 2027 volume is already locked in via contracts with both customers and supply-chain partners.

03 Supply chain as the real constraint

Pressed by Faber on where the tightest bottleneck lies, Su declines to name a single constraint, instead describing an end-to-end chain — wafers, substrates, advanced packaging, memory, data centers, land, power and shell — that must all come together, and says AMD is working each link rather than facing one chokepoint. She says AMD plans its roadmap and data-center build-out three to five years in advance with partners including OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle, and notes AMD has already landed first racks of its newest systems at all of these customers.

04 Competitive position vs Intel and NVIDIA

Su says she favors AMD’s competitive position against both rivals. On CPUs, she points to the new Venice server chip, just shipping in Q3, describing it as the best CPU in the industry based on customer demand for performance and cost efficiency. On AI accelerators, she highlights Helios and the MI455 platform, claiming leadership in memory bandwidth, and argues AI itself is accelerating customer adoption of new hardware. Cramer calls Intel a loser in both PCs and data-center racks; Su answers only that she likes AMD’s competitive position.

05 Election risk and margins

On the midterm election and local opposition to data-center siting, Su argues the industry needs clearer dialogue with communities about AI’s value proposition, but maintains AMD has strong diligence and visibility into where its forecasted capacity is being built, and reaffirms the $1 trillion data-center TAM by 2030 is unchanged. She does not directly address Cramer’s follow-up on whether debt-funded builders like Hut 8, CoreWeave and IREN can still build, instead reiterating that AMD partners with large, well-resourced frontier labs. On margins, Su notes gross margin exited Q3 at 56% and is guided to 55% in Q4 as the AI mix grows faster than CPUs, and expects margins to hold steady into 2027 aided by operating-expense leverage.

THE SIGNAL

  • Casado says model charts in pitches show a frontier model’s commercial life is just 3-6 months, so one slipped release could cost a lab the 80%+ dollar-weighted share leaders have held for three years.- Casado says coding may be near saturation, where users have automated ~95% and need no more tokens, but that doesn’t mean cheaper models: competing startups will always pay 10x more for the smartest one.- Casado says Snowflake-type data infrastructure growing 30-40% faces no real AI disruption, because operating it takes a long tail of features and performance tuning, and more vibe-coded software lifts infra demand.- Casado says AI cluster networking has new entrants for the first time since Arista, with changes in build materials and a shift toward optical (our read: watch optical suppliers).

01 Investor mindsets and moats

Casado outlines four investing archetypes he says are routinely conflated: financial investors who proxy business health through SaaS metrics and market size; productive investors (his own camp) who diligence technical approach, adjacent markets, and value-capture speed; frontier investors, a 1960s-70s-style bet purely on founders and talent (he cites Chris Dixon’s bets on Nootropics, Soylent, Oculus, and Coinbase); and speculative investors who trade assets without caring about underlying business metrics, common in crypto and secondaries. He warns that without clarity on which archetype you are, you can end up justifying any deal, or none, based purely on personal bias.

On network effects, Casado says the term is used inconsistently — some mean Metcalfe’s-law super-linear growth (true network effects, historically rare, exemplified by telcos, LinkedIn, and GitHub), others mean mere product improvement with scale, and others mean self-reinforcing “flywheels” without knowing if they’re actually super-linear. He argues the real question is whether a moat’s growth is super-linear, linear, or sub-linear relative to competitor spending — sub-linear moats let challengers catch up regardless of brand or mechanism. He adds a stronger claim: in growing markets, defensibility debates are largely moot because companies find new white space rather than fight head-on; this, he says, is why years of investor skepticism toward models and Neo Clouds as undefensible missed how much value they’d still accrue.

02 Infrastructure under maximum disruption

Casado frames infrastructure investing around five classic layers — compute, network, storage, databases, and (newly) models, which he says behave more like ASICs than software given their capital intensity and competitive pressure. He says agentic software is forcing a rebuild of sleepy categories: OLTP databases are being reinvented because agents don’t need human-friendly query languages; networking (his PhD field) is seeing its first new entrants since Arista due to massive, rigid-performance AI clusters; and compute abstractions (containers vs. VMs vs. durable workflows like Temporal) are being rethought for agent workloads.

03 Frontier model economics

Casado says current data shows frontier models get the vast majority of dollar-weighted market share because enterprise AI penetration remains near 1% of budget, leaving enormous room to grow before any defensibility debate matters. The open question, per Casado, is what happens if use-case saturation hits — he says nobody knows whether value would crash back into open-source models, an outcome without precedent in software’s usual long-tail retention. He also distinguishes two separate claims often conflated: having enough tokens because tasks are saturated doesn’t imply switching to cheaper models, because competitive dynamics mean startups will always pay more for the smartest available model rather than risk falling behind rivals. He also discusses his personal use of an AI assistant bot for automation and an ambition to have it “embody itself” in his home, and cites World Labs’ Atlas model as evidence that generated, navigable 3D scenes are near.

04 Capital, debt, and market structure

Casado describes profitable late-stage companies growing 40-60% with hundreds of millions in ARR that “pale in comparison” to AI opportunities for capital allocation, creating a market-structure gap since they’re subscale for IPOs; he notes SPACs are resurfacing as a workaround. He distinguishes equity capital, which he says remains abundant, from debt capital, which is scarce and increasingly reserved for investment-grade issuers — meaning non-IG, debt-reliant companies may need to demand customer prepayment. On infrastructure categories, he defines Neo Clouds as physical GPU/token providers and Neo Labs as model-architecture builders whose business model is effectively to solve intelligence first, noting the binding constraint across both is power and chip supply, not capital — leading 80% of the market toward hyperscale-type providers and 20% toward niche players, including a GPU marketplace he’s seen sourcing capacity from farm chicken coops. He also notes regulatory pressure, with roughly 500 proposed bills against US data centers, pushing some compute offshore or into orbit.

05 M&A lessons from Cursor, OpenRouter, World Labs

Casado, who sold his own networking company to VMware at what he believes was then a record revenue multiple for enterprise software and later worked on the Tabular/Databricks deal, says strategic M&A hinges on personal alignment between buyer and seller leadership and on the acquired company proving an inductive growth story into a much larger market. On Cursor, he says the enormous cost of building frontier models forced a choice between a fresh raise or a strategic deal, and SpaceX-scale compute access combined with Cursor’s user and data lead made the acquisition logical. He contrasts this with OpenRouter, which he says suffered from leaks that undermined the negotiation, versus World Labs, which stayed confidential among a handful of people and never leaked — his overall lesson being that treating these as adversarial auctions with sophisticated counterparties like strategic acquirers is a mistake.

06 Capital as the new scaling mechanism

Casado’s closing framing is that AI has turned engineering, historically bottlenecked per Fred Brooks’s “mythical man month,” into a capital-allocation problem, letting a small team deploy a billion dollars productively rather than detonate under its weight. He adds commentary on evolving investor roles, warning that late-stage/PE investors increasingly sit on early-stage boards without matching operating intuition, and reiterates his view that private markets are efficient and have no fixed TAM — more capital deployed, in his view, creates more investable value rather than diluting existing opportunity.

01 Origin of the AI Revolution call

Ives traces his bullish call to late 2022 conversations with developers and private companies, where he concluded the underlying technology (NVIDIA’s chips, hyperscaler infrastructure) was ready to scale. The trigger, he says, was Microsoft’s roughly $10 billion OpenAI investment in January 2023 — a stake he says is now worth many multiples of that — which he read as evidence a major hyperscaler had fully committed, implying Amazon and Google would be forced to follow. He contrasts this with the largely negative contemporaneous reaction, where analysts called the investment reckless.

02 Field research as edge

Ives credits his research edge to decades of channel checks, citing 3.5 million air miles accumulated over his career, including a recent trip to Korea to assess memory players and a trip to the Midwest to view five data centers under construction in Nebraska and Iowa. He frames this as a corrective to desk-bound bears who, in his telling, watch AI only in spreadsheets from high-rise New York offices and rarely travel to the field. He also cites calls he made early — AMD when it was a sub-$10 to $20 stock, NVIDIA and Palantir in AI, the “SaaS apocalypse” narrative that he argued was overstated, and cybersecurity names like CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks and Zscaler that he says bears wrongly expected to be disrupted by AI.

03 Positioning across the stack

Ives lays out holdings across layers: on chips/memory, NVIDIA, AMD, TSMC, SK Hynix and Micron; among hyperscalers, broad exposure while flagging caution on Oracle given its debt load as it builds out data centers; and in software, Palantir, Snowflake, MongoDB, plus cybersecurity names where he expects budgets to potentially double over the next few years as agent-driven workloads expand. He also flags unconventional AI beneficiaries — Caterpillar, Lockheed Martin, and Dell — as quasi-AI plays tied to infrastructure and defense tech. On the consumer side, he frames Meta as benefiting from monetizing roughly 3.5 billion users and Apple as a toll collector positioned to capture AI access for about 20% of the world through its devices, pushing back on bearish calls that the stock should trade under $200 due to AI underinvestment.

04 Bubble comparison and macro skepticism

Ives directly rebuts dot-com bubble comparisons, noting the average tech company then traded at 25-30x revenues funded by venture capital and PowerPoint-deck business models, versus today’s roughly 23x earnings multiples funded by trillion-dollar big-tech balance sheets generating $300-400 billion in annual free cash flow. He argues investors scarred by the dot-com bubble and 2008 have repeatedly missed generational opportunities by over-indexing on macro and geopolitical risk, invoking a Buffett-style framework of focusing on fundamentals over macro noise. He also predicts AI will create more jobs than it displaces over the next five years, while acknowledging tech companies have partly created their own regulatory backlash through dystopian messaging about job cuts.

05 Agentic AI and the Anthropic IPO

On agentic workflows, Ives says winners and losers will be defined by which industries — retail, financials, healthcare, government — successfully embrace AI-driven agents versus those disrupted by them, citing drug discovery in pharmaceuticals as an example of embrace. On Anthropic, he expects it to evolve from a frontier-model provider into a broader enterprise monetization platform resembling Palantir’s model, and frames the eventual public listings of Anthropic, OpenAI and SpaceX as healthy for market transparency and efficiency, despite valuation sensitivity in current private-market pricing.

06 Yorkville Ives build-out

Ives describes his move to Yorkville Ives (founded in July) as building a merchant/investment bank spanning research, sales, advisory, and a hedge fund, aimed at underserved companies and investors amid industry “juniorization.” He also references his roles chairing the Ives Ultra Fund (described as the first public vehicle investing in private AI tech companies) and the AI 30 he and his team track, framing the current environment as a multiyear tech bull market warranting an entrepreneurial, opportunistic posture.

01 HBM’s unlikely payoff

Jon frames HBM as “the ultimate case” of building something expensive and advanced and waiting to see if anyone buys it — in HBM’s case, for about eight years before it became a major profit driver for a handful of memory makers (implicitly SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron). Tim, citing an unnamed SK Hynix executive, calls HBM simply “the solution we have is the solution we’re using” — framed as the best available option rather than an ideal one. Tim connects this to a broader architectural shift in computing: where performance used to be about clock speed and cores, today it’s almost entirely about memory access speed, and Jon ties the shift to Intel’s failed Itanium bet that inadvertently locked it into x86.

02 Why compute-in-memory failed

Jon recaps Samsung’s earlier attempt to move logic processors directly into memory base dies rather than moving data out to the GPU — an approach meant to sidestep the Von Neumann bottleneck. He says the concept was sound but execution failed because it required extensive custom software work, and Samsung underestimated how entrenched CUDA was as the dominant programming framework, so the approach was never widely adopted.

03 HBF’s technical premise and limits

HBF’s core idea, per Jon, is packaging flash near the GPU core the way HBM packages DRAM, trading some bandwidth efficiency for vastly greater storage capacity at the limited “shoreline” space around chip edges. The tradeoff is flash’s endurance problem: cells degrade after tens of thousands of cycles, far short of DRAM’s durability. Jon reports SanDisk engineers believe they can tune the flash recipe — thickening cell walls and sacrificing long-term data retention (flash is historically built to retain data ten years, competing with hard drives) — to prioritize more read/write cycles instead, since AI workloads don’t need decade-long retention.

04 Where HBF actually fits

Tim proposes a hybrid architecture mixing some HBM and some HBF slots around a GPU, but Jon counters that implementing this requires deep, model-specific software customization to balance the two memory types, making it a bespoke rather than general solution. Tim argues HBF is a better match for ASIC-based inference than GPU-based training, since ASIC designers know their specific models in advance and only need to read (not constantly write) weights or KV cache — unlike GPUs, which are more generalist. He also floats edge AI as a strong use case, where models are hardwired or updated infrequently (e.g., annually in factory robots or cameras), reducing exposure to flash’s endurance limits. Jon pushes further on the KV cache angle: he says today’s models tend to top out around a million tokens of context before they need compacting — far short of meaningful “life experience.” He argues DRAM-based HBM can’t scale context windows further since DRAM is already capacity-constrained, making flash-based KV cache storage the plausible path to billion-token context windows, though this trades against flash’s endurance weakness since KV cache involves heavy access cycles.

05 Supply chain winners

Tim notes HBM’s rise strongly benefited Taiwan, since HBM is fundamentally a packaging technology that boosted TSMC and related players. He questions whether HBF will replicate that: based on conversations at SEMICON Taiwan, he reports strong interest from metrology and test equipment companies developing tools specifically for HBF, with some already verifying solutions for Micron ahead of fab deployment next year. Taiwanese firms may still benefit via microcontrollers that manage HBF, but Tim suggests the test/inspection segment — a long-standing but newly reinvigorated industry driven by CoWoS, AI, HBM and HBF demand — may be the bigger beneficiary this cycle.

01 Unit economics versus Big Tech IPOs

Evans compares Anthropic’s filing to Meta’s and Google’s IPO-era financials as a deliberately unfair but relevant benchmark. Anthropic’s quarterly revenue jumped from about $1.4 billion in the September quarter last year to nearly $17 billion this year, confirming earlier leaked annualized figures with real reported numbers. Gross margin sits at 85%, close to Meta’s 78% and just under Google’s 87-88% at their respective IPOs. But R&D — the cost of building the next frontier model — consumes a far larger share of revenue than Google’s 9.5% and Meta’s 10.5%, pushing combined cost-of-sale-plus-R&D to about 75% of revenue. Evans notes models released in the past six months produced 80% of Q3 revenue, comparing the dynamic to a mining or pharma company where the asset depreciates almost as fast as it’s built.

02 Training costs and the leverage problem

Training spend rose from $4.5 billion in the second half of last year to $9.7 billion in the first half of this year, with fixed costs rising faster than marginal costs — an inversion Evans says runs counter to how a maturing business should behave. Separately, Anthropic has made non-cancellable future compute purchase commitments that Evans estimates at over a trillion dollars depending on how they’re totaled. He argues the cancellation question is largely beside the point: if the business collapses, Anthropic won’t need the capacity regardless of contract terms, so these commitments function like leverage — fine if growth continues, catastrophic if it doesn’t. About a quarter of revenue goes to distribution partners like Amazon, a cost Evans says functions as a marginal cost/rev-share despite the circular supplier-partner-shareholder-competitor relationship these companies have with Anthropic.

03 Why nobody knows pricing power

Evans’s central argument is that current profitability — thin as it is — occurs in an environment of severe supply shortage where Anthropic can price up to customer ROI, yet it’s barely breaking even. Meanwhile cost-per-result is falling roughly 10x per year from compute efficiency gains, even as token usage could grow 10x-100x as agentic use scales beyond today’s narrow base (a billion casual users, a few million agentic coders — nowhere near Instagram-scale adoption). Because models are largely commodities — fungible systems where buyers often mix two or three vendors — Anthropic currently has shortage-based pricing power like an oil company, not brand-based pricing power. Whether recursive self-improvement produces a winner-take-all dynamic, Evans says, remains unknown.

04 Agents, chat, and the context gap

Toni Cowan-Brown raises whether chat or agentic usage is the better business, since agents consume far more tokens but are costlier to build. Evans notes Mark Zuckerberg’s claim of product-market fit for Meta’s Muse agent is undercut by the actual figures: 10 million app setups, roughly 2-3 million people actually using it, a quarter making e-commerce purchases averaging $200, and Zuckerberg’s own admission that scaling requires compute costs to fall at least 10x. Evans’s deeper point is structural: Meta, Google, Apple and Amazon can passively observe user context — email, messages, browsing — to proactively suggest agent actions, while Anthropic and OpenAI only see what happens inside the agent session itself, with essentially nothing else about the user to draw on — making it unclear whether agents will be vertical, horizontal, or even controlled by the model builders at all. He likens the uncertainty to open platform questions from the 1997 internet and 2009 mobile eras.

05 Credit markets enter the picture

Evans identifies two structural shifts this year: genuine product-market fit for AI in software development, and hyperscaler capex now outgrowing free cash flow, forcing recourse to capital markets — an estimated $500 billion to $1 trillion in AI-related debt, plus over a trillion each in leases and future purchase commitments. This makes credit investors newly relevant, since they care only about whether cash flows (e.g., Google’s ad business) can service debt, not whether AI itself succeeds — creating a near catch-22 where even an AI failure might leave ad-supported giants able to pay their debts, while Neo Clouds or Oracle have no such cushion. He dismisses Nvidia’s argument that chips are safe collateral because they’re resellable, arguing that if a Neo Cloud fails from insufficient usage, any other buyer of those chips would face the same lack of demand.
