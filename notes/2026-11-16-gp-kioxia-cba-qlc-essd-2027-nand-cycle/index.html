Overview

The expert discussed Kioxia’s NAND architectures, competition among flash manufacturers, enterprise SSD adoption, pricing, and capacity trends. The expert has experience across the NAND supply chain and said enterprise and data-center applications are becoming increasingly important, particularly as customers consider QLC for high-capacity storage.

Key Insights

CBA separates the NAND array and control circuitry onto different wafers. The expert sees it as a meaningful performance and density improvement, with cost benefits becoming clearer after yields stabilize. The gains from BiCS8 over BiCS6 are expected to exceed those from BiCS10 over BiCS8. Higher layer counts increase manufacturing complexity and equipment costs, while potentially delaying yield improvement. QLC is attractive for cost-sensitive, read-heavy enterprise workloads, but its lower write endurance limits use in write-intensive and embedded applications. CBA does not, in the expert’s view, eliminate QLC’s endurance constraint. Kioxia’s advantages include CBA, available production capacity, and existing QLC deployments at some large cloud customers. The expert also noted that Kioxia has less server-sales experience than some rivals. The expert expects NAND supply to remain tight rather than become substantially oversupplied, as some manufacturers prioritize DRAM and HBM. Pricing forecasts and market-share estimates are the expert’s expectations, not confirmed outcomes.

For BiCS8 and BiCS10, Kioxia introduced CBA, a wafer-bonding architecture in which the peripheral circuitry and memory array are manufactured separately and then hybrid-bonded. This architecture was not used in the previous generation, BiCS6. Could you analyze the advantages and disadvantages of introducing it?

MS: We can understand it this way. As you just mentioned, CBA is what we call wafer-bonding technology. It differs from traditional 3D NAND, where the circuitry and memory array are built on the same wafer. We started working with Kioxia as early as BiCS5. At that time, it used CNA architecture, where the entire CMOS control circuitry was laid out across the wafer. This occupied a relatively large area, and taking up more area increased costs because every inch of a wafer is valuable. A lot of space was therefore wasted. As the process matured, it evolved into BiCS6, which used CON architecture. This made a small adjustment by placing the CMOS directly underneath the array, meaning that the CMOS circuitry was positioned directly below the memory array, reducing both volume and area. The array was then bonded to the CMOS underneath through processes such as high-temperature baking.

By BiCS8, it had moved to the CBA architecture, which is the bonding technology you just asked about. Previously, the memory and CMOS were built on a single wafer. Now, they are manufactured independently, with the CMOS on one wafer and the NAND memory dies on another wafer, and the two wafers are bonded face-to-face to form the CBA architecture. The main advantage is a relatively substantial improvement in performance, while storage density can also be increased accordingly. Those are its key advantages.

As you know, the industry is now moving from TLC toward QLC. Enterprise SSDs are currently one of the largest application areas for NAND flash. AC: We also discussed TLC and QLC. In the high-intensity usage scenarios of AI data centers, what are their respective P/E cycles, meaning their write endurance?

MS: TLC can achieve 3,000 cycles, while QLC can achieve only 1,000 cycles. If you configure TLC as PSLC, it can reach 30,000 cycles, a tenfold increase. That is the advantage of TLC. Once its usable capacity is reduced, its write endurance can increase exponentially. TLC has a significant advantage when configured as SLC, while QLC cannot do that. TLC is already close to what we call the optimal balance between cost and efficiency. Moving further upward crosses the boundary. Costs decline, but write endurance decreases rapidly. Although a cell originally stores three bits and QLC adds one additional bit to make four bits, which might theoretically suggest a linear increase, the actual result is an exponential decline in write endurance. There is no way around it. Lower costs are achieved by sacrificing write endurance. That is the logic.

That is why QLC is used almost entirely in enterprise SSDs or consumer SSDs and is rarely used in embedded products. Embedded products cannot tolerate such low write endurance. They need to last ten to twenty years, or at least ten to fifteen years. QLC cannot support that. Why are enterprise SSD users less concerned about service life? The core reason is that after reducing the initial capital expenditure, they can simply remove and replace the SSD later. How would you replace an embedded eMMC or UFS device, or an embedded motherboard inside a vehicle? You cannot. Therefore, embedded devices must have higher write endurance. That is the logic. We believe TLC will certainly remain the mainstream technology in embedded products over the long term.
