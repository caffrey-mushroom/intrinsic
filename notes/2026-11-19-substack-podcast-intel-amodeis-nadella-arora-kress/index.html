01 The opening-bell mission speech

Dario Amodei’s speech frames Anthropic’s founding mission around using AI (Claude) to accelerate biology and medicine. He says AI could help compress a century of scientific and medical progress into a single decade, while cautioning these are only early results and that experimental and clinical processes still take time. He enumerates risks including misuse by individuals or nation-states, economic disruption, and loss of control of AI systems, framing these as avoidable rather than inevitable. He commits Anthropic to pacing training and deployment for as long as safety requires, independent of business or political pressure, and frames this as part of a race-to-the-top strategy meant to lift safety standards industry-wide regardless of which company wins commercially.

02 Public benefit corporation structure

In the interview with Sorkin, Daniela Amodei explains Anthropic’s status as, per Sorkin, the largest public benefit corporation to go public, meaning it is legally required to balance shareholder interests against public benefit — a structure she says the founders deliberately chose because it matched their original intent. Dario reflects that even having understood scaling laws early, seeing the technology’s consequences play out in the real world was something else entirely, referring to the company’s rapid rise to a valuation Sorkin notes starts in the trillions.

03 Pacing as strategy, not just safety

Pressed on how to reconcile growth with safety-driven pacing, Dario frames it as inherent to the technology rather than optional, arguing every company in the space faces this tension whether explicitly or not. He contends pacing — sacrificing some speed for safety — will ultimately benefit investors too, since the company that earns enterprise trust through safety will perform best long-term; he describes seeking synergies between safety and commercial success. Daniela adds that despite the IPO’s magnitude, Anthropic’s day-to-day focus remains unchanged, describing the company as essentially the same one it was the day before, just at a notable milestone.

04 Competition, pricing, and compute spend

On competitive pressure from OpenAI’s cheaper tokens, open-weight models, and Microsoft offering overlapping features, Dario argues returns accumulate disproportionately to frontier capability (chatbots → coding → biology/robotics), making competition about quality rather than price at the top end — while frontier competence also enables cheaper, efficient models like Haiku 5.5 to undercut rivals. On the roughly $900 billion in compute commitments Sorkin cites, Dario says these span many years and that Anthropic intentionally forecasts conservatively, supplementing with spot-market purchases, justified by historically strong returns on compute-driven capability gains. Asked about reaching investment-grade status to access debt markets given a potential $250 billion combined cash-and-IPO-proceeds position (per Sorkin), Dario says that’s a future goal but offers no timeline.

05 Partners, risk, and national security

Daniela Amodei emphasizes Anthropic’s multi-vendor compute strategy — partnerships with NVIDIA, Amazon, Google, and Broadcom — as a strategic advantage, while the Amodeis decline to comment on whether Jensen Huang should return amid his dispute with Anthropic over China chip policy, noting partners can disagree on policy while remaining collaborators. On SpaceX, Dario neither confirms nor denies speculation Anthropic might scale back SpaceX compute use when contracts renew, saying only that the compute has met a high bar. Daniela says Anthropic has had a strong working relationship with the U.S. national security apparatus since founding and expects that to continue, without detailing the Pentagon relationship. On alignment and liability risk — including Sorkin’s reference to Satya Nadella’s skepticism that alignment scales automatically — Daniela stresses that enterprise customer relationships have taught Anthropic about real-world risk factors, and reiterates that safety has defined Anthropic’s identity since its founding.

01 Outages and the cyber threat landscape

Nadella addresses what Cramer called a “confluence” of recent outages (United, Schwab, Chase, Apple services), attributing part of it to AI-accelerated bug discovery that forces rapid patching, which in turn destabilizes systems running on older infrastructure. Separately, he warns that AI has lowered the floor for nation-scale cyberattacks—bad actors can now trigger persistent, automated attacks with minimal effort by sending a simple remote message to an automated attack system that keeps attacking on its own. On the Outlook outage specifically, he says it stemmed from a latent bug surfaced only by a specific, rare traffic pattern, and that Microsoft is publishing an incident response and hardening its architecture to build in bulkheads against global outages going forward, given its self-described responsibility as “dial tone” infrastructure for the world.

02 Human agency and AI trust architecture

Nadella frames AI product design around preserving human dignity and agency rather than ceding control to models, invoking the Pope’s encyclical as validating his view that technology without human-centered design will lose societal permission. He cites software engineering as proof: coding agents didn’t eliminate the profession but raised its level of abstraction. On alignment, he states plainly that AI capability is scaling exponentially while alignment and mechanistic interpretability lag far behind, which is why Microsoft is building a trust system kept separate from the intelligence system—treating capable models like an insider-risk problem. This architecture underlies Copilot, which he says offers multiple form factors (chat, co-work, autopilot) and model choice (OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft’s own) with authority deliberately separated from intelligence.

03 Agentic disruption of enterprise software and work

Nadella predicts a shift from human-seat software consumption to agent-driven, usage-based consumption, citing his own workflow where AI agents read his OneNote meeting notes and interact with CRM systems (Salesforce, Dynamics) more than he ever did manually. He says these “headless” CRM systems will become essential back-office infrastructure, pushing business models from per-seat pricing toward usage-based models—already underway in Dynamics. On jobs, he argues companies must reconfigure team structures around “token budgets” similar to AI-native startups, where founders and employees each have a compute/token allocation that scales—calling this transition necessary but not linear or instantaneous.

04 Azure, capacity strategy, and capex discipline

Nadella says Azure has crossed $100 billion, framing the growth through a total-addressable-market lens: markets expand rather than remaining zero-sum, as seen with Teams replacing a narrower server product (Lync) and becoming a subscription embedded across Office. He says GitHub and coding agents exemplify this pattern and that Azure must be rebuilt for an “agent era” rather than the prior “human web era.” On capital discipline, Nadella credits CFO Amy Hood with a strategy of investing in long-lead infrastructure (land, power, “cold shells”) while separately matching near-term demand through a build-lease-rent hierarchy, including using neo-clouds when supply is short (Cramer says they charge about 30% more), calling single-customer concentration “not a business.”

05 Data centers, local communities, and the AI investment thesis

Nadella points to Quincy, Washington—a twenty-year-old Microsoft data center site—as evidence that data centers create durable local benefits: lower property taxes, new schools, and wages roughly double the prior community average, pushing back on the idea that such jobs are transitory. He frames the overall AI buildout as analogous to railroad and fiber infrastructure booms, warning of possible overbuild while expressing strong conviction in resulting broad GDP growth, preferring to talk about a “frontier economy” rather than just frontier models. On NVIDIA governance, Nadella calls Jensen Huang a singular founder and praises NVIDIA’s team but defers board decisions about Huang’s status to NVIDIA’s board. Asked for investment advice on AI IPOs like Anthropic or OpenAI, Nadella tells individual investors who believe AI will generate broad economic growth that they can go long on it, while deferring stock-picking specifics to professional judgment.

01 Quarter and platform momentum

Arora tells Cramer the company’s quarter reflected what he calls a “mythos moment” for cybersecurity reached roughly six months ago, now intensified by recent outages and AI-assisted attacks that have pushed customers away from a scattershot, multi-vendor approach toward platform consolidation. He says firewall (hardware and software), SASE, AI security, and automated SIEM/AI SOC businesses are all performing exceptionally, with observability up 4x by his account (he did not give the comparison period), and describes the company’s main constraint as execution capacity rather than demand. He credits the platformization strategy — once met with skepticism — for the results, noting Palo Alto signed 170 platformization deals this quarter, more than the combined total of the first quarters of the prior two years.

02 CyberArk integration

Arora highlights the CyberArk acquisition, closed in February, as a model for execution: within roughly nine months the team was rebranded, integrated, and cross-sold across the Palo Alto platform. He says CyberArk’s margins have nearly doubled in the last six months and that company margins, which dipped after the deal closed, are back to what he calls record levels; he did not say which margin measure he meant.

03 The Applied Compute acquisition

Palo Alto announced an all-stock deal (valued at $4.5 billion per Cramer’s introduction) to acquire Applied Compute, a private company specializing in post-training and inference of open-weight models. Arora says Palo Alto sought a partner to train frontier-capability cyber models that remain open-weight — unlike closed models, which don’t expose their chain of thought for inspection — so the company can embed security training simultaneously with model training and intervene when models start to misbehave mid-process. He says Applied Compute’s research team came out of OpenAI and other frontier labs and had been providing elite model-training services to companies like Palo Alto before the acquisition conversation began. Asked why a cash-rich rival like Anthropic didn’t buy the company instead, Arora says Anthropic already has a successful closed model and isn’t targeting the same enterprise niche — companies wanting controllable, inspectable, securable models for use cases like proprietary data handling, logistics, or cancer research. He calls the addressable market huge, citing hundreds of billions of dollars in anticipated open-weight/open-source delivery as neoclouds build compute capacity to serve such models, with Applied Compute training models that neoclouds will serve.

04 Patch-pocalypse and agent risk

Arora explains recent high-profile outages and breaches as the product of two simultaneous dynamics: AI tools now let small groups attack many companies at once (he cites one group of attackers hitting 19 companies simultaneously), and vulnerability discovery itself has accelerated roughly sevenfold, overwhelming IT teams who rush to patch without adequate testing, causing collateral outages. He expects this dynamic to persist for 12 to 18 months with no quick fix. On AI agents, Arora predicts consumer deployment will outpace enterprise adoption because enterprises bear legal and board-level accountability for agent actions and will move cautiously, with Palo Alto and Applied Compute positioned to support enterprise agent security. On attacker accountability, he notes some groups are caught — citing a UK-based ransomware group called Scattered Spider that was arrested — but says many attacks are nation-state sponsored and difficult to trace across jurisdictions.

Kress said the upgraded 75% growth forecast for fiscal 2028 reflects both demand-side and supply-side improvement: customers’ hunger for compute to run agentic AI, paired with NVIDIA’s own progress coordinating suppliers on chips, advanced packaging and memory. She said that in recent weeks customers have signaled they may want more early in that year. On capital return, Kress said NVIDIA discussed adding roughly $150 billion of stock repurchases during the quarter and is fully committed to completing the repurchases — a pace Cramer said was more aggressive than he expected.

Addressing analyst concerns Cramer raised about a large guarantee figure weighing on cash flow, Kress stated NVIDIA’s guarantees have been carefully assembled to date at approximately $118 billion. The most significant component is a land, power and shell backstop tied to a Portsmouth, U.S. site designed for multiple buildings totaling about 4.5 gigawatts, which she described as solely for NVIDIA’s infrastructure. She said the company continues diligence on additional guarantees, evaluating counterparties’ progress on land/power/shell builds, capital readiness, and confirmed end-customer offtake before committing further.

03 Ecosystem investments and competitive position

Kress defended NVIDIA’s investments in AI-native companies and model builders as disciplined and internally debated, framing them as areas of future AI growth. She said the company’s top capital priority remains procuring supply and supporting its own engineering workforce. On competition, she rejected the idea that AMD’s GPU offerings or hyperscalers’ custom chips are pulling business from NVIDIA toward riskier “NeoCloud” customers, saying hyperscalers are actively pursuing more NVIDIA compute and praising the architecture’s durability and fungibility.

04 China exposure and leadership transition

On China, Kress said shipments there are about 10% of NVIDIA’s business, confined to gaming, automotive, and workstation products; the licensed H200 chip shipped to China last quarter, but NVIDIA has since stopped shipping H200 entirely. On the leadership change, she said employees view NVIDIA as their life’s work and that leadership is united behind moving forward, pushing back implicitly on reports of brain drain following Huang’s departure. She confirmed the board’s decision is final and reiterated the company’s focus is now on business execution for employees, customers and shareholders. She also confirmed continuity of the roughly $500 billion financing partnership with Goldman Sachs, BlackRock and Apollo to support ecosystem customers’ financing needs.

01 NVIDIA’s leadership question

Baker, describing 27 years in semiconductor investing and a personal relationship with Huang dating to 2000, frames NVIDIA’s leadership situation as a crisis requiring deliberate board process rather than a rushed CEO search. He notes CFO Colette Kress is capable of steady management in the interim, and states that he and Altimeter’s Brad Gerstner wrote a board letter advocating patience and, contingent on facts clearing Huang, his potential return as CEO. Baker repeatedly stresses this is his belief and that Huang has not been charged with anything — comparing the stakes to losing an irreplaceable star like Michael Jordan or Larry Bird. He says NVIDIA employees, shareholders and partners have reached out wanting Huang back, framing it as reflecting shareholder sentiment rather than established fact.

02 Huang, Taiwan, and the China risk

Baker asserts Huang is uniquely positioned with Taiwan’s chip supply chain — speaking the native dialect, lacking a formal TSMC contract yet allegedly having been asked by Morris Chang to lead the company — and claims Taiwan has displayed public signage urging his return. He calls Huang the most fervently pro-America CEO he knows, citing Huang’s immigrant background. These are Baker’s personal characterizations, not verified facts. He warns — speculatively — that U.S. policy shifts restricting NVIDIA’s China sales could backfire by forcing Chinese labs onto Huawei hardware, potentially accelerating its improvement and risking proliferation of Chinese AI tech to adversaries including Iran, Russia, and North Korea.

03 Open-source AI as overlooked demand

Baker argues a vibrant American open-source AI ecosystem matters because frontier models embed value systems, and he doesn’t want AI dominated by just two or three systems. He describes wanting to fine-tune an open-source model on formative works (Dune, Star Wars, Lord of the Rings, Shakespeare) for his own family, crediting Huang as the leading advocate for open-source within NVIDIA’s ecosystem. This open-source demand, he says, is a major undercounted driver of compute consumption given its lower margins requiring more volume.

04 Market disconnect on AI demand

Baker contends current equity selloffs reflect a “voting machine” phase disconnected from underlying fundamentals, citing strong commentary from Michael Dell, Cerebras’s Andrew Feldman, Astera’s CEO (post-earnings acceleration), and Fireworks AI’s Lynn Chow at the TomorrowX event. He suggests that once 2027 guidance emerges, even modest 5-10% growth rates would make AI stocks look cheap relative to current multiples, implying the market is overly fixated on decelerating growth rates (second derivatives) rather than absolute demand durability.

05 SpaceX’s Starship and orbital ambitions

Baker describes SpaceX as the most important national defense contractor for America, citing control of 85% of mass launched to orbit. He describes the recent Starship upper-stage catch — with reference to Walter Isaacson’s account of Elon Musk’s “Mechazilla” tower-arm concept once deemed impossible by engineers — as pivotal for enabling rapid reusability, orbital compute (he says Brad Gerstner told the summit earlier that day the first satellites launch in 2027), and Starlink direct-to-cell service. He says SpaceX engineers have already solved skeptics’ concerns about cooling and compute density in orbit, basing this on firsthand visits to Starbase. He also credits Starlink with humanitarian impact (connecting schools in Honduras and the Amazon).

THE SIGNAL

  • Arda’s Anduril traction: system access in January, first user in April, cutting an actuator virtual build from a few days to 90 minutes; now deployed across all Anduril product divisions, from drones to missiles, and the Fury CCA.

01 The hard takeoff critique

McGrew directly rebuts an argument he says he’s heard since 2018: that automating increasing fractions of a researcher’s time (10%, 50%, 90%, 99%) produces compounding speedups toward a singularity. He calls this argument straightforwardly wrong because researchers aren’t the limiting factor — experiments are. Drawing on his time as OpenAI’s chief research officer, including in 2024, he says the actual constraint was never headcount but GPU availability and whether compute was networked correctly. He estimates GPUs today deliver discoveries at perhaps 35% of theoretical optimum, and full automation might push that to 90% — a large one-time gain similar to the shift to reasoning models, but not a runaway loop, since gains from 90% to 99% are comparatively small.

02 Physical bottlenecks across domains

This logic extends to biology and robotics, McGrew argues: labs produce cheap intelligence (hypothesis generation), but wet labs and GPU layouts are the scarce complements. He questions whether frontier AI labs (OpenAI, Anthropic) have any comparative advantage running wet labs versus startups. Elad Gil pushes back somewhat, noting domains like protein folding where strong simulation has already reduced the need for physical experiments; McGrew responds that even perfect digital simulation only produces a one-time acceleration before the system is again constrained by physical limits — citing an anecdote where Ilya Sutskever, even as a brilliant theorist, always asked what the results of an experiment were. McGrew also offers a thought experiment about building data centers: a shortage of bulldozers requires a bulldozer factory, which requires clearing ground, which requires bulldozers — illustrating why intelligence alone can’t short-circuit physical construction chains.

03 Why US manufacturing lags and Arda’s fix

McGrew says he spent a year interviewing manufacturing leaders after leaving OpenAI and concluded the US problem is pure economics: automated lines only pencil out when products stay roughly static (cars, 7-year cycles, large upfront buildout costs), but innovative products like the iPhone have 75% of parts change annually, forcing mostly manual, hand assembly in Asia. Flexible automation requiring near-90% automation to pencil out is the unlock Arda targets, aiming to let manufacturers reconfigure automated lines within seven days by uploading a product design — matching the pace manufacturers consider agile for bringing a design change into production on a manual line. He identifies defense (citing fast design iteration driven by the war in Ukraine), consumer electronics, and data-center supply chains as the industries most needing this flexibility, and favors simple two-arm-and-camera robotic cells over humanoid robots, since existing robotic arms already have sufficient dexterity — the barrier is economic (capex must be redesigned annually), not mechanical.

04 Open vs. closed-world robotics and Arda’s product

McGrew distinguishes open-world robotics (e.g., cleaning an unfamiliar bedroom, requiring long-horizon, human-like intelligence) from closed-world problems like assembly lines, where everything is controlled and repetitive — ideal for traditional automation plus AI-driven perception and reinforcement learning to replace precise mechanical calibration, which he compares to a Rube Goldberg machine of fixtures and chutes. Arda’s current product, built with Anduril over the past year, is explicitly positioned as the manufacturing-engineering equivalent of Cursor: first building an IDE-like data structure (a version-control analog) to encapsulate manufacturing processes scattered across many different documents, then layering AI agents on top. Rather than training proprietary frontier models, Arda uses off-the-shelf models as orchestrators atop specialized tools (motion planning, CAD inspection) — a deliberate problem-first choice McGrew contrasts with OpenAI’s technology-first approach, recalling Sutskever’s rejection of customer-driven iteration in favor of scaling training data.

05 Robotics ecosystem and general models

On why general robotics models like Astra and Fable suddenly work, McGrew attributes progress not to proprietary robot-data advantages but to models finally extracting fine-grained real-world representations from vast internet video data, which can then be fine-tuned for robotics tasks. He sees a tension: large general models work well out-of-the-box but are hard to fine-tune and slow, creating opportunities for specialist players (he names Pi and Generalist) to fine-tune for specific use cases, and for agentic robotics approaches that treat these models as one tool among many. He and Gil briefly discuss on-device models, noting speed and safety tradeoffs push some capability onto distilled, smaller edge models.

06 Forward-deployed engineering and the endgame

McGrew credits Palantir CTO Shyam Sankar for originating the forward-deployed engineering (FDE) model, which he says only works if it’s a genuine product strategy — embedding engineers with a single power user until the product works, then scaling via sponsors and visionaries in the “Crossing the Chasm” sense, rather than functioning as disguised consulting (a critique FDE faced from 2015-2020 before Palantir’s profitability validated it). Looking forward, McGrew envisions factories building factories, increasingly automated and intelligent, as a near-term (10-year) extension of a “physical singularity” alongside the digital one, eventually enabling self-replicating probes as humanity’s (or AI’s) means of interstellar expansion, which he offers as his resolution to the Fermi Paradox.
