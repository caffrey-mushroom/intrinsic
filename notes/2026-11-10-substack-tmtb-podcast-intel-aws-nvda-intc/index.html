THE SIGNAL

  • Garman expects AWS to stay capacity-short for the next couple of years and says it could sell out every accelerator landing in 2027 today, so supply, not demand, likely caps near-term AWS growth (our read).- Garman says each 50% cut in inference cost has brought roughly 10x more usage, and he puts inference below 1% of its eventual size, which argues price cuts expand AWS AI revenue rather than shrink it.- Rationing is already happening: AWS sometimes pushes customers from a 2-week to a 4-week delivery, or from the East Coast to the West Coast or India, while landing one GPU every 14 seconds.- Most large-customer commitments run 3 to 6 years, some 8 to 10, and customers often spend above them; Garman also puts on-prem at about 85% of workloads, the market Trainium on-prem sales target.

01 The growth case for $1 trillion

Heath notes Andy Jassy’s framing moved from roughly $600 billion by around 2036 early this year to a trillion floated in Q2 and a firmer Q3 tone; Garman ties it to AWS’s current $182 billion run-rate business growing 39% year-over-year, with year-over-year growth accelerating for six consecutive quarters. He attributes this less to AI hype itself and more to a structural shift: companies that kept core workloads on-premise for two decades are now migrating them specifically so AI agents can access and act on that data, since agents struggle to operate against siloed mainframes or legacy data warehouses. He frames this as opening an entirely new pool of revenue and business creation for customers, rather than cloud share simply reallocating spend from one place to another.

02 Capacity, risk, and who gets the GPUs

On CapEx discipline, Garman describes a staged investment approach modeled on Amazon’s supply-chain roots: buying long-lead-time assets (land, power contracts, permits) years in advance while deferring purchases of servers and networking gear until closer to need, which limits downside if demand assumptions prove wrong since land can be resold. He says large customers often sign five-to-ten-year capacity commitments that give AWS visibility, and that demand still exceeds supply despite heavy spending. On allocation, Garman says AWS deliberately avoids selling 100% of future capacity to large labs, reserving blocks for enterprises needing small GPU counts and for startups that don’t exist yet, plus capacity set aside for its Bedrock service. Asked about systemic compute-spending risk tied to neo-clouds and data-center financing, Garman argues AWS’s exposure is structurally different: its customer base is broad and not predominantly high-rate debt-financed, and even if one model provider — Anthropic or OpenAI, both close AWS partners — stumbled, inference demand would simply shift to other models rather than disappear.

03 Inference, training, and open vs. closed models

Garman calls misleading a stat Heath cites, that only about 2% of Americans have paid for AI, as a sign the market is small, comparing it to how few people directly pay for database software despite relying on it daily through other products. He argues inference — not training — is the dominant and still-nascent AWS AI workload, embedded invisibly in applications like ad serving, trading, and product search. Training, by contrast, he describes as valuable but concentrated among a small number of customers and a minority of AWS’s business. On open versus closed weights, Garman speculates frontier labs may slow open-weights progress by locking down models against distillation (hiding reasoning tokens, encryption), though he expects other players, including in China, to keep building open models from first principles; he also notes OpenAI and Anthropic have begun releasing cheaper distilled versions of their own frontier models rather than ceding that market to open weights.

04 Nova reset, safety, and automated reasoning

Garman says Amazon remains just as committed to frontier in-house models despite recent Nova layoffs and pausing, characterizing it as a strategic reset to fix talent, data, and infrastructure approaches, not a retreat. On agent safety, he highlights automated reasoning — neuro-symbolic AI combining mathematical proofs with AI systems — as a major internal investment, citing AWS’s IAM Access Analyzer as a working example, with ambitions to let agents (e.g., processing insurance claims) operate under provably correct guardrails rather than needing human-in-the-loop review; he expects some such tools to ship next year.

05 Stack strategy, external chip sales, and data-center politics

Garman says AWS intends to compete at every layer of the cloud/AI stack — chips and networking, managed services, and end-user applications (Connect, Quick, Kiro) — while treating inference providers like Fireworks, Baseten, and Modal as largely complementary partners building atop AWS, akin to Snowflake or MongoDB. He confirms AWS is now selling Trainium chips for on-premise and sovereign-AI deployments, noting a large share of workloads remain on-prem globally and that NVIDIA already sells broadly beyond the major hyperscalers. Asked about the political backlash to data centers after the midterms and the Texas governor’s race, Garman argues AWS is unfairly lumped in with less responsible builders, pointing to AWS’s carbon-free energy investments, a 2030 water-positive commitment, and his statement that AWS’s largest data centers use less water than 40 US households, while acknowledging the industry needs better community engagement and codified standards with state governments. He dismisses space- and ocean-based data centers as impractical for a decade or two given launch costs, heat dissipation, and radiation, but sees desert locations as plausible given cooling and land advantages.

01 Why the affair matters strategically

Host Jordan Schneider and guest Chris McGuire (CFR senior fellow, former NSC deputy senior director) frame the alleged relationship between Huang and a figure tied to Chinese intelligence as significant not because of infidelity but because of what Huang knows and controls. McGuire says Huang has personal, direct communications with President Trump on AI and export-control policy, and that NVIDIA has more visibility into global compute allocation than the U.S. government itself. Because demand for advanced chips vastly exceeds supply, Huang effectively arbitrates who receives them and when — making any compromise of him a potential channel for adversary access to both policy intelligence and allocation decisions.

02 The enforcement decision at the core

McGuire identifies the real inflection point as Commerce’s May 2025 choice not to enforce the Biden administration’s third-country license requirement, which he says was made at Huang’s request, with the administration promising a replacement rule that never materialized. The result, he says, is an unclear, selectively enforced patchwork of controls that Chinese buyers are exploiting through Southeast Asian intermediaries — a bigger practical loophole, in his view, than the long-running debate over H200 sales to China directly. Legal gaps compound this: an Inspur-linked U.S. subsidiary reportedly supplied Megaspeed despite Inspur’s entity-list status, illustrating how listed-company restrictions can be circumvented domestically.

03 Commerce’s non-response and the Kessler report

Both speakers question why Commerce allegedly held intelligence about Megaspeed’s leadership links to Chinese intelligence yet took no action to block the shipments or warn Huang. McGuire calls the absence of a defensive briefing a deliberate choice, speculating it could reflect a separate ongoing investigation or a desire to preserve intelligence sources, though he stresses this is speculation. McGuire cites a New York Times report that under secretary Kessler discussed Commerce’s Megaspeed investigation directly with NVIDIA — behavior McGuire calls a clear breach, comparable to FBI leadership briefing a suspect’s business partner — and he says DOJ then took the probe over from Commerce, disputing NVIDIA’s public claims that investigations had cleared Megaspeed of diversion.

04 Conflicts of interest and industry culture

Schneider distinguishes personal corruption from corruption that harms national strategic interests, noting Huang’s political donations went to legal entities like a presidential library rather than personal pockets. Citing its public SEC filings, McGuire flags Cantor Fitzgerald, tied to Commerce Secretary Lutnick, as holding NVIDIA as what he calls its third-biggest unhedged exposure (behind the S&P 500 and Amazon), alongside heavy AMD and Marvell positions — raising optics concerns given policies favorable to the sector. McGuire broadens the critique to the tech industry generally, arguing it hasn’t internalized national-security obligations the way banks did after adopting sanctions compliance regimes, citing what he says DOJ appears to allege, a Saudi government plot to spy on OpenAI, as a parallel example of foreign governments targeting strategically important private firms.

05 Legislative and political fallout

McGuire sees the revelations boosting prospects for bipartisan bills — the Match Act, RASA, and Overwatch — potentially attached to the NDAA, though he notes NDAA passage faces unrelated complications. Republican committee chairs have already sent oversight letters to NVIDIA, and incoming Democratic chairs (Senators Warren and Coons) are signaling scrutiny. McGuire argues legislation alone won’t close counter-smuggling, remote-access, and domestic-purchase loopholes — Commerce itself needs to impose global license requirements and ban Chinese entity-linked purchases — but calls Commerce’s resistance to such steps baffling and unexplained. The episode closes with Schneider’s unrelated historical segment on espionage honey traps, covering biblical, Cold War, Soviet, and WWII examples.

01 The PC collapse

Lyons and Sekar describe a classic elasticity problem: memory price surges driven by data-center AI demand forced Intel to raise client-chip prices, and consumers/enterprises responded by delaying refreshes rather than paying more. Sekar cites Intel’s own Q2 earnings call, where the CFO reportedly confirmed client revenue grew from price hikes rather than unit volume, and predicted continued softness — yet framed it as fine since surplus wafers could go toward server CPUs instead. That reallocation plan, the hosts argue, never materialized as expected, leaving Intel with excess client-chip inventory sitting unsold with OEMs, which will depress the next quarter’s guide further. Lyons notes PC client demand is surprisingly elastic even in enterprise, where companies simply push back refreshes when per-seat costs rise.

02 Substrate and chiplet bottlenecks

Even if Intel had diverted Intel 3 wafers from client to server chips, the hosts explain, modern chiplet designs require advanced packaging (EMIB) to combine compute tiles with IO dies built on older nodes (Intel 7/10), and there simply aren’t enough substrates industry-wide to assemble finished CPUs. Lyons details how different server CPU families — Sapphire/Emerald Rapids (Intel 7), Sierra/Granite Rapids and Clearwater Forest (Intel 3/18A) — are node-specific designs that can’t simply be moved to underloaded 18A capacity without lengthy validation and qualification. The only lever Intel has is physically relocating fab tooling (etching, deposition) between nodes, a slow process the hosts say would also pull resources away from ramping 18A yields, which is critical to the external foundry’s long-term competitiveness against TSMC’s 2nm node.

03 The Fab 28 explosion

The hosts describe a gas-leak explosion at Intel’s Fab 28 in Israel in October, which Sekar says killed two people and which disrupted supply of 10nm-class IO dies needed to complete server CPUs. Sekar says Intel has lost roughly $300 million this quarter from the explosion and expects another $300 million-plus next quarter, and he sees no certainty the total won’t run higher. Lyons raises the possibility Intel prioritized external foundry customers over internal product needs in the aftermath — a defensible long-term trust-building move he says Intel never articulated publicly, representing what he calls a missed communications opportunity to reinforce the foundry growth story.

04 A planning and coordination failure

Both hosts repeatedly return to the idea that Intel, as an integrated device manufacturer with client, server and foundry units under one roof, should have coordinated wafer allocation well in advance given known memory and substrate constraints. Lyons proposes a counterfactual: had Intel deliberately over-produced server CPUs and under-produced client chips a year ago, investors likely would have forgiven a miss framed around betting on the AI buildout. Sekar adds that Intel seemed surprised by agentic-AI CPU demand as recently as Q1 2026, which he finds implausible for a company with decades of CPU expertise, while Lyons speculates Intel’s lack of a competitive GPU may have cut it off from early demand signals that NVIDIA and AMD get through direct relationships with AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic.

05 Foundry outlook and closing lessons

Despite the miss, both hosts view Intel’s 18A and 14A nodes as strategically sound — 18A as the volume alternative to TSMC’s 2nm process, 14A as a likely Apple-centric mobile node — but caution that 2027’s first half still looks weak pending substrate supply recovery and an external customer announcement that hasn’t materialized. They close with broader lessons for investors beyond Intel: that unmet demand alone doesn’t translate to earnings, that AI tailwinds in one business line (servers) can actively cannibalize another (PCs) by straining shared inputs like memory, and that markets increasingly demand sustained execution rather than one good quarter, especially amid high rates and volatility.

01 Power as the binding constraint

Terlizzi frames the entire conversation around compressing the time between signing a lease or buying land and delivering GPU capacity to customers. He identifies power access as the top strategic limiter — how quickly a substation can be built, how much power is available, and whether a site’s transmission and distribution infrastructure can actually be accessed. Bond adds nuance from the brokerage side, noting that seeing a 138kV or 345kV line near a site doesn’t guarantee usable power; generation, transmission, and distribution access must all align, and utilities and municipalities increasingly push development costs for grid upgrades onto developers rather than ratepayers — costs that can make an otherwise viable site economically unworkable or delay delivery by years.

02 Modular construction and dual-track development

To compress schedules, CoreWeave prefabricates components off-site — self-contained UPS units built as skids or containers, mechanical/chiller modules, hot-aisle containment tech loops, and electrical distribution units — so capacity is fungible and can shift between projects running ahead of or behind schedule. Terlizzi describes pursuing self-performed development (buying land, building, owning, and operating) because it grants control over pace and improves total cost of ownership and margins, while simultaneously maintaining joint ventures and leases with data center partners to meet demand that he says exceeds what any single approach could satisfy.

03 Community, sustainability, and the New Jersey site

CoreWeave is delivering its first self-performed facility in its home state of New Jersey, a former research and technology center being adaptively reused, which Terlizzi frames as reestablishing the site as an East Coast technology epicenter. Terlizzi calls the facility 100% renewable and says its nuclear power is bought through the utility — Bond describes nuclear as likely to be a significant part of the industry’s solution to rising utility demand. Terlizzi emphasizes closed-loop, liquid-cooled designs placing cooling close to the load to address community concerns about water and energy use (which Bond attributes partly to misinformation circulating in the market) while also driving efficiency and lowering power costs. The New Jersey site will also host CoreWeave University, a training hub for apprentices and existing employees combining classroom and hands-on labs for data center technicians and engineers, with a second location planned in Texas; CoreWeave also engages in local community initiatives like food banks and golf outings.

04 Demand, training vs. inference, and sector lessons

Terlizzi says demand continues to intensify across industries and geographies, with CoreWeave supporting customers including OpenAI, Anthropic, and Perplexity. He distinguishes between large, often remote campus facilities used for training and smaller, tier-one or tier-one-adjacent facilities built for inference, where latency matters — a split Bond says is driving a broader industry shift toward tier-one inference product alongside continued gigawatt-scale remote training campuses. Bond draws a historical parallel to the early-2000s telecom crash, noting that failed dot-com infrastructure was repurposed into the first wave of colocation facilities, though he notes today’s AI-driven buildout differs substantially in scale and technical requirements. Terlizzi also reflects on his prior roles at Telx and Digital Realty (which acquired Telx), describing how that interconnection-focused, entrepreneurial culture shaped his approach at CoreWeave.

05 Financing and market scale

Terlizzi notes CoreWeave finances self-performed builds through joint ventures with partners as well as direct capital investment, aiming to own the buildings it develops. Bond observes a broader capital-markets trend of large data center providers moving toward public listings, reversing an earlier era when data center REITs went private to access capital, now reversed because capital requirements have grown into the billions. On raw scale, Bond cites roughly 60 gigawatts of global interconnection/data center capacity currently, against five-year forecasts of dramatically larger capacity — an expansion Terlizzi frames as an exciting but genuinely hard engineering and planning problem given that data centers take 18-24 months to build while GPU reference architectures change every six to eight months.

01 The semi-apocalypse sell-off

Recording just after a brutal session two days after the election, Goldberg (whom Bajarin calls the show’s bear) says the SOX index had one of its worst days, with good and bad companies, beaters and missers, all down — many by double digits — with no clear catalyst. Goldberg separately proposes that analysts are now building 2028 models for the first time, forcing uncomfortable assumptions about a year when new fabs, data centers, and energy capacity all come online simultaneously — which Bajarin ties to his own research note on the coming year as peak constraint, questioning whether demand will absorb the step-change in capacity.

02 Enterprise adoption as the counter-thesis

Despite the panic, both hosts remain bullish on enterprise AI as the sector’s real funding engine. Bajarin, citing CIO/CTO/CSO survey work and conversations with a major OEM, says the top 20% of enterprises are deep in a structural rethink of security, data governance, and observability for agentic deployment, while the remaining 80% lag via phased pilots. He notes a frontier model being positioned as a dedicated cybersecurity tool, and that 80% of enterprise edge data is unstructured — a problem agents are just beginning to address. Goldberg counters that he’s more bullish on timing, pointing to Cloudflare’s customer surge as evidence enterprises are already ramping agentic workloads faster than expected, alongside direct conversations where companies are rushing adoption purely for productivity gains.

03 Anthropic’s hidden compute crunch

Goldberg dissects Anthropic’s S-1 in detail: sequential growth fell from 150% to 40%, which bears read as demand saturation, but he argues the deeper story is supply-constrained. The company suffered an outage of about a day earlier in the year attributed to running out of compute, and, by Goldberg’s reading of the filing, it bought much of its Q3 compute on the spot market rather than under contract, to avoid throttling sign-ups ahead of an IPO.

04 AMD, Intel, Arm, Qualcomm earnings

AMD’s quarter is framed as the season’s standout: Bajarin says AMD now signals about 80% growth for 2027, up from roughly 70%, with Bajarin suggesting AMD could plausibly approach $100B in annual revenue. Both hosts emphasize Su explicitly addressed secured supply chain (substrates, memory, TSMC capacity) — the variable that hurt Intel, which failed to secure comparable supply and which, Bajarin says, AMD has now passed in server revenue for the first time. Arm, previously vocal about supply anxiety, sounded notably more confident this quarter after locking in agreements, though its stock didn’t react. Bajarin recalls Qualcomm’s CFO signaling a bigger non-mobile adjacency business next year, with new “high bandwidth compute” data drawing host interest. The broader theme: AMD, Arm, and Qualcomm are all de-emphasizing legacy client/mobile narratives in favor of data-center positioning, even though CPUs/mobile still represent 60-70% of some of their revenue bases.

05 SiTime’s timing-chip opportunity

Bajarin, fresh off a deep-dive report, frames SiTime as benefiting from an analog-to-digital shift — replacing quartz crystal oscillators with programmable MEMS-based timing chips. As compute density rises and racks require tighter synchronization across networking, CPUs, and GPUs, timing content per rack increases, with higher-frequency roadmap parts carrying higher ASPs. SiTime has closed its purchase of Renesas’ timing division, which brings some quartz parts with it; Bajarin thinks that leaves SiTime with a large share of the timing market, though quartz will still be needed in some uses, such as satellites. Goldberg calls it a niche-but-essential technology now capturing a rapidly expanding addressable market, declining to comment on valuation.

01 Role and research agenda

Chatterji, OpenAI’s first chief economist and a Duke professor (previously White House CHIPS coordinator and Commerce Department chief economist), says he had to define the job himself two years ago. His team covers three areas: the future of work, enterprise AI adoption (which he says is underweighted by AI researchers despite being the channel through which AGI’s economic impact will actually flow), and frontier topics like recursive self-improvement and multi-agent collaboration.

02 AI versus past general-purpose technologies

Drawing on history — the printing press, electricity, semiconductors, IT — Chatterji notes two recurring patterns: experts diverge sharply on a new technology’s impact in real time, and diffusion is uneven with a long last-mile lag. Electricity’s productivity gains, he notes, came not from the technology itself but from reorganizing factories around it years later; semiconductors took decades to move from Air Force procurement to consumer products. He argues AI is diffusing faster than those precedents, but organizations are still in an early reorganization phase comparable to the factory-electrification era, with AI-native startups holding an advantage over incumbents trying to bolt AI on like a SaaS plug-in.

03 Growth, adoption gaps, and frontier intelligence

Luzzetti cites Deutsche Bank chart-book data: Census Bureau figures show only ~28% of firms use AI regularly, and Ramp data put median per-employee AI spend at $16. Chatterji says the frontier/median gap (the 8x Enterprise Signals figure) is the key number to watch, alongside industries likely to demand frontier intelligence specifically — he names cyber, bio, and financial services as leading demand drivers. He stresses that productivity will come both from efficiency and cost-cutting (the dominant current narrative) and, more importantly in his view, from entirely new products and ideas — an innovation channel he says is underappreciated.

04 Labor market: complements over displacement, so far

Chatterji says earlier predictions of structural AI-driven unemployment reflected an attribution error — extrapolating from O*NET task-level exposure data to assume displacement was imminent — and underweighted the political economy of adoption (citing teachers as a profession people don’t want automated regardless of benchmarks). He expects new occupations to emerge that don’t yet have established names, referencing his team’s Substack piece on the subject, and points to job listings and freelance-platform data as early indicators; freelance work, he notes, is shifting toward larger, multi-stage projects requiring orchestration and delivery rather than piecemeal tasks. He also expects continued new work to appear within existing human-intensive sectors such as education and healthcare as productivity gains raise incomes.

05 Inflation and infrastructure constraints

Near-term inflation pressure is coming from AI infrastructure hardware (DRAM, GPUs, CPUs, broader energy supply chains) tied to the compute buildout; medium-term, productivity gains should turn disinflationary, though he cautions this effect has not yet begun and will be hard to isolate from other factors. The biggest risks to AI’s pace, in his view, are infrastructure-related: tightening supply chains and local permitting or community pushback, which he frames as an expected part of operating within a democratic system rather than a flaw.

06 Expertise effects and downside risks

Chatterji distinguishes task types: for routine, repeatable work AI helps novices reach median performance quickly; for tasks requiring critical thinking and delegation, AI advantages existing experts — a skill-biased pattern he says undercuts earlier fears that AI would displace the consulting industry, suggesting instead that the best consultants will be those who use AI best. His own top-of-mind downside risks are less about jobs and more about education and critical thinking — citing his team’s field experiment with an Italian university showing that, under some conditions, AI paired with critical-thinking training benefited students — plus a broader erosion of public trust in AI outputs. To track progress, he recommends watching revenue-per-employee trends, sector-specific frontier-intelligence deployment (cyber, bio, financial services), and qualitative Fed district reports and earnings-call commentary.
