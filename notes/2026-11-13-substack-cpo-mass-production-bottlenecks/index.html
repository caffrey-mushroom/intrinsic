Why this note matters

This note is for investors following the co-packaged optics (CPO) ramp and the companies exposed to it: hybrid-bonding tool makers (Besi, Tokyo Electron, EV Group), CPO test equipment vendors (ficonTEC, MPI, Teradyne, Advantest, FormFactor, Chroma), and the foundry and OSAT supply chain (TSMC, ASE/SPIL). We interviewed several equipment and process experts on where CPO mass production stands, with a focus on hybrid bonding, the step that currently limits yield. The pace of scale-out CPO also matters for NPO: the longer CPO takes to mature, the more likely NPO is to be used in scale-up, and for longer. We then walk through the open issues in each of the four test insertions, our checks on the main vendors at each step, and a bottom-up estimate of the CPO test equipment TAM for 2027-2028.

Conclusion 1:

Compared with our June note on the CPO delay debate and our October OCP summary, we are now more cautious on the pace of scale-out CPO. CPO for scale-out switches is in production and shipping in small volumes, but the ramp is limited by bonding and testing, and large-scale delivery will come in late 2027 to 2028. CPO volume production still faces two main hurdles: 1) the W2W tools for hybrid bonding are not ready; and 2) an equipment vendor has not yet been selected for Insertion 2 testing. Bonding yields on the TSMC COUPE line, the open Insertion 2 vendor decision and customers adjusting their timelines mean 2027 deliveries will fall short of the roughly 100k Spectrum-X CPO switch target cited in our OCP note, with volume shipments pushed to late 2027 to 2028 (later than the market expects).

This report covers the optical engines used in NVIDIA’s COUPE-based scale-out CPO switches. Our view on scale-up is unchanged: NVIDIA’s next-generation scale-up will certainly use optics, but whether that is CPO or NPO is not yet decided, and the constraints described here make an early move to CPO in scale-up less likely. Demand for optical interconnect and high-power external light sources is not affected; what changes is the timing.

(I) Direct cause of the CPO delay #1: hybrid bonding

This report uses the 3D hybrid-bonded optical engine in Spectrum-X CPO as its main case. Wafer-level hybrid bonding on the COUPE platform works much like SoIC. It has two steps, D2W (current yield 80%) and W2W (current yield 50%), and neither yet meets the yield needed for stable, profitable mass production (each step needs to exceed 90%, and W2W is the step that most needs to improve). The main bottleneck today is that W2W tools with a vacuum chamber, which could lift W2W yield to about 90%, have not been successfully developed yet. These tools are expected to sample in 2H27, with the vendor decision in 1H28. This is one of the direct causes of the CPO delay.

Source: TSMC public materials, redrawn by FUNDA

How hybrid bonding works: Hybrid bonding forms the dielectric bond and the Cu-Cu metal bond at the same interface in one step. It is a bumpless direct-bonding technology. Compared with conventional micro-bumps at a 40-50 µm pitch, hybrid bonding can shrink the interconnect pitch below 10 µm, giving much higher I/O density over a shorter electrical path. The standard flow is: (1) CMP the bonding surfaces so the dielectric (SiO2/SiCN) is extremely flat, with the Cu pads recessed slightly below it by a few nanometers; (2) clean and plasma-activate the surfaces to raise bond energy; (3) align and bring the surfaces into contact at room temperature, so the dielectric bonds first; (4) anneal at about 200-400°C, where the Cu expands to fill the recess and forms a metallic Cu-Cu bond; (5) after bonding, use infrared or acoustic scanning (C-SAM) to look for voids and unbonded areas. In TSMC’s SoIC and COUPE flows, D2W and W2W are two consecutive steps in the same flow, not two competing technology routes.

How the two steps fit together: In the COUPE flow, D2W first places screened, known-good EIC dies and lenses one by one onto the PIC wafer. W2W then bonds the whole wafer to a carrier wafer, which supports the later flip, thinning and backside routing. The expert we spoke with has hands-on bonding process experience and sees the two steps as consecutive stages of one flow, with W2W partly serving as temporary support for the backside process.

Expert A (bonding process expert at an equipment maker):

“Die-to-wafer and wafer-to-wafer are often used together in the same product flow. For example, if you need backside routing later, you first do a temporary wafer-to-wafer bond to hold the structure.”

The yields of the two steps therefore multiply, and a weak step drags down the final output of the whole wafer. That is why we use compounded yields when sizing the equipment for both steps below.

(1) Step 1: Die-to-wafer (D2W)

This step covers EIC-to-PIC hybrid bonding and OE-to-lens bonding. The technology is relatively mature. The tool vendor is Besi, at roughly US$3mn per tool, and Besi is close to a sole supplier. Assuming a 90 mm² OE die, a 12-inch wafer yields about 700 dies. Bonding takes 2-3 hours per wafer, so one D2W bonder at 100% utilization can process about 2.04mn OEs a year (700 dies per wafer × 8 wafers per day × 365 days). Current yield at this step is 80%.

Process flow: The EIC wafer is tested and diced first, and only known-good dies (KGD) are kept. After the dies and the PIC wafer go through CMP and plasma activation, the bonder picks each die, flips it, aligns it to its target site on the PIC wafer and places it, forming a pre-bond at room temperature. Once the wafer is fully populated, it is annealed in one batch to form the Cu-Cu bonds. Lens dies are placed the same way, one at a time.

What this step does well and where it falls short: D2W lets you bond only good dies and allows dies of different sizes to be integrated together, which helps overall yield and cost. That is why steps like EIC and lens attach, where you want to screen before bonding, use D2W. Placing dies one at a time limits throughput. Particles and die-edge defects from dicing also require extra cleaning. Besi is the main supplier of D2W hybrid bonders and develops integrated solutions together with Applied Materials.

Capacity and tool supply: The core value of D2W is screening first and bonding second. The expert sees the ability to pick good dies before bonding as the main yield and cost advantage of D2W.

Expert A (bonding process expert at an equipment maker):

“The biggest advantage of die-to-wafer is that you can pick out the good dies first and then bond them. That makes a very clear difference to overall yield and cost efficiency.”

On capacity, the expert estimates that D2W bonding of a 12-inch wafer takes about 2-3 hours, so one tool handles about 8 wafers a day. The foundry plans for roughly 100 D2W tools and has about 30 today. Roughly 30 tools translate into about 7,300 wafers a month. The expert also flagged Besi’s delivery capacity as a possible constraint on expansion.

Expert A (bonding process expert at an equipment maker):

“The foundry needs a lot of Besi tools. I think Besi’s delivery capacity could become a bottleneck.”

(2) Step 2: Wafer-to-wafer (W2W), the main hybrid-bonding bottleneck

This step bonds the carrier wafer to the lens surface. Because the lens surface is not flat, gas tends to get trapped between the lens and the carrier. Tool vendors need to supply bonders with a vacuum chamber to prevent voids, but those tools are still in development. This is where the biggest yield problem in hybrid bonding sits.

Figure 3: COUPE wafer-to-wafer carrier bonding and the risk of voids

Source: Expert interviews, industry process-flow materials, redrawn by FUNDA

W2W still runs on non-vacuum tools today, with a low yield of about 50%. EV Group (EVG) and Tokyo Electron (TEL) are the vendors able to design vacuum-chamber tools, and TEL is seen as being further ahead. We expect early demo tools in 2H27 and mature tools in 2028. A tool is expected to cost about US$30mn (an expert estimate, since vacuum-chamber tools have not yet been successfully developed; the price should come down once in volume production). Throughput of the vacuum tool cannot be estimated yet. Taking the non-vacuum tool as a reference at about 20 wafers per hour, one tool can process about 122.6mn OEs a year (20 wafers/hour × 24 hours × 365 days × 700 dies). At 50% utilization, with 50% yield at this step and 65% downstream yield, one tool can support about 550k CPO switches.

Process flow: W2W aligns two full wafers and bonds them in one shot. In the COUPE flow, after D2W attaches the EICs and lenses, the whole wafer is bonded to a carrier wafer. The carrier gives mechanical support for flipping, backside thinning, TSV reveal and bumping, before dicing and 2.5D packaging. Our channel checks indicate the new platform has settled on silicon carbide (SiC) carriers.

What hurts yields: The lens surface is not flat. When it is bonded to a flat carrier, gaps form at the interface and trap gas, leaving bubbles or unbonded patches. Bubbles can also expand during anneal and spread from one die to its neighbors. A vacuum chamber pumps the gas out before contact and is the main way to cut voids. Carrier bonding bonds the whole wafer at once, so throughput is high, but one void can affect many dies on the wafer, and the step is more sensitive to warpage and particles. Yield at this step directly sets the output of the whole wafer.

The expert believes the EIC-PIC bonding process itself is largely proven. The main mass-production challenge is bonding a non-flat surface like the lens to a flat carrier.

Expert A (bonding process expert at an equipment maker):

“EIC-to-PIC bonding and the related processes have basically been verified. Different ICs and substrates may need recipe tweaks, but that is not the main problem now. The harder part is bonding an uneven structure like the lens to a flat carrier.”

The expert then explained how voids grow from a single-die issue into a wafer-level yield problem, and gave a rough estimate of yield below 50%. This is a judgment, not measured data.

Expert A (bonding process expert at an equipment maker):

“Whether the lens is concave or convex, there will be gaps at the interface, and you get bubbles or areas that do not bond. If the bubbles or particles expand during anneal, a defect can spread from one die to several, and yield can fall below 50%. For volume production you normally need at least around 90%.”

Timeline for vacuum bonders: Bonding in a vacuum reduces trapped gas at the interface, but there is no proven production tool yet. The expert gave several ranges for the development timeline, all with wide uncertainty.

Expert A (bonding process expert at an equipment maker):

“For the new generation of vacuum tools, I think it will take at least two to three years, or a year and a half to two years if things go fast. We might see some demo results by the end of 2027, and it could happen in 2028, but none of this is certain.”

On vendors, our channel checks show that EVG already has a relevant platform, but the foundry has concerns about its bonding alignment and leans toward evaluating TEL, with EVG as a backup. The foundry also wants a second source, though other vendors do not yet seem to match that performance. If vacuum tools are not ready in time, the process sequence may need to change, for example by modifying the wafer bumping steps or doing some steps on the carrier earlier. Whether these alternatives work, and which one the foundry will choose, is still unclear.

Inspection: Before bonding, high-resolution optical scans can catch particles of 1-2 µm. After bonding, acoustic scanning (C-SAM) checks for voids and unbonded areas. Inspection only finds defects; it cannot replace control of where the defects come from.

Expert A (bonding process expert at an equipment maker):

“Inspection can find the defects, but it cannot replace controlling the sources of those defects, like particles and bubbles.”

So W2W yield improvement depends mainly on the tools and the process. Adding inspection only keeps more bad parts from reaching the back end; it does not raise bonding yield.

(3) Summary: how bonding sets the pace of CPO mass production

Overall, D2W tools and the process are relatively mature, and near-term capacity can cover 2027 demand. Low bonding yields raise product cost, but they have not stopped shipments. What really sets the pace of volume production is W2W carrier bonding: non-vacuum tools yield about 50%, vacuum tools should only reach early samples in 2H27, and they may not mature until 2028. This matches what we hear from the test side. The test expert we interviewed believes low yields and integration difficulty have already pushed some customers to adjust their adoption timelines.

Expert B (test equipment expert):

“Wafer-to-die yield is only about 30% to 40% right now (the combined yield from the PIC wafer to the final good optical engine, not the yield of the D2W step alone), and integration is hard, so enthusiasm for CPO has cooled recently. Several customers are adjusting their timelines and pushing full CPO adoption to 2028.”

(II) Direct cause of the CPO delay #2: Insertion 2 testing

Figure 5: CPO test flow: burn-in and four test insertions

Source: Expert interviews, drawn by FUNDA

Conclusion: The Insertion 2 vendor has not yet been selected, and the test environment is not yet mature. The test process itself (for example, clamping force) can also affect OE yield, so bonded wafers can be damaged by an imperfect test process. Because the Insertion 2 vendor has not been selected, the later insertions cannot be finalized either. Measured from the start to the end of Insertion 2, the yield of the wafer (which carries bonded, undiced OEs) is low, at about 30% (40% bonding yield × 80% Insertion 2 test yield).

(1) Insertion 1: lowest cost, high value as an early screen

Insertion 1 runs on bare 300 mm wafers with 100% coverage before dicing. The tester must precisely control wavelength, polarization, attenuation, incidence angle and alignment, then inject light and capture both the reflected light and the electrical response. This step is fast, about 2 seconds per EIC and 10 seconds per PIC, and the foundry currently runs it. The expert sees this step as having high diagnostic value within the whole test flow.

Expert B (test equipment expert):

“Insertion 1 is a key diagnostic step. Before you spend heavily on back-end packaging, systematic process problems like mask defects, lithography errors and etch issues should be caught here.”

(2) Insertion 2: die-by-die light finding makes this the slowest and hardest step

This is the hardest of all the test steps and very slow, at 7-8 hours per wafer. Silicon photonic waveguides are sub-micron, about 0.4 µm, so the optical signal is extremely sensitive to position, and the external fiber must be aligned very precisely to the on-chip structures.

The test equipment expert we interviewed believes Insertion 2 takes so long because every die area needs its own active alignment. In the expert’s view, there is little room to shorten this step, so higher throughput will mainly come from buying more tools.

Expert C (test equipment expert):

“A 12-inch wafer has a few hundred separate die areas, and the tool has to find the light on every one of them, which means high-precision active alignment. Physically it is the same thing as optical coupling in packaging later on. The time is already at its limit, and there is basically no room to simplify.”

Specifically, light enters the grating coupler from below at a near-vertical angle (about 11 degrees) and goes into the waveguide. The test checks both directions: light injected through the grating to confirm the internal optical path, and light coming out to verify the chip’s output. The tool is usually set up as “electrical on top, optical below”, with the optical coupling tester handling light finding and optical tests and the ATE vendor handling electrical tests.

Wafer warpage and clamping: The PIC and EIC wafers have different stress profiles, so warpage after bonding changes the local coupling angle. Today the angle is mostly found by trial and error, wafer by wafer.

Expert C (test equipment expert):

“When the wafer is flat, you can use a preset optimal coupling angle. Once it bends, the local surface angle changes, and during testing you must correct the preset angle for the bend. Right now, we try wafer by wafer to find the right angle.”

A warped wafer also has different heights at different points, so the tool must handle height changes as well as X-Y positioning. Cracked dies are spotted by vision and skipped. For clamping, standard vacuum chucking may not suit badly warped wafers, and because the PIC is stress-sensitive, clamping stress can itself change the optical structure. The tool has to balance holding the wafer firmly against keeping stress low.

Mechanical challenges for the prober: Industry interviews explain why double-sided testing is hard from the prober’s point of view.

Expert D (prober expert):

“Normal electrical probing relies on a solid chuck to support the wafer. For double-sided testing you have to open an optical window under the wafer, so the chuck is less stiff. When the probes press down, the chuck can bend, and the optical alignment shifts with it.”

The interviews also confirmed that the foundry is temporarily skipping Insertion 2, as a stopgap until a production-ready Insertion 2 tester and a second source are in place.

Expert D (prober expert):

“Because there is no qualified double-sided wafer tester yet (meaning the foundry has not settled on its final vendors and their shares, including the first and second source), the foundry is skipping Insertion 2 for now. It hands the wafers to the OSAT and relies on Insertion 3 to pick out and scrap the bad dies. For a foundry whose core product is the processed wafer, that is not ideal.”

This supports our view in Conclusion 2 that Insertion 2 will most likely stay within the foundry’s flow.

(3) Insertion 3: testing and temperature control

The expert described the current Insertion 3 test process and its throughput target.

Expert E (test equipment expert):

“Devices come in on trays. After pick, rotate and position, the tool runs the electrical test first and then the optical test, one device at a time rather than the whole wafer. Right now it is about one minute per device, UPH around 60 to 65, and the tool makers are pushing toward UPH 80.”

Insertion 3 yield is currently about 90%. The hard part in volume production is meeting both throughput and yield targets. Tests run at room temperature (25°C) and at about 70°C, and keeping temperature repeatable under 24-hour heavy load is the main sticking point.

(4) Insertion 4: connector design is the key choice

This step usually includes an electrical test, where an ATE platform checks each electrical pin, signal path and die with the optical interfaces connected to confirm the module works electrically. It also includes an optical test: the module’s own transmitter (TX) output is looped back to its own receiver (RX) through a path with controlled loss, and bit error rate and link margin are measured under realistic link loss. This avoids fitting every channel with an external light source and optical receiver, so many channels can be tested in parallel.

Insertion 4 requires a test FAU to be plugged into the OE receptacle repeatedly. The test expert we interviewed believes durability is still 50-200 times short of what volume production needs, close to two orders of magnitude. We plan a separate note that takes this component apart in detail.

Expert B (test equipment expert):

“Today’s glass FAUs get tiny chips on the edges after repeated mating with metal, and they are rated for only 50 to 200 cycles. Running 24 hours a day in volume production, you need them to stay stable for about 10,000 cycles.”

Conclusion 2:

The market believes TSMC has dropped Insertion 2 for now because of its low yield and long test time. We think Insertion 2 will stay in TSMC’s flow once the choice of test equipment vendor is reasonably settled in 2H27. Over the long run, demand for Insertion 2 test equipment should grow, but new demand in 2027 may be limited.

TSMC is currently skipping Insertion 2 because it takes up to 8 hours per wafer, and market talk is that TSMC “cut” Insertion 2 to keep capacity and floor space for higher-margin business. In practice, after hybrid bonding TSMC sends the wafers straight to the OSAT for dicing. Before attaching the OE to the final PCB substrate or adding the FAU, the OSAT mounts each OE on a transitional PCB (a temporary fixture) and tests it individually, mainly optically. Only then is the FAU attached and Insertion 3 performed.

Why use a temporary fixture: The equipment expert we interviewed thinks the temporary fixture approach is technically workable, and its main purpose is to limit scrap losses.

Expert C (test equipment expert):

“With the foundry skipping Insertion 2, if you glue an OE that has not been fully verified permanently onto an expensive substrate and later find a problem, you cannot take it off, so the whole substrate and the chips on it are scrapped. If you first power it through a temporary fixture and test it with an optical probe from above, you only throw away that one OE when it fails, which keeps the loss to a minimum.”

As a rough estimate, scrapping one OE costs about US$1,000, while scrapping a whole switch chip package costs about US$50,000, roughly 50 times as much. If the failure happens at a customer site and the whole unit is returned, the loss exceeds US$100,000 at selling price. We therefore do not think NVIDIA will drop any insertion in the future, because it needs to catch bad parts as early as possible. And since TSMC ships hybrid-bonded wafers, it has to guarantee the yield of what it delivers, so Insertion 2 will stay at TSMC.

The Insertion 2 vendor has not yet been selected, and each candidate has its own strengths:

  • ficonTEC has established capabilities in coupling and alignment. It can integrate offerings from FormFactor and Coherent Solutions and has flexible software (PCM, Process Control Master) with strong integration capability. It lacks prober design experience and has less experience working alongside electrical test.
  • MPI is strong in probing, with expertise in prober structural design, force and deformation control, and backside optical alignment accuracy. These skills come from years of engineering as a prober maker, but MPI still needs to build up optical coupling experience.
  • We think ficonTEC will take a temporary lead in the Insertion 2 vendor evaluation on the strength of its optical coupling capability, but it will not hold an outright monopoly.

NVIDIA needs multiple suppliers, and ficonTEC’s competitor MPI will not launch its new tool until 1Q-2Q27. NVIDIA wants to start volume production with both the first and second source ready, which is pushing out the overall CPO ramp. Interviews suggest a second source with good enough optical coupling could take a 30% share.

ficonTEC’s delivery constraints: ficonTEC’s delivery capacity is limited by both staffing and the supply chain, so 2027 deliveries look conservative. Because its German plant delivers slowly, NVIDIA and other customers may bring in a second source, and if ficonTEC cannot deliver faster, it could lose share.

MPI’s path to catch up: Channel checks suggest MPI will launch a production-ready Insertion 2 tester in 1Q-2Q27, aiming for foundry qualification in 2027. MPI’s strengths are its long engineering history in wafer probers and its in-house probe cards; its probe card design also suits fine-pitch micro-bumps better than socket-based approaches, which should help as optical engines scale.

On ATE, channel checks indicate Advantest uses an open architecture that integrates mature instruments from vendors such as Keysight onto its platform. Teradyne entered the market first with a vertically integrated approach and filled its optical test gap through an acquisition, so it now covers the full test chain from PIC to optical engine to switch, though it still needs to prove its accuracy and capacity ramp to customers.

The designs for Insertions 3 and 4 depend on the Insertion 2 decision. Once TSMC settles on an Insertion 2 vendor, around 4Q27, we expect the OSAT and EMS responsibilities to split as follows. The OSAT’s scope starts when it receives the bonded PIC+EIC wafers from TSMC: dicing, packaging onto the ABF IC substrate, attaching the heat spreader, and connecting the FAU/coupler to produce a complete OE with fiber pigtails, which then goes to EMS partners such as Hon Hai.
