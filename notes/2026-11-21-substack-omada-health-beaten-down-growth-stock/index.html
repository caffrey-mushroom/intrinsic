The company is Omada Health (OMDA).

It closed at $18.95 on November 5, just before it raised its full-year forecast.

Today we’re looking at $11.87. Down 37% in a little over two weeks.

EV fell from roughly 3× trailing sales to 1.5×.

I have been looking for ways to gain exposure to healthcare and AI medicine.

When getting a quick understanding of OMDA, think about how you would manage something like high blood pressure. Most of that work happens at home, between visits to your doctor. Omada can send you a blood pressure monitor that feeds readings into its app and give you a coach and a specialist to work with along the way.

(When I first started digging into this, I was concerned about how easy it would be to replicate the software. But that’s not the right way to think about this. I’ll explain more on that in a bit.)

They do this for diabetes, weight management and a few other chronic conditions. Employers or health insurers generally pay them as people enroll and use the service.

I think the growing adoption of GLP-1s could be a pretty nice tailwind here. If you’re an employer spending a ton of money on weight-loss drugs for your employees, you probably want some help making sure they’re seeing results. One interesting example is that Disney actually requires people taking covered weight-loss medication to participate in Omada to maintain their coverage.

(I was a bit surprised by this. In KFF’s 2026 survey, a third of companies with 5,000+ employees that offer health benefits cover GLP-1s for weight loss. And among employers with 200+ employees that cover them, 40% require some form of clinical consultation or lifestyle program to get coverage.)

Omada also joined Eli Lilly’s employer program this year, giving it another way to reach people taking these drugs. Employers can even pay for Omada’s care while leaving the cost of the medication to their employees.

Interestingly, diabetes and high blood pressure were the big drivers of growth last quarter. These tend to be higher-priced programs, and people generally stay in them longer.

There’s room to sell more programs to existing customers, too. Only about a third currently use Omada for multiple conditions.

And Omada already has an agreement in place with Health Care Service Corporation (HCSC), a major Blue Cross Blue Shield insurer. They’re expanding their prevention and high blood pressure programs into three more states. That’s expected to launch in 2027 and could make these programs available to another 1.5 million people.

What I really like though is how much room they have to grow within the business they’ve already won. Over 25 million people have access through their benefits, subject to clinical eligibility. Omada had roughly 1.13 million enrolled members who were billed at least once over the last twelve months.

That’s only 4.5%.

So how could that number get bigger?

They are investing in targeted outreach and AI to get more of the people who qualify for care to actually sign up. GLP-1 programs and selling other programs to existing customers could drive enrollment, too. People are also staying in their programs longer (average member tenure was up 10% in Q3).

As an example lets take that 4.5% to 5% on a fixed base of 25 million people and that’s roughly 125,000 additional members. At today’s $294 per member, I get about $37 million in additional annualized revenue, assuming similar engagement, program mix and a full year of billing.

This is also where AI could help on the expense side. Members can log a meal and get nutritional feedback from AI, potentially freeing up some of their coaches’ time. Management says AI and automation are already helping care teams support more members per person.

Over the past year, trailing revenue per member rose from $280 to $294 and the adjusted cost to serve that member fell from $94 to $81. That’s an additional $27 in adjusted gross profit per member.

Going back to the moat question I had earlier. I could probably build a basic version of the app pretty quickly. But then imagine trying to get a large insurance company to cover my program. They’d want evidence that I can improve the health of their members and reduce costs. I’d need clinical teams that can actually deliver that care, and the systems to bill the insurer. That’s where the defensibility lies.

Omada has over 2,000 customers and averaged over 90% customer retention over the three years through 2025. I give those relationships and their ability to deliver care at scale a lot more weight than the software of course.

The numbers

At my entry price of $11.87 we’re looking at a market cap of roughly $736 million. Subtract the $243 million in cash (zero debt) and we’re effectively paying about $493 million for the business. Allow for dilution using the latest quarterly diluted share count as a rough proxy and that’s closer to $554 million.

Omada has only been public since June 2025, but look at where it’s traded on a trailing EV/revenue basis at each quarter end, using the financials available at the time.

  • September 2025 at 5.1×
  • December 2025 at 3.1×
  • March 2026 at 2.0×
  • June 2026 at 3.8×
  • September 2026 at 3.4×
  • Today at 1.5× (November 20).

The March comparison is particularly interesting to me. The stock closed at $12.57 on March 31. We’re within a dollar of that price today, with trailing revenue up 28% since then and two consecutive quarters of GAAP profitability under their belt.

This might not really mean anything when it comes to trying to predict where it could trade in the future. I just like to see this when I am analyzing a company. Of course, the multiple the market will pay in the future depends on how the business evolves.

Here’s how I’m thinking about the next year.

  • 2026 guidance (midpoint) of $345 million in revenue (+33%) and $33 million in adjusted EBITDA (about a 10% margin).
  • My 2027 estimates of $414 million in revenue (+20%) and $62 million in adjusted EBITDA (a 15% margin).

So even allowing for dilution, I’m paying roughly 1.3× my estimate for next year’s revenue and 9× my estimate for adjusted EBITDA.

The jump in margins is a big assumption. Q3 came in around 14% on an adjusted EBITDA basis, with some help from seasonality and hiring timing.

Using that $62 million of adjusted EBITDA, holding cash flat and assuming ~70 million diluted shares, here’s where the stock may trade over the next 12–24 months at different multiples.

  • 12× adjusted EBITDA gets me $14.10 (+19%).
  • 15× gets me $16.80 (+41%).
  • 18× gets me $19.40 (+64%).

The risks

Here’s where I could be wrong.

First there is the company-specific risk. Two Cigna affiliates accounted for 63% of Q3 revenue. A lot of employers sit behind those arrangements, but that gives Cigna considerable leverage. In April, Omada disclosed that several key agreements with Cigna and Evernorth were due to expire at the end of 2026. Some programs run longer and the contracts can be extended. So there is some uncertainty here.

The Q4 guide implies revenue down 4% sequentially and up about 16% year over year. Omada gets a lot of new members in Q1 when employers roll out new benefits. By Q4, new sign-ups and engagement usually slow during the holidays.

Last year was unusual because one of their partners launched a new GLP-1 offering in the second half, bringing in a lot of new members during what’s typically a slower time of year. That makes for a tougher comparison this year.

What concerns me more is that employers are taking longer to commit to 2027 benefits. Covering these drugs is expensive. In that same KFF survey, 61% of companies with 5,000+ employees that cover GLP-1s for weight loss said they had a significant impact on prescription drug spending.

Some employers have cut that benefit altogether. And when employers and insurers start looking for ways to save, other providers can come in with a cheaper program. Omada could lose members or have to accept lower prices.

It would also be nice to see more cash coming from the business. Free cash flow was just $2.5 million over the nine months.

If 2027 revenue growth slows to 10% and the margin comes in at 8%, a 12× adjusted EBITDA multiple gets me $8.70 a share, or 27% downside. A contract loss or further multiple compression could take it well below that.

Then like I’ve been saying for a few weeks, I’m still very cautious about this market, and that chart is ugly. A small-cap growth stock can get a whole lot cheaper during a selloff, regardless of where I think fair value is. A weaker economy could also hurt hiring and employer benefit budgets.

I could easily be early. I took an initial position today and want to leave myself room to add if the stock starts building a base.
