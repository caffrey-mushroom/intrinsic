SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion assessed how sustained jet fuel prices near $200–$225 per barrel could affect APAC airline profitability, capacity, fares, networks, and fleet deployment. The expert expects elevated prices through the first half of 2027, followed by possible easing, with roughly $159 per barrel becoming a longer-term baseline.

At $200–$225, industry profitability would largely disappear. Airlines would need to raise fares, reduce capacity, retire inefficient aircraft, protect premium routes, and potentially accept short-term losses. Full-service carriers appear better positioned than low- cost carriers because premium cabins, cargo, connecting traffic, and diversified networks provide greater pricing flexibility.

Key Insights

APAC carriers have little capacity to absorb further fuel inflation without operating at a loss. Airlines should preserve capacity discipline and restore seats only when demand supports profitable pricing. Full-service groups combining premium and low-cost operations have a structural advantage because they can allocate demand across brands and cabins. Low-cost carriers face the greatest risk because their customers are highly price sensitive and competitors may capture routes when capacity is withdrawn. Hub airlines should protect profitable trunk frequencies and Europe–Asia connectivity while cutting thin regional links and weak connecting flows. Domestic Japanese routes are especially vulnerable due to high-speed rail alternatives, aging demographics, and short-haul operating inefficiency. Governments and regulators were strongly criticized for maintaining taxes, slot requirements, and other rules that compound airline financial pressure. Maintenance should not be deferred. Cost reductions should instead emphasize efficient aircraft, engine durability improvements, scheduling, ground operations, baggage enforcement, and reduced product frills.
