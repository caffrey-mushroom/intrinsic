THE SIGNAL

  • Murphy said Inphi had about $700M annualized revenue when bought, looked expensive, and grew roughly 10x in five years on scale-out optics; he presents Lumentum as the same bet on scale-up optics (our read: helps justify the premium).- Murphy said the NVIDIA investments in Lumentum and Marvell were of similar size, implying Marvell’s is near Lumentum’s $2B; Lumentum’s centered on supply and Marvell’s on technology cooperation, so the combined company holds both NVIDIA ties.- Murphy tied scale-up demand to the copper wall inside the rack and said it holds regardless of supply swings, answering holders who fear the laser shortage ends before closing, which is more than 18 months away.- Hurlston singled out COGS synergies alongside revenue synergies, with Lumentum gross margin already at what he called a record 53% (our read: there may be further margin upside from integrated co-packaged optics that isn’t in standalone estimates).

01 Deal rationale and category creation

Murphy, as acquirer, describes the combination as merging Marvell’s leadership in AI data-center semiconductor connectivity with Lumentum’s leadership in optical technology, to form a new category of AI connectivity company. He points to the industry shift of optics moving further inside the data center as GPU clusters scale from hundreds to thousands of units, requiring semiconductors and optics to work seamlessly in what he calls the scale-up era, distinct from prior scale-out architectures.

02 Lumentum’s standalone strength and the sale decision

Hurlston says Lumentum’s just-reported quarter showed some of its best numbers ever, with gross margins of 53%, which he called the best Lumentum has seen. Asked by Jim Cramer why sell now given that trajectory, Hurlston says the timing reflects a now-or-never convergence between semiconductors and optics, pointing to Murphy’s own past instinct that led him to the earlier Inphi acquisition, and stating Lumentum must move first and act decisively or risk being left behind.

03 Commodity concerns and differentiation defense

David Faber relays shareholder worry that Lumentum’s key products are a commodity currently in short supply rather than durably differentiated technology. Murphy pushes back directly, calling the indium phosphide laser technology highly differentiated and comparing it to the esoteric bipolar and silicon-germanium process technologies he worked with for twenty-two years at Maxim. He argues today’s supply shortage reflects exploding demand across the whole optical and semiconductor industry, not fragility, and says cross-checking Lumentum’s and Marvell’s shared customer designs gave him added confidence in Lumentum’s growth durability.

04 Regulatory timeline and China risk

Faber presses on the extended timeline — deal closing not expected until first half of 2028 — and on China regulatory approval risk specifically. Murphy cites Marvell’s track record: the Cavium deal, announced in 2017, cleared regulatory review in seven to eight months, and the Inphi deal closed in about five months, both despite rising US-China tensions at the time. He calls this a very clean deal with no business overlap, says the longer stated timeline simply builds in ample buffer, and says he is very confident on China approval.

05 Stock reaction and shareholder value

Cramer notes Lumentum shares are down and asks Hurlston whether shareholders are losers long term. Hurlston responds that the company is not reacting to short-term share price moves since the deal rumors emerged, and cites expected revenue synergies and cost synergies as the basis for confidence in value creation over a three- to five-year horizon under Marvell’s leadership.

06 NVIDIA’s role and co-packaged optics roadmap

Cramer raises that both CEOs recently struck separate NVIDIA investment deals — Lumentum’s being a $2 billion convertible preferred, Marvell’s being more of a strategic technology-cooperation arrangement — and asks whether this marks the end of NVIDIA’s so-called circular deals. Murphy frames the Marvell-Lumentum combination as reinforced support for NVIDIA as ecosystem leader, saying NVIDIA received the news well. Hurlston then details the product vision: shifting toward co-packaged and near-packaged optics that combine Marvell’s semiconductor technology with Lumentum’s optical technology into a single integrated solution for hyperscaler customers, rather than each company selling discrete components — a strategy he terms extreme co design, intended to speed time-to-market and deepen differentiation.

01 Anthropic’s S-1: growth, risk, and chip diversity

Bajarin and Goldberg both read Anthropic’s full S-1 and flagged its heavy use of existential-risk language alongside strong growth metrics. Goldberg highlighted the company’s declared belief in scaling laws as the primary engine of AI progress, framing it as good news for semiconductor suppliers betting on ever-larger models. Both noted Anthropic is, in Bajarin’s words, the fastest-growing enterprise software company in history by revenue, still early in enterprise AI budget penetration — a point Anthropic itself emphasized via Fortune 500 customer data showing AI spend as a small share of enterprise budgets today. Goldberg pointed out Anthropic disclosed that 90% of its own code contributions are now written by Claude, illustrating internal dogfooding.

On infrastructure, Goldberg detailed Anthropic’s shift toward owning/controlling data centers via Anthropic Direct, moving away from pure reliance on cloud-rented compute (previously mostly through Amazon). He compared how the filing describes Anthropic’s chip relationships with NVIDIA, AWS Trainium, Google TPU and AMD, finding notably different contractual language for each: NVIDIA and AMD get described collaboration on design and engineering, Trainium gets an “opportunity to influence” future generations, while Google’s relationship was described with no comparable collaborative language. Bajarin connected this to his own thesis of workload fungibility across diverse silicon, comparing it to Meta’s vertical compute strategy discussed in Meta’s Prometheus blog with NVIDIA.

02 Enterprise adoption and ROI survey

Bajarin presented findings from his newly published second round of the EA index CIO/CTO survey. Most of the 200-plus CIOs surveyed aren’t tracking AI ROI at all; among the 33 who are, 67% saw ROI by the survey’s measure, and he says that ROI grew as AI usage scaled from one business unit to several. Bajarin also noted some business units are funding AI spend outside official IT budgets — a form of shadow AI becoming official — which he called a high-signal but hard-to-track phenomenon. Both hosts agreed the adoption curve likely inflects suddenly rather than gradually once enterprises cross an education and skills threshold.

03 Anthropic’s governance and capital structure

Goldberg spent considerable time on Anthropic’s five (soon four) classes of stock with differentiated voting rights, designed — in his reading — as a direct reaction to the 2023 OpenAI board turmoil, giving the seven co-founders effective control. He flagged the company’s ethics-oversight board structure and the S-1’s repeated warnings that shareholders’ pecuniary interest is not the company’s top priority. Goldberg also noted, as speculation, that Anthropic was founded in 2021 — two years before the OpenAI leadership clash — suggesting the founders may have planned a competing lab in advance.

On capital commitments, Goldberg said Anthropic was actually profitable and cash-flow generative in Q2, but likely received below-market, subsidized compute pricing (he estimates discounts around 50% versus his own cost models) from partners like Amazon, given Anthropic’s scale as AWS’s largest AI customer. Both hosts debated whether such discounts are sustainable or an early-stage subsidy, comparing it to Uber’s early pricing, that fades once labs reach steady-state profitability around 2029-2030.

04 Hyperscaler earnings and the flywheel question

The hosts covered a week of big capital raises — Meta’s equity offering, Amazon’s debt offering, and Oracle’s analyst day, where Oracle said it would not need further capital raises. Earnings reactions were mixed: Meta’s results were seen as fine, helped by engagement from its AI agent Muse; Google’s stock fell on growth concerns despite search holding up; Amazon’s numbers drew similar skepticism. Both hosts framed the core question as whether AI infrastructure investment is lifting the rest of each hyperscaler’s business — a flywheel test. Goldberg argued Google’s founders are true AI believers willing to keep spending, and that consumer ad-search risk is navigable by improving search quality; he and Bajarin agreed Amazon and Microsoft face more enterprise-dependency risk since they rely on Anthropic and OpenAI models rather than owning frontier capability themselves.

05 Intel’s repeated guidance stumble

The hosts described Intel’s pattern as a repeat of the same setup two years ago: a strong Q3 followed by weak Q4 guidance, with management citing substrate constraints and insufficient server-chip capacity after over-allocating to a weak PC market. Bajarin’s PC shipment model now tracks a negative-14% annual decline, the worst since 2022. Both hosts said this reflects a planning and execution failure — flagging confusion on the earnings call over which products would run on Intel 10, Intel 3, or 18A — rather than an existential survival question, since foundry yield and EMIB demand data looked encouraging. Bajarin noted possible upside if, as he has heard rumored, the government requires chips for military use to come from US companies on US soil, but said the recovery narrative has again been pushed out, now toward 2028.

06 Marvell-Lumentum and optical consolidation debate

The hosts discussed a reported deal (not confirmed) in which Marvell is reportedly in advanced, potentially all-stock talks to acquire Lumentum. Goldberg framed this as consistent with Marvell’s repositioning as a networking company following its Cavium and Inphi acquisitions, and said all-stock is the only way Marvell could afford it; Bajarin asked why Lumentum would agree. Bajarin offered a theory that Lumentum and Coherent may fear disruption and commoditization in optical components if Broadcom’s in-house lasers prove as good as an analyst’s tweet he cites suggests, pushing networking players toward vertical integration (citing Credo as a model). Goldberg pushed back that this is a bearish read on the optical industry’s long-term differentiation, while agreeing NVIDIA is unlikely to be able to make its own bid for Lumentum; Goldberg cites a Reuters report that the DOJ is still investigating NVIDIA on antitrust and notes NVIDIA owns a stake in Marvell.

01 Reading the S-1 as real data

Andrew Sharp opens by noting this is a rare chance to see actual numbers from a frontier AI lab, after two years of speculation and risk-factor satire, including viral parody tweets mocking Anthropic’s own doom-laden disclosures. Asked whether Anthropic’s IPO marks the equivalent peak for the AI trade that Coinbase’s IPO marked for crypto, Ben Thompson says a bubble and eventual pullback are inevitable, citing prior discussion of credit mismatches among neoclouds, but declines to call timing. He separates that macro question from what the S-1 itself reveals about the underlying business, which he says looks amazing — the Claude subscription is profitable (if less so than the API), and users develop workflow lock-in.

02 Training costs as the core risk

Thompson’s central argument is that Anthropic’s cost structure is dangerous not because of inference but because of training spend, which rose faster than cost-to-serve even as usage exploded in H1 2026. He frames this as inherently uncontrollable: the need to build better models is dictated by competition with OpenAI and others, meaning the pace of spending is out of Anthropic’s hands rather than a one-time cost that gets absorbed and then stabilizes. He draws an ironic parallel to Anthropic’s safety rhetoric about pacing the frontier, suggesting it aligns conveniently with the company’s commercial survival needs.

03 Unpacking the $80 billion loss and Amazon’s role

Thompson walks through how Amazon’s early investment — delivered partly as a loan Anthropic used to buy Trainium compute — gave Amazon the option to convert debt into equity. Every time Anthropic raises a new round, that conversion is repriced, and the increased liability shows up as a booked loss for Anthropic even though no cash changes hands; the mirror-image gain lands on Amazon’s own income statement as an investment markup. He expects this dynamic to recur across hyperscaler earnings broadly (citing Google’s SpaceX stake as a similar complication), making headline net income less informative than operating income going forward.

04 Revenue recognition through the clouds

Thompson details how roughly half of Anthropic’s revenue is billed through AWS, Azure and Google, and that Anthropic records the full gross amount — e.g., $100 — as revenue even though a substantial share goes back to the platform. That cut is booked as a sales and marketing expense rather than cost of revenue, which Thompson argues inflates both headline revenue and the reported margins; counting only Anthropic’s share as revenue, he says, would mean lower revenue and compressed margins, since the costs are unchanged, and failing that he thinks the platform cut belongs in cost of revenue.

05 Infrastructure flexibility and enterprise advantage

Both hosts link Anthropic’s chip-agnostic build (NVIDIA, Trainium, TPUs, and a deal with AMD) and its presence across all three major clouds to competitive advantage, particularly in enterprise sales — Sharp asks whether being on AWS specifically helps win customers already storing data there. Thompson says OpenAI, by contrast, was stuck on Microsoft, and argues Anthropic’s multi-cloud posture is underrated as an explanation for its enterprise traction.

06 Dilution as the funding endgame

Responding to a tweet Sharp cites tracing hyperscaler funding from free cash flow to debt to equity dilution, Thompson agrees dilution is the last stage, noting it’s arguably healthiest because new shareholders take on the upside risk rather than the company retaining debt obligations. He flags built-in dilution mechanics in Anthropic’s structure — multiple outstanding share-conversion rights to Amazon, Google and Broadcom, plus, as he reads the S-1, a right to issue additional equity to its benefit corporation if the stock rises about 50%. Sharp raises the counterpoint that today’s IPO retail buyers effectively fund further dilution risk, while Thompson notes earlier private investors already captured Amazon-scale upside; Sharp considers broader public disclosure preferable to Anthropic staying private indefinitely.

07 What the stale H1 numbers can’t tell us

Because the S-1 data ended June 30, 2026 when they recorded (before Anthropic’s November 2 amended filing), both hosts flag unresolved questions: anecdotal spend-tracking data (cited via Ramp) suggested growth decelerated in H2 amid OpenAI’s enterprise push, but the release of Opus 5.5 complicates that narrative. Thompson also speculates that H1’s strong margins may partly reflect that Anthropic didn’t have enough compute capacity at the time, causing outages, meaning frustrated users inadvertently helped make pre-IPO numbers look better than the underlying cost structure might otherwise show once compute scales up.

  • Shtan says 95% of Nebius customers run modified or fine-tuned open models, not stock weights, which underpins its bet on selective pruning and compression for narrow-domain workloads, a value-add beyond raw GPU rental (our read).- Prefill/decode disaggregation is widely discussed but rarely done well, per Shtan; Nebius runs hand-managed disaggregated setups only for top customers, and fast rack-scale Blackwell interconnect removes much of the need, so it rents those racks only whole.- Shtan says long-running agents can go minutes without calling an LLM because the work runs as code and tools, lifting CPU demand amid a current CPU shortage (our read: bullish general-purpose compute).- Shtan says AWS list GPU prices mislead: on large deals hyperscalers price very competitively and win big contracts, so their limited share reflects chip constraints, not weak pricing.

01 Nebius’s origin and positioning

Nebius emerged from Yandex, formally separating and rebranding during 2024 with no remaining ties to Russian assets, according to Shtan. Its hardware team, inherited from Yandex, brought experience operating infrastructure at genuine hyperscale (hundreds of thousands to millions of servers), but the software platform was rewritten from scratch, and by the end of 2024 a significant portion of the software engineering team had been hired from outside Yandex. Shtan says Nebius deliberately built a software-first company — roughly 200 software engineers versus 40-50 on hardware procurement in 2024 — rejecting the “Neo Cloud” label entirely, positioning itself instead as the AI Cloud competing directly against AWS and Google Cloud despite having, by his estimate, about 1% of AWS’s revenue.

02 Why hyperscalers haven’t won

Shtan’s central argument is that AWS, Google, and Microsoft are not losing this market by choice but by chip-allocation constraint: if NVIDIA could supply far more chips, the hyperscalers would already dominate both internal AI workloads and external rental. Because chips are scarce, these companies must constantly triage between internal research/product needs and external capacity, and the rental opportunity — a relatively small business for them, in Shtan’s telling — remains too small relative to their established core businesses to justify the infrastructure overhaul (networking, storage throughput) needed to serve it at scale. He predicts that as chip shortages ease over the next several years, hyperscalers will redesign internals and apply their sales power to compete harder, and frames Nebius’s bet as needing to scale fast enough to compete with them on equal footing by then.

03 Inference economics and customer segmentation

Shtan describes two distinct customer archetypes: new AI labs with billion-dollar compute contracts who care only about GPU performance, availability, and cost (indifferent to data-center location), versus enterprise customers with smaller contracts but heavy compliance, security, and certification requirements. On technical differentiation, he says Nebius builds one universal cloud platform rather than customizing clusters per workload type, and resolves latency/cost tradeoffs through SLA engineering at the orchestration and model layers rather than hardware. He states that inference remains unsolved industry-wide, citing deep untapped potential in model-weight optimization, selective pruning, and kernel-level tuning — Nebius made an acquisition earlier in the year specifically for model-compression and speculative-decoding talent, roughly doubling that team’s size.

04 Build-out bottlenecks and data center model

Asked to rank AI infrastructure bottlenecks (power, chips, labor, cooling, networking, HBM), Shtan says all of them are real simultaneously — including a current CPU, NVMe, and power-transformer shortage with multi-year lead times — but insists the singular bottleneck and moat is execution: sourcing flexibly, reallocating resources between facilities, and navigating constant tradeoffs. On infrastructure, he says Nebius fully controls everything inside its data centers regardless of ownership model, including a newly announced asset-light model in which a third party funds a facility that Nebius still builds and operates to its own specification.

05 Products, acquisitions, and market structure

Shtan discusses the Tavily web-search acquisition as a complementary low-level infrastructure play feeding into agentic tooling (alongside sandboxes Nebius is building), and frames OpenAI’s partnership with Baseten as a move to offer cheaper open-model fallback while keeping users inside OpenAI’s ecosystem for upselling. Looking five-to-ten years out, he expects today’s roughly 300-to-500 neo-cloud players to partially consolidate and die off but believes a long tail of niche independent providers (analogous to DigitalOcean, MongoDB, Snowflake, and ClickHouse persisting alongside AWS) will endure even as large AI labs expand their own full-stack offerings.

06 Personal reflections on AI tooling and strategy

Shtan says the biggest thing he changed his mind on this year is software engineering itself — he describes AI coding tools going from nearly worthless a year ago to now a dependable part of daily work. He describes Nebius’s policy of placing no limits on employee AI token usage while requiring every output remain the engineer’s personal responsibility, with fully autonomous agents barred from touching core services. His biggest self-identified mistake was underestimating client scale — a single customer can now need more than the 30-megawatt facility that once counted as a major data center, with some clients pulling hundreds of GB a second from one shared file system. Looking ahead, he says he’s stopped tracking raw model size/capability gains and is now most focused on agent-level outcome optimization — improving reliability of long-running, multi-turn agentic workflows independent of the underlying model.

01 Career and launch rationale

Terzian spent over a decade on the buyside, starting in private equity, then single-manager funds (including an activist campaign at Black and White Capital that pushed Etsy to move to the cloud and cut internal R&D “science projects” like its own email system, after which he says margins exploded), before joining Balyasny’s Anthem PM training program. He is now launching his own fund structured as market-neutral with above 80% idiosyncratic risk and tightly limited net market exposure, explicitly designed to hold ideas 6-18 months rather than the 2-4 week holding periods he says multi-manager “pod” risk models effectively force. He says this duration constraint at multi-managers prevents monetizing the biggest fundamental theses, which is his stated reason for leaving.

02 The software precedent

Terzian’s framework for agentic disruption is modeled directly on what he watched happen in software: ChatGPT’s late-2022 launch led to coding agents (2024) and “vibe coding” (2025), and investors realized SaaS per-seat pricing models were structurally impaired as seat counts shrink. He says six companies in his 37-name coverage group are down over 50% despite revenue estimates moving only slightly, illustrating that multiple compression preceded any visible hit to fundamentals — a pattern he expects to repeat in internet stocks. He is not calling software “bottomed” yet, though he says equity prices have bounced off lows; he distinguishes infrastructure/”picks and shovels” names like Twilio and Snowflake (already priced like winners) from application software, where he singles out Workday as relatively protected due to low churn and limited per-seat exposure, while noting (without endorsing as fact) market chatter of a possible buyout that could value shares well above current levels.

03 Agentic winners

Terzian names Meta, Google, Apple, Shopify, Twilio, and Etsy as winners. Meta and Google benefit from owning consumer data and advertising rails; he argues Meta’s real upside isn’t direct Muse monetization (sell-side estimates range 2-9% of Meta revenue, which he calls “almost irrelevant”) but a surge in advertiser ROAS as agentic intent data flows back into Facebook’s ad ecosystem. He expects Apple to launch a real consumer agent within 12 months, arguing the company largely ignored AI without cost until now, but the App Store threat forces its hand. Shopify he calls a near-term winner because it represents the open-internet long-tail of sellers that agents like Muse can surface, especially where Amazon blocks access. Twilio benefits because agents currently use traditional texts/calls to transact — though he flags a 3-5 year risk that direct agent-to-agent communication (via APIs/MCPs) could cut Twilio out, calling it a near-term rather than durable winner; he says Q3 earnings is the place to look for early agentic evidence. Etsy’s moat, in his view, is that over half its sellers have no other online storefront, making it un-substitutable the way Pinterest is not.

04 Agentic losers

Terzian’s loser list centers on Pinterest, DoorDash, Expedia, and Booking.com. Pinterest, he argues, mostly aggregates content pinned from elsewhere, so an agent with superior personal context (via Meta/Facebook data) can replicate its discovery function without needing Pinterest itself; he forecasts a double hit of declining users and declining revenue-per-user. DoorDash’s blended take rate (about 13%, he estimates) could fall toward the roughly 4-6% he triangulates for delivery-only if agents reduce it to “dumb delivery rails.” Expedia and Booking are “clear losers” among OTAs because they’re disintermediated layers with little unique inventory; he views Airbnb as less exposed since some listings are unique, though less protected than Etsy. He also cites a Morgan Stanley note on cruise lines, where roughly 60% of bookings still go through human travel agents — a consultative sale Terzian thinks agentic AI could capture, potentially raising take rates for brands like cruise lines, hotels, and airlines rather than OTAs.

05 Portfolio construction and risk discipline

Terzian describes running the fund via “trade units” — a core long or short idiosyncratic idea paired with single-name hedges rather than sector baskets or ETFs — to isolate alpha risk rather than take unintended factor bets (he cites a past experience being inadvertently short housing and rates via a real-estate short). He avoids heavily shorted names, defining that threshold as roughly 15% of float after backing out convertible bonds, arguing such names are often more consensus than they appear and vulnerable to short squeezes without enough duration to withstand them. He also addresses Meta’s capital-intensive build-out of AI infrastructure, arguing the company has earned the right to spend aggressively given demonstrated ROI, and that the stakes of winner-take-all agent dominance justify extremely heavy spending (Terzian discloses that his fund is long and short some of the names discussed; host Doug Garber says he owns Meta, Google and Apple).
