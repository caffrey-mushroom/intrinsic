原标题：The AI Opportunity and the Cost of Getting There

#ai-demand

Substack · 2026-10-18

Let me start off by saying that I think we’re still very, very early in AI adoption.

More businesses are paying for it, and we’re starting to see what happens when they build it into work they do every day. Getting from a subscription to that point takes time. The companies supplying the computing, software and infrastructure have years of opportunity ahead if they can turn that growing use into a good return on the money they’re investing.

While I truly believe we are in the early stages of AI usage, as an investor I need to separate that from the returns I expect to receive from these companies.

I raised some cash early this week after trimming down some AI exposure and have started putting some of it back to work gradually. Back into AI yes but also diversifying my portfolio further into companies that are not dependent on AI spend and adding some hedges. More on that later.

Iran and the disruption to energy supply remain the main macro pressure in my view. Within AI, the cost of financing the buildout is making me more selective about what levels I want to add at and where I want my exposure to live. This article you are reading will lay the foudnation for my current thoughts on the state of the market. Later today I will have my portfolio release and weekly update that will get into the individual positions.

Why this reminds me of July

When prices fall and volatility rises, a leveraged fund has less room to carry the same portfolio. It may sell one position, then another, then another. Regardless of fundamentals or growth. Enough funds doing that together can push prices much further than the company strength would suggest, which looks like part of what we’re seeing across crowded AI positions.

Reuters reported six consecutive sessions of AI-stock selling in Goldman’s prime-brokerage data. Sales of owned stocks exceeded the buying back of short positions as funds shrank their exposure. Reading that took me back to Goldman’s July discussion of semiconductor allocations falling from their June peak. Volatility and risk management pushed investors to reduce positions then too, with selling spreading across hyperscalers, semis and infrastructure suppliers. Strong operating results can become temporarily less important to a fund that needs a smaller portfolio. (For the July comparison, read this.)

Once that pressure eases, prices can recover before the whole macro picture improves. July also gave us a reminder of how hard that is to time, because selling continued after the first signs of a reset. I’m buying in stages so I have room if that happens again, while keeping exposure to a recovery.

In the Schwab chart through October 15, the equal-weight S&P 500 appears as candles and the semiconductor index in purple. They use separate axes, so direction is the useful comparison.

Semis have taken much heavier pressure than the equal-weight index. If you own the kinds of companies we discuss here, that goes a long way toward explaining why the market can feel worse than the broad index suggests. It’s also why I’m looking at businesses with different earnings drivers alongside the additions within AI. It’s interesting to see all of the different dynamics on X. You have investors that aren’t feeling this selloff at all, but it’s probably because they’ve been positioned in companies that have been underperforming. Whereas investors that have heavily been positioned in AI, you are really feeling this selloff, although you are probably sitting on some great gains if you’ve been around since earlier in the year.

Oil

A household spending more on fuel has less left for everything else. A business paying more to move goods and run its operations has to pass along those costs or accept lower margins. Eventually some trips, orders and investments become too expensive to justify. That’s how we can end up with weaker activity and uncomfortable inflation at the same time.

Gasoline contributed more than half of September’s 0.6% monthly CPI increase. Over twelve months, headline inflation was 3.7% and inflation excluding food and energy was 2.6%, the two measures in the BLS chart below.

NY Fed president John Williams has described pressure from both the Middle East disruption and demand for AI inputs. Expensive fuel makes the path back to lower inflation harder, and for a tech business whose valuation depends heavily on future profits, that uncertainty can bring down what investors are willing to pay today.

Two Singapore-registered vessels were struck by unknown projectiles on October 13 and 14, according to Singapore’s maritime authority. Deliveries can still be interrupted while negotiations are under way. Reuters also reported that senior Iranian officials described stepping up attacks on shipping to sustain high oil prices and influence the US vote. That is their account of the strategy, and it makes the period around the midterms particularly important for the energy outlook.

Trump predicted in September that the war would end immediately after the election. In his TIME interview, he also left open heavier bombing afterward, and on October 8 he ruled out an attack before the November 3 vote. Taken together, those comments leave room for a material change in either direction around the election. I wouldn’t build a position around a reliable end date for the pressure of course, but its always on my mind. (For his fuller comments, read this.)

The IEA’s October report forecasts global oil demand falling 2.6 million barrels a day in 2026 compared with 2025 as restricted availability, high prices and weaker economic conditions reduce consumption. Supply losses are larger still, leaving a shortage. Middle Eastern product exports were 1.9 million barrels a day in September against 4.7 million before the war. The IEA expects global refinery runs to recover from October’s seasonal low into year-end, which would help turn available crude into diesel and other fuels, provided those products can get to the people who need them. (The IEA report is worth a look, read this.)

In the inventory chart, orange is 2026 and the observations run through July. OECD gasoil and diesel stocks were around 565 million barrels then, leaving a smaller buffer against another disruption.

IEA members agreed on October 15 to accelerate about 100 million barrels remaining from their existing commitments, including plans to release more than 20 million barrels of diesel as soon as possible. Those barrels help when they reach the market, buying time for production, refining and transport to recover.

That’s the kind of improvement I’d like to see behind lower prices. Restored supply reduces business costs and frees household spending for other things, helping earnings while improving the inflation outlook. A further collapse in demand would leave us with a much weaker recovery case. My willingness to own the rebound rests on companies retaining customers through this period and benefiting as the supply pressure eases.

Earnings

TSMC just reported $46.72 billion of Q3 revenue and guided to $52.3 billion to $54.3 billion for Q4. The midpoint is about 14% sequential growth, on top of the substantial orders already reaching manufacturing.

On the earnings call, management described rising AI demand from agents and greater adoption of open models. They expect those applications to create more demand for advanced chips. Customers are committing money to capacity they expect to use or sell, and TSMC is guiding to another substantial increase next quarter. That deserves a lot of weight in how we think about the selling, and it’s part of why I intend to slowly be adding to AI infrastructure. (For management’s explanation, read this.)

A falling share price can mean the market expects weaker profits, is willing to pay less for those profits, or both. If earnings keep growing while the multiple contracts, the business becomes cheaper relative to its earning power. If orders and margins deteriorate as well, the lower price may offer much less than it first appears to.

The Nomura contribution chart shared by The Kobeissi Letter shows ten stocks supplying 70% of the S&P 500’s gain from March 30, with the remaining 490 supplying 30%.

Kobeissi also calculated that 59% of constituents were at least 20% below their own all-time highs, measured from each stock’s individual peak. A few large businesses can carry the index while many constituents struggle, so the index alone tells us surprisingly little about the experience of owning a broader portfolio.

There’s more earnings growth underneath it than you might expect from those prices. FactSet’s August report put Q2 earnings growth for the 493 companies outside the Magnificent Seven at 31.8%. The index’s 52% headline included large investment gains at Alphabet and Amazon. For Q3, the October 16 update put blended earnings growth at 29.0%, although only 9% of companies had reported. The early aggregate earnings beat was 2.6%, below its five-year average of 7.5%, and forward P/E stood at 19.0. (You can check this out for the latest earnings snapshot used here.)

That’s enough growth to make an eventual recovery interesting, with a lot still to learn from the coming results. Easing energy costs could improve profits at the same time that lower uncertainty makes investors more willing to pay for them. I’d like to own some of that opportunity before the outlook feels comfortable again.

Who earns as AI gets cheaper

Open-weight models give businesses more choice because the model files are available for others to run, subject to the license. A customer can hire a provider to host one or operate it themselves. They can change both the model and the company supplying the computing behind it.

Vercel’s AI Gateway routes applications’ requests to different model providers. In its October report, open-weight models processed 71.5% of September tokens while representing 23% of estimated spending, valued at published prices.

A cheaper model can take on a large volume of routine work while premium models earn more from difficult tasks. Within that same gateway, frontier-model spending grew tenfold from December. Customers are finding uses for both and choosing a different tool as the task changes. (Vercel’s production report is useful here, read this.)

Coinbase’s fraud work gives us a more concrete example. Its initial experiment used Claude Opus alongside existing controls and produced 30% fewer fraudulent transactions and a 22% reduction in fraud value in an online test. After learning more about the job, Coinbase adapted a smaller open-weight model using its own data. The later Qwen work reported better performance than Opus 4.5 on a private benchmark and 55% lower median production latency.

The chart compares models on Coinbase’s own task, while the earlier live fraud reduction came from the Opus experiment. What interests me is the progression from proving AI could help to improving the cost and speed of delivering it. A customer can become much more committed to using AI as some of its work moves away from a premium lab, and it can continue renting cloud GPUs throughout that process. (For Coinbase’s account of the later work, check this out.)

Intercom sells AI customer-support software. Its agent handles customer questions and support requests, and Intercom charges $0.99 per billable outcome, including resolutions and certain completed procedures. If a cheaper model can deliver that outcome reliably, the application owner has room to improve margins, invest in the product or compete on price. The customer relationship and the ability to complete useful work can become more valuable as the underlying models become easier to substitute.

Vercel estimated September spending at about 13 times December’s level while average token prices fell 47%. The median paying customer active in both months more than doubled spending over those nine months. That continuing-customer result is particularly encouraging because it shows businesses expanding their use after they were already paying.

Among businesses using Ramp’s token-billing tools, spending was roughly flat in August and September while usage increased. Ramp had described price cuts and businesses choosing cheaper models as their default. A team can use AI more often and still spend about the same by reserving the expensive models for harder jobs. That is a sensible decision for the customer, and it makes revenue growth more demanding for the model provider. (For Ramp’s explanation, read this.)

I see that pause as a reason to be more measured about how quickly usage turns into revenue. Customers are learning how to get more out of their budgets. The longer Vercel comparison shows that existing customers can still increase spending substantially through falling prices, while Ramp gives us a more cautious picture of the last two months. For infrastructure investors, the pace of that spending growth determines how much expansion customers can sustain in the long run.

Early October brought a new weekly spending high in Ramp’s data, 1.5% above the previous August peak. That is a modest improvement. I want to see it develop into a sustained increase as more work moves into production. My longer-term view is that lower costs make useful AI affordable for more tasks, and that recurring work can eventually expand the budget. Building those applications and getting people to rely on them takes time. (For the October update, check this out.)

Think about an agent following an investor’s portfolio. It could read new filings, earnings calls and company announcements, compare guidance with last quarter, flag changes in cash flow or debt maturities, and update the research for the investor to review. Lower costs could make that affordable across more companies and with more frequent updates. If the service saves enough time or improves coverage, it can earn a recurring place in the customer’s budget. Multiply that kind of change across the work businesses do every day and you can see why I think we’re still early.

Buying a subscription is the beginning of that process. The larger opportunity develops as people reorganize recurring work around useful AI. How quickly customers reach that stage will matter more to long-term returns than any single month’s token count.

Hyperscalers can earn across those choices by hosting models, connecting applications to customer data and providing the security and software needed to run them. Amazon hosting Google’s open Gemma models in its European Sovereign Cloud is a good example of a customer being able to change models while keeping the cloud relationship. That flexibility is one reason I’ve been adding to the hyperscalers.

Anthropic

Anthropic’s quarterly revenue rose from $4.7 billion in Q1 to $11.6 billion in Q2 and $16.8 billion in Q3, according to Reuters. Lumina’s chart below shows that progression, including the 45% increase in the latest quarter. (For the original chart, check this out.)

Analysts who met executives estimated a $110 billion annualized revenue pace at year-end. That estimate describes a twelve-month run rate, separate from the revenue earned during 2026. Bloomberg also reported positive free cash flow, citing people familiar with the results, and The Information reported an 11% adjusted operating margin year to date through Q3, excluding stock compensation. Customer revenue is beginning to support a stronger financial case alongside the rapid growth.

Even so, Reuters reported investor discussions around a $1.5 trillion valuation for a November offering, compared with earlier expectations near $2 trillion. Some investors focused on Q3’s slower sequential growth after revenue more than doubled in Q2. You can have a tremendous quarter and still find that the price assumed an even faster trajectory. (For the reported valuation discussions, read this.)

For the suppliers building capacity around that growth, I’m more interested in how much fresh cash reaches Anthropic and when. The reported IPO target of up to $100 billion remains a financing ambition. We need the final offering structure to know how much cash becomes available for expansion. If Anthropic raises the same primary cash at a lower valuation, existing owners accept more dilution and the buildout can remain funded. Suppliers face more immediate consequences if proceeds are smaller, arrive later or come with terms that change deployment plans.

Reuters’s prospectus reporting put commitments at $518 billion across a decade, with about 80% non-cancelable or payable regardless of use.

Positive free cash flow helps, and the public offering disclosures should give us a better view of how customer cash generation and financing line up with payments as capacity comes online. I want to see the latest cash balance, net proceeds going to Anthropic, and payments due over the next year or two. Those figures would tell us much more about how comfortably the business can carry the commitments behind today’s supplier growth.

What older GPUs and expensive debt tell us

CoreWeave’s September presentation describes an A100 renewal through 2029, with a three-year commitment priced comparably to typical one-year terms. It also reports a three-year H200 renewal above the original price.

Customers are committing fresh money to older-generation equipment, extending the earning life of an investment already made. On the August earnings call, management described a typical five-year deployment repaying its equipment financing and generating additional cash during the initial contract. I see a lot of potential in the later rentals adding returns from that same infrastructure. Older equipment can remain attractive for the right workload and price, and as the original borrowing is paid down, more rental income can be available after operating costs. Continued renewals could make the lifetime return much better than the first contract alone suggests. (The renewal details are in the presentation, read this.)

CoreWeave also reported an approximately 25% price increase across its GPU offerings in July. Here is the original slide from its September presentation.

Nebius raised its published on-demand GPU prices from October 1. That gives us another provider reporting higher prices, alongside the commitments to keep renting older equipment.

I find that combination encouraging. CoreWeave is describing pricing and renewals in its own business, and Nebius is raising its advertised on-demand rates. Two large providers seeing room for higher prices gives me more confidence that useful computing capacity is still in demand.

To work out what shareholders receive, we also have to follow the borrowing. CoreWeave borrows against equipment and the customer payments attached to it, giving lenders claims on those assets and receipts. The company supplies the rest of the upfront money through customer advances, other cash or additional financing. At June 30, it directly owed or guaranteed $31.4 billion of its $35.1 billion recorded debt balance. Those guarantees mean the wider company has to cover an obligation if the borrowing subsidiary cannot, so much of the exposure reaches beyond an individual project.

In the latest quarter, CoreWeave reported $2.575 billion of revenue, a $49 million operating loss and $640 million of net interest expense. Demand can be strong and still leave shareholders waiting for a return once equipment, electricity, facilities and financing have been paid for.

The Axios chart using Tradeweb data shows CoreWeave’s July 2032 bond yield above 15% on October 14.

Investors in that secondary market are asking for more compensation for the risk. Existing fixed-rate coupons stay the same, but additional borrowing becomes more consequential. CoreWeave did complete $4.2 billion of convertible financing in September, demonstrating access to capital before this latest pressure. The terms on the next round will matter to how much future income reaches shareholders. (For the bond-market picture, check this out.)

Lower Treasury yields can help with the underlying rate a company pays, although lenders also demand an extra return for that company’s credit risk. If that extra cost rises enough, it can outweigh the Treasury move.

Higher interest takes more of the future income, new shares spread ownership more widely, and construction delays postpone receipts. A buyer acquiring distressed capacity cheaply could compete at lower rental prices while the original shareholders bear a loss. Useful equipment and growing industry demand can coexist with a poor return for the people who financed it.

Opportunities beyond AI

John Hancock’s sector returns through October 16 show staples and energy each gaining 5.0% for the week and healthcare gaining 3.4%, while the S&P 500 lost 1.9%. The original table gives weekly returns on the left and year-to-date returns on the right.

I want some earnings that depend on something beyond the next round of AI spending, and I want additions I’d still be happy to own when tech rebounds. A company winning customers or recovering its margins has a reason to perform beyond money rotating into its sector for a week.

One example is WMT. Walmart’s August results described market-share gains across income groups and 23% global eCommerce growth. Selling groceries and everyday necessities at good prices can attract customers as household budgets tighten, which is why Walmart interests me. Winning a larger share of that spending creates room to grow even when consumers are cautious.

At 22.5 times forward earnings in Yardeni’s October 16 chart, staples already have a price on that dependability.

I’m looking for company-specific progress that can justify what I pay. In healthcare, Yardeni’s consensus forecasts show earnings growth of 1.6% in 2026 and 22.5% in 2027, pointing to a much stronger year after the current weakness.

Established products, treatment demand and improving margins could support that recovery at individual companies. Clinical-stage biotech depends much more on trial outcomes and development, giving it a different role and risk in a portfolio. I’m interested in the earnings-recovery case where we can follow the products and margins already in place.

Energy’s strength is directly linked to the high oil prices hurting other businesses, so supply relief would change that advantage. Healthcare and businesses selling groceries and everyday necessities give me other earnings drivers to consider. I like being able to add businesses that can make progress through different economic outcomes.

The next reports should give us a clearer view of customer spending, project delivery and how much cash remains after the buildout. On energy, I’ll be watching whether promised supply releases reach the market and help ease fuel costs.
