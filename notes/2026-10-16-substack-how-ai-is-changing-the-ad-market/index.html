原标题：How AI Is Changing Advertising Market

#internet #media

Substack · 2026-10-16

Interview: September 28, 2026 · US Pacific Time · Approximately 54 minutes Interviewed and edited by Damnang · Damnang Research Expert: Meta advertising software engineer

When I look at Meta, I usually focus on how much computing capacity it needs to develop AI models and what that investment means for semiconductor demand. I had spent less time looking at what AI already does in Meta’s main business, advertising. I wanted to understand what better models change for advertisers and whether those improvements actually lead them to spend more.

Using Muse recently gave me more questions. Beyond handling email and organizing my schedule, it could open websites and do things I would otherwise have to do myself. That was what caught my attention. If people use services like this regularly, how might Meta’s existing business benefit? I wanted to explore the connection to advertising and the experiences that might become possible alongside products such as smart glasses.

The expert I spoke with is a software engineer at Meta who supports large advertisers. The role involves integrating capabilities developed by the AI team into advertising products and adapting them to advertisers’ objectives, rather than researching the models that predict how people will respond to ads.

Later in the conversation, we moved from AI in software development to Muse and smart glasses. Could a service I find useful in daily life also help Meta’s advertising business? What might change when smart glasses enter the picture? I wanted to hear the perspective of an engineer working in advertising.

01 Connecting Advertising AI to Advertiser Goals

Damnang: Could you start by introducing yourself? You work in advertising at Meta. What does your role involve?

Expert: I’m a software engineer working on advertising at Meta. Our team supports large advertisers. Even when companies use the same Meta advertising platform, what they want can be quite different. Some want to focus on photo ads, while others prefer video. They sell different products to different customers, so their requests can get quite complicated.

They also want different things from their advertising. Some campaigns aim to generate purchases right away. Others aim to get people to install an app or add a product to their cart. We develop and support the features that let advertisers use Meta’s capabilities for those goals.

Damnang: Does that mean you also build the algorithms that decide which ads to show to which users?

Expert: That part is a little different. A separate AI team builds the technology that predicts what a user will do after seeing an ad. My work is more about connecting the capabilities that team develops to the actual advertising products.

Damnang: Could you give an example of what that involves?

Expert: Let’s say a clothing retailer advertises a collection of fall products. There might be sweaters, pants, and various other items. There’s a feature that optimizes which product to show to each user. For this example, imagine it was originally available only for ads with a purchase objective.

The advertiser might want to use that feature in other campaigns too. This time, they want more people to add items to a cart or a wishlist, rather than make a purchase. Someone then needs to make that same optimization feature work for those objectives. That’s closer to the kind of work I do.

02 How an Ad Slot Is Filled

Damnang: I’d like to start by understanding how Meta’s advertising actually works. Once an advertiser sets up an ad, how does the system decide which ad to show to which person?

Expert: Let’s use a bakery as an example. The owner wants to receive cake orders online and decides how much to spend on advertising. That gives the system an objective and a budget. In many cases, advertisers provide those two things and the system bids on their behalf, rather than the advertisers setting every bid themselves.

Then, when a user is browsing Facebook or Instagram and an ad slot becomes available, an auction takes place. It decides which ad to show in that particular slot. Someone next to you could be using the app at the same time and see a different ad.

First, the system quickly narrows a very large pool of ads down to candidates that might suit that user. Meta’s public explanation describes a stage that reduces tens of millions of ads to a few thousand. It then evaluates those candidates in more detail. If it shows this person the bakery’s ad, how likely are they to order a cake? It is predicting the likelihood that the person will take the action the advertiser wants.

The ads are evaluated that way, and the one selected in the auction appears on the screen. The user is simply scrolling, but all of this happens in that brief moment.

Damnang: If it’s an auction, does the advertiser willing to pay the most win? That sounds like it would be difficult for a small shop to compete with a large company.

Expert: Not necessarily. The bid isn’t the only thing the system considers. It looks at how valuable the result is to the advertiser, how likely the user is to take that action, and the quality of the ad.

Suppose a car dealer and a bakery both want to show an ad to the same user. One customer might be worth much more to the car dealer. But this person isn’t thinking about buying a car. They’re looking for a cake. They would be more likely to respond to the bakery’s ad. In that case, the bakery can win the slot.

Showing an ad to people who aren’t interested just because the advertiser is spending a lot doesn’t help the advertiser either. It’s also unpleasant for the user. Users need to see ads they might find relevant, and advertisers need those ads to bring in customers. Both sides need a reason to keep coming back, which is why relevance and quality also matter when the system chooses an ad.

Damnang: When does the advertiser get charged, then? When the ad is shown, or when an order actually comes in?

Expert: Many ads are charged based on impressions. Setting orders as the objective doesn’t mean the advertiser pays only when someone places an order.

The basis for charging and the result the system is trying to achieve are different things. The bakery wants orders, so when the system serves the ad, it looks for people who are likely to order. The advertiser then looks at how many orders came in relative to the money spent.

03 How Meta Understands User Behavior

Damnang: How does Meta judge whether that person will buy a cake? It might know what I’ve looked at on Instagram, but can it also know what I actually bought?

Expert: It looks at what the user has shown interest in within Meta’s services, and it also uses actions on the advertiser’s website as signals. It looks at a range of behaviors to predict which ads that person is likely to respond to.

For example, a property management company might want to advertise to homeowners. That doesn’t mean Meta directly knows whether someone owns a home. Think of it as assessing how likely someone is to respond based on relevant behavioral signals.

Two common ways to connect an advertiser’s website are the Meta pixel and the Conversions API, or CAPI. The pixel sends information about actions in the browser, while CAPI sends events from the advertiser’s server. When information comes in about whether someone viewed a product, added it to a cart, or placed an order, the system can learn from it.

Events sent from the server are important because browser side signals don’t always come through reliably. On the other hand, if the same order arrives through both routes, it needs to be handled so it isn’t counted twice. Knowing what happened after an ad was shown helps the system make better decisions about whom to show it to next.

Editor’s note: The pixel is code installed on a website, while CAPI provides a connection for sending events from sources such as servers. It does not automatically transmit everything on a website. The events and information sent depend on the implementation, settings, and applicable consent requirements and policies. CAPI is not a way to bypass privacy requirements. When the same event is sent through both routes, information such as the event name and identifier is used to remove duplicates. Hashed email addresses or phone numbers may be used to match events to accounts. Hashing transforms an original value according to a defined rule and is different from encryption. Hashed values can still be used for matching, so hashing alone does not make information fully anonymous.

Damnang: What happens if an order comes in but that information isn’t properly passed on to Meta?

Expert: It becomes harder to tell which ads produced good results. Even if an order was placed, the system can’t learn from that result if it doesn’t know about it. Having a reliable connection to send the results is just as important as setting the right objective.

Even with those connections in place, privacy settings or device restrictions can limit the signals that can be observed. In those cases, some results need to be estimated, and experiments are also needed to check whether those estimates are accurate. Meta does not have a complete record of everything a user does.

04 Small Performance Gains Can Change Advertiser Decisions

Damnang: If the system keeps learning from those results, I imagine the settings advertisers have to choose themselves have changed quite a bit. What has changed in practice?

Expert: In the past, advertisers spent more time guessing who their customers would be and setting detailed criteria. A bakery might choose people in a particular age group who lived in the area and were interested in baking.

But the customers the owner imagines and the people who actually buy can be different. Some people may personally love cake, but an office employee who orders cakes for team birthdays could be an even better customer. If the owner defines the audience too narrowly at the start, they could miss those people.

The system is now moving toward taking the desired outcome and budget, then finding more suitable people based on actual responses and orders. Of course, advertisers still need to set conditions such as reaching people in the local neighborhood. But within those boundaries, there’s less pressure on advertisers to work out exactly who will make a good customer.

As AI models improve, they can understand the images and text in ads better and consider more signals together. We’re moving from choosing an audience mainly on the advertiser’s assumptions toward finding people based on both the content of the ad and the responses it actually receives.

Damnang: When those improvements are only a few percent, the numbers can seem small. Do advertisers respond strongly even to differences of that size?

Expert: Advertising is quite sensitive to small differences. Large advertisers usually don’t advertise only on Meta. They split their budgets across platforms such as Google and TikTok too. The advertiser decides how much of that money goes where.

If Meta delivers the desired result even a little better, that gives the advertiser a reason to spend more on Meta the next time they allocate their budget. From what I see in my work, even a seemingly small performance difference of 1 to 2% matters. For a company spending a lot across many ads, that difference adds up repeatedly.

Editor’s note: In its second quarter 2025 earnings call, Meta said that improvements related to GEM increased ad conversions by approximately 5% on Instagram and approximately 3% across Facebook Feed and Reels. These figures describe the company’s reported improvement in conversion performance from those changes. They do not mean that Meta’s total revenue grew by the same percentages or that conversion rates rose by that many percentage points.

05 Buying After Seeing an Ad Versus Buying Because of It

Damnang: Advertisers ultimately decide whether to spend more based on performance. How do they check that performance? Is it enough to see that orders have increased?

Expert: An increase in orders alone isn’t enough. Some people would have ordered even without the ad. A regular customer at the bakery, or someone who walks past it every day, may already have been planning to buy a cake. Just because they placed an order after seeing an ad doesn’t necessarily mean the ad caused the purchase.

That’s why we use a comparison group. People are divided randomly into two groups. One can receive the ad, while the other does not receive it. Comparing the orders from the two groups afterward lets us estimate the additional effect of the ad. It’s a similar principle to using a comparison group when testing a new drug.

Regular reports can show who placed an order after seeing an ad. That is useful too, but it may include people who would have bought without the ad. To find out how many orders were caused by the ad, you need that kind of comparison.

Damnang: How does Meta check a new AI model before using it? Does it also try the model with a subset of users first?

Expert: Yes. We don’t apply a new model to everyone all at once just because it has been built. Some users get the new system, while a comparison group stays on the existing system, and we look at the results. We check whether advertiser performance improved and what happened to the user experience.

In the ad effectiveness measurement I just described, we compared a group that could receive the ad with a group that did not. Here, we compare a group using the new model with a group using the existing one. That’s because this time we’re trying to measure the effect of changing the model.

Damnang: The system already sounds quite sophisticated. What problems need to be solved for it to improve further?

Expert: The technology for finding people who are likely to buy keeps improving, but I think there is still a lot of room for better measurement. Moving beyond knowing what happened after an ad to establishing what the ad actually changed is still difficult. Privacy protections also limit the signals we can observe, and we need to get accurate results within those limits.

There’s also the challenge of evaluating new ads. The system looks at the images and text, but when an ad is completely new, some judgments require actual results to accumulate. The advertiser has to spend time and money while that happens.

Suppose the bakery makes five ads. Today, it needs to show them and observe the responses to find out which ones work well. An experienced marketer can look at an ad and judge its potential. If the system could also recognize promising ads more quickly, advertisers could reduce that trial and error.

06 Why Getting Started Is Harder for Small Advertisers

Damnang: If the system needs to run ads and collect results before it can learn, wouldn’t that make things harder for a small shop just starting out? It probably doesn’t get many orders to begin with.

Expert: That’s right. A major problem is the lack of examples for the system to learn from. Every order can teach it something about who becomes a customer, but if orders are rare, there aren’t enough of those signals.

A large advertiser can accumulate many results, but a small bakery might receive only around five online orders a week. That means learning is slower, and performance can fluctuate from week to week. Even if setting up ads becomes easier, the difficulty of accumulating enough results remains.

In that situation, one option to consider is an objective based on an action that happens before a purchase and occurs more frequently, such as adding an item to a cart. The idea is to first obtain examples the system can learn from, then switch back to a purchase objective once orders have increased enough.

Damnang: What about choosing clicks as the objective, since they happen more often? Could you start by trying to bring in as many people as possible?

Expert: You have to remember that people who click and people who buy aren’t necessarily the same. If you ask the system for more clicks, it looks for people who are likely to click. You might get a lot of people who enjoy clicking on pictures of cakes. You could end up with plenty of inexpensive clicks without getting more orders.

That doesn’t mean the system failed to do its job. It did what the advertiser asked. So the first question should be what result your business needs, rather than what result is easy to obtain in large numbers. If orders are the goal, you ultimately need to check whether those actions lead to orders and send those results back to the system.

At the same time, if orders are too infrequent, the system doesn’t have enough signals to learn from. You need to consider both issues. Advice to switch the objective to an earlier action is something to consider in that context. It isn’t a rule that applies equally to every small business.

Editor’s note: Adding an item to a cart and making a purchase are different optimization objectives. Increasing an action that comes earlier does not guarantee more purchases. When purchase signals are scarce, the choice of a proxy objective needs to be assessed alongside its relationship to actual orders.

Damnang: Someone new to advertising might assume that fluctuating performance means they’ve set something up incorrectly. Are there mistakes beginners are particularly likely to make?

Expert: They keep changing things based on the first few days of results. The results are poor on day two, so they change the image. Then they change the audience, then the budget. But major changes to the settings can affect the system’s learning process.

If you keep adjusting things before enough results have accumulated, it becomes harder to tell what worked and what the problem was. I tell people starting out to choose one clear objective and give it enough budget and time to assess that objective.

07 People Still Need to Set Business Goals

Damnang: Even setting a goal and waiting for results sounds difficult for someone just starting out. Creating the ads must be a challenge too. How much can they leave to AI now?

Expert: It can already help with quite a lot. There are tools that suggest ad copy, create different versions of photos, and help produce videos. Once an ad is made, the system can also decide who sees it, where it appears, and how to allocate the budget.

The direction is to have advertisers specify the results they want and their budget, then let the system handle more of the rest. For a small business without a marketing team, that can reduce work that used to require hiring someone or spending a lot of time on it themselves.

But being able to create an ad automatically does not mean the AI automatically understands the business. The advertiser still needs to tell it what they want and which results matter.

Damnang: Earlier, I understood that if you want more orders, you should set orders as the objective. Are there things you need to specify beyond that?

Expert: Different orders can leave the business with different amounts of profit. Take the bakery example. It might make little profit on a box of cupcakes even if it sells a lot of them, while a single wedding cake could be worth much more. A customer who comes back every month might also matter more than someone who buys once.

If you only ask for more orders, the system will not work out on its own which orders matter more to that bakery. If profit differs by product, or certain customers are especially valuable, you need to give it goals and information that reflect that.

Someone also needs to review the ads it creates. You need to check whether the product description is accurate and whether the photos and wording suit your business. Even when creating ads gets easier, there are still things that someone who knows the business needs to check.

08 Extending Support Beyond Large Advertisers

Damnang: For someone running a shop alone, even interpreting ad performance could feel overwhelming. If an ad is not doing well, can they talk it through with someone at Meta?

Expert: The large advertisers I work with can meet regularly with their contacts at Meta. We look together at which features they could try and what they could improve in their current ads. But it is difficult to assign people in the same way to such a large number of small businesses.

I do not work directly with small advertisers, but my understanding is that they often submit inquiries and receive responses that way. If a problem comes up, they submit a ticket to get it resolved. As far as I know, they have fewer opportunities than large advertisers to meet regularly and discuss their campaigns.

I am interested in how AI can help close that gap. If advertisers can ask what they should change in their current ads, some of the support that people used to provide in meetings could become available to more businesses.

Damnang: Even when an ad works and brings in customers, the shop owner still has work to do. Can AI also help respond to customers asking about products or placing orders?

Expert: Yes. That is the role of AI that handles customer inquiries. It answers product questions on behalf of the business, recommends products, and helps with bookings and purchases. It is talking to a different person from an AI that advises advertisers on running their ads.

To continue with the bakery example, a customer might see an ad at 11 p.m. and send a message. They might ask whether a cake can be made without gluten, or whether the bakery can prepare a cake for twenty people by Saturday. By the time the owner checks the message the next morning, the customer may already have ordered somewhere else.

It helps if an AI with information about the shop can answer those questions and provide guidance at that moment. In a small bakery, the owner might also do the baking, work the register, and make deliveries. AI can help where a business might otherwise lose an interested customer before they place an order because no one is available to respond.

Of course, the business needs to decide what information the AI can use to answer questions and when it should hand the conversation over to a person. Its guidance needs to match what the shop can actually provide.

Editor’s note: Meta AI business assistant, as described by Meta, suggests campaign improvements and provides performance analysis and account support within an advertiser’s account. [[6] Business Agent, which serves customers, helps with tasks such as product recommendations and bookings. Its role differs from that of an AI that advises advertisers on running campaigns. This does not mean every business can automatically process every transaction without additional integrations.

09 How an Advertising Engineer Uses AI

Damnang: How do you use AI in your own development work? Do you use Muse at work as well?

Expert: I have not personally tried Muse yet. I mainly use AI to write or review code, and I also use it to create work tickets.

Damnang: Beyond helping with coding, is AI changing the work of engineers who build advertising systems?

Expert: I think the focus is shifting from building settings and buttons for advertisers to operate toward checking whether the system is making good decisions. That changes what we focus on as engineers compared with when advertisers had to manage more settings themselves.

As more is handled automatically, we need to check that the features work properly and that advertisers can easily assess the results. Even if tools take on more repetitive work, people still need to decide what to try and what to release.

Editor’s note: Meta has publicly described an AI agent for development that generates hypotheses for advertising models, runs training, handles errors, and conducts repeated experiments. This is an example of automating internal development work. It does not establish that the expert uses that particular tool or that the consumer product Muse has been adopted internally.

10 Using Muse and Using Its Data for Advertising

Damnang: I found Muse convenient because there was less to install and configure myself. I could grant it permissions and give it tasks in the environment it provides. Today, I asked Muse to visit my card issuer’s website and ask whether I was eligible for any benefits, and it actually went through a support chat. I did not get any additional benefits, but I liked that it handled something I would otherwise have had to wait around and do myself.

When I use it this way, though, I explain my situation and connect various services. That seems like it could help Meta understand users. Is that information something the advertising business can use too?

Expert: Working in advertising, I initially had that hope as well. Slightly better information can improve predictions and affect the results. But in our current advertising work, we do not have a connection that brings in customer data from Muse for us to use.

A user entrusting information to Muse does not mean they have agreed to its use in advertising. You have to consider consent, privacy, and the purpose of the use. Just because the information exists does not mean it can be taken and used somewhere else.

When I think about privacy and consent, I find it hard to see a way to bring Muse’s information into advertising.

Editor’s note: In its launch announcement for Muse, Meta stated that users’ conversations and the data in their personal virtual machines are not shared with Meta’s advertising systems.

11 Why the Expert Is More Interested in Smart Glasses

Damnang: How do you see smart glasses? What would it mean for Meta to have a device of its own?

Expert: I actually have higher hopes for smart glasses than for Muse. Meta makes software and services, but today they mostly run on devices made by other companies. I think having the devices people use in everyday life could change what Meta is able to do.

Of course, that does not mean smart glasses are ready to replace smartphones today. I do not think they are there yet. There is still a lot to improve. But I am hopeful that, as they get better, people could use them for more things in daily life.

Damnang: What kinds of everyday situations should we picture? I am curious about what might be more convenient than using a smartphone.

Expert: Say you are walking down the street and suddenly have a question. With a smartphone, you have to take it out, type something, and keep checking it as you walk to see whether an answer has come back. With glasses, it could be convenient to ask while continuing what you were doing and see the information right away.

You could also imagine asking for something to be looked up while working on something else at your desk, then seeing its progress in front of you. Or the glasses might show you directions to an item you are looking for in a store. If users could easily ask their glasses to do things they usually ask Muse to do, that would give them another reason to use the device. Those are uses I personally look forward to.

Editor’s note: According to information shared after the interview, Muse integration with smart glasses is currently limited to the microphone and audio, with plans to add camera integration first and display integration later.

If those plans materialize, the experience could expand from making spoken requests and hearing responses to sharing the scene in front of the user and seeing results or task progress directly. That could reduce the need to take out a phone to explain the situation to Muse or check its work. The visual guidance discussed above should therefore be understood as a possible experience once those integrations are in place.

Damnang: Glasses seem as though they could understand the surroundings a person is looking at, so I wonder whether that changes anything for advertising. Would the information available for ads be very different from what comes through a smartphone?

Expert: At first, I also thought there might be more information. But thinking about it now, I do not think the amount of information used in advertising would increase dramatically. If an app moves from a smartphone to glasses, it might simply change the device the information comes from.

We cannot assume that everything the glasses see and hear can be used for advertising. We still have to consider which data can be used for which purpose, and the same privacy and consent issues remain. Changing the device alone does not resolve them.

12 Analysis by Damnang

Better performance from advertising AI can contribute to Meta’s growth, but an announcement that conversions increased by a few percent cannot be read as an equivalent percentage increase in revenue. An advertiser’s available budget, inventory, and profit per order also affect how much they spend next. That is why Meta’s reported performance improvements need to be considered alongside whether advertisers actually increase their budgets and continue running existing campaigns.

When thinking about the connection between Muse and advertising, I am interested in how much more work advertisers might be able to delegate to AI. Even after receiving advice on what to improve, advertisers still need to organize information, change settings, and check results. If the task planning and tool execution capabilities demonstrated by Muse were also applied to AI for advertisers, it could expand the range of work they can delegate. The connection I have in mind is the application of Meta’s models and agent technology to advertisers’ work. I have not confirmed that Muse is currently integrated into that advertising solution.

For example, suppose an advertiser connects their own ad account and product information and authorizes their use. An agent could examine existing campaign performance alongside inventory for each product and suggest which products to promote more that week. Once the advertiser chooses a direction, it could use creative tools to prepare revisions and set up tests within an approved budget. We could also imagine it reviewing the results and suggesting what to change next. The information needed in this scenario is material the advertiser has provided for their own business. It does not require access to the private conversations of other people who use Muse.

For businesses short on specialist staff, that could make a practical difference. Advice has limited value if the advertiser receives an explanation of ad performance but has no time to move between screens and apply it. If AI also helps execute the work and reduces the time spent on campaign management, it could become easier to start advertising or restart a paused campaign. For Meta, that could mean more businesses running ads and existing advertisers continuing to use its services. To see whether those effects materialize, we need to look at how much the workload falls and whether businesses achieve the results they want and keep advertising. These changes on the advertiser’s side are also worth watching when considering the relationship between Muse and Meta’s advertising business.
