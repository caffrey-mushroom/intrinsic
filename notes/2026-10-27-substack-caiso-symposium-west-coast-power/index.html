Ask Not What Energy Can Do for AI // CAISO Symposium and West Coast Markets Review – Energy Model

Ask Not What Energy Can Do for AI // CAISO Symposium and West Coast Markets Review

Author: Robert Boswall

Date: Monday, October 26, 2026 (published 2026/10/26 12:37:58)

We went to the California Independent System Operator (CAISO) Symposium to see what untapped opportunities there might be in a market often overlooked or discounted for datacenter development. The system modelers told us they were more focused on transport and building electrification than they were datacenters, and the Symposium asked not what energy can do for AI, but what AI can do for energy.

This note is part conference report and part market review of the Western power markets for datacenters.

Executive Summary

California’s prospects.

  • We see no re-rating for California exposure, with Arizona staying the West’s winner through Salt River Project (SRP).
  • Only one datacenter developer attended the symposium, although it did claim pockets of potential in CAISO.
  • One upside to watch is that if vehicle and building electrification does not happen at the pace forecast then there might be additional spare headroom into 2030.

Permits and taxes.

  • Santa Clara’s sales tax is 6% state tax plus 3.75% local. Cities cannot waive local sales taxes, as they must match the level set by state law.
  • From next year Senate Bill 887 means every datacenter, whatever its size, needs environmental review under the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA).
  • Onsite power is limited as gas plants are prevented from exporting to the grid and anything over 50 MW needs a state permit.

Oversized large-load tariffs.

  • We plan to write on this in more depth soon, but large-load tariffs are being introduced with punitive terms that make projects unviable.
  • Oregon and NV Energy have both set a 90% minimum charge.
  • From 1st January 2028, the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) will set another Large Load Tariff.

The limits of flexibility.

  • CAISO’s flexible connection only deals with transmission limits and is not intended to fix a lack of supply should one occur at the local level.
  • Silicon Valley Power is experimenting with flexible datacenter deals; but this is driven by extreme power limitations at premium locations so is not scalable.
  • As the shell build out catches up to chip supply, curtailable sites will lose pricing power. Developers see this and set terminal values accordingly.

Grid wars: large, lucrative, and mobile demand turns neighbors into rivals.

  • California is particularly reliant on transmission, which creates tensions as new load gets threatened by connections happening upstream.
  • For example Santa Clara’s datacenters are dependent upon the same capacity as the San José datacenter deal of Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E).

AI for energy: the regulator is pushing, but the impact will be slow.

  • The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) commissioner opened the Symposium calling advanced grid technologies “a must-have, not an option, not a pilot”.
  • Utilities are by their nature slow adopters, especially as these operational improvements do not add to their rate base and so cannot earn them a return.

1. Power

Source: EIA-861 2025, HIFLD, SEC filings

Grid operator staff told us datacenter load is “not overwhelming” next to electric vehicles and building electrification. This is an unusual position to take these days but it is backed up by the California Energy Commission’s (CEC) planning case; in which datacenters account for 41% (1,647 MW out of 4,019 MW) of CAISO’s peak growth to 2030. Whether this other load growth materializes is dependent upon macroeconomic trends; if it does not then California may be left with more grid headroom than it bargained for.

Source: California Energy Commission, CED 2025 Peak Forecast (21 Jan 2026), Data Center Memo (15 Apr 2026)

PG&E’s datacenter pipeline reached 12.9 GW at the end of September, and in conversation it still claims 5-7 GW of this is coming. Yet only about 174 MW has come online since 2024. In September it deferred “large loads beyond 1.8 gigawatts” as part of a $2 billion cut to its 2027 capital plan. On its 22 October earnings call PG&E said the 1.8 GW it expects online by 2030 remains in the plan. This is not the first time there has been some overzealous signaling. The CEC found that of the 1,900 MW that PG&E had expected to come online in 2025, only 70 MW energized. We are told that executives strongly encourage new large loads to help prevent rate increases and to drive system utilization, and PG&E pitches each gigawatt as cutting other customers’ bills by 1% or more.

PG&E made the capital cut because California’s wildfire liability rules leave its financing costs too high. It has since put the whole company under a strategic review, with a decision due by summer 2027. Fitch moved PG&E’s outlook to negative this quarter, and on 14 Oct S&P’s Regulatory Research Associates lowered its ranking of California’s regulation. PG&E’s chief executive said datacenter growth needs “a liability reform that enables that to happen sustainably”.

Source: SemiAnalysis Datacenter Model (Oct 22, 2026 edition); California Energy Commission 2025 Planning Forecast

California’s grid is particularly reliant upon transmission capacity, both across the state and into neighboring states. Its high reliance on intermittent power means it imports power when it is short and exports solar power when it is long. Within the state long transmission lines run North to South as different regions of generation and load rely upon each other depending upon conditions.

Source: SemiAnalysis Energy Model; WECC; California Public Utilities Commission; HIFLD; OpenInfraMap (OpenStreetMap contributors)

Fervo Energy, which is building the Cape Station geothermal plant in Utah (its first 33 MW block went commercial on 1 Oct 2026), came to the Symposium hoping to sell into California to have offtaker diversification. This is understandable, but IPPs do not make venture scale returns. Our view is that it is better positioned as a powered land enabler, with captive demand, which puts a premium on fuel diversification, clean power, or both.

CAISO has established a new power pool called the Extended Day-Ahead Market (EDAM). There is competition over utilities between this western option or Markets+, run by the Southwest Power Pool (SPP). The Regional Organization for Western Energy (ROWE) is the new body that will govern CAISO’s Western markets. The goal is that a bigger power pool improves market efficiency. Initial results show that EDAM delivered $11.38 million of gross benefits across its participants in the second quarter of 2026. EDAM is still growing with new entrants over the next two years. Portland General Electric joined on 1st October 2026. Four more will join in 2027: Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP), the Balancing Authority of Northern California (BANC), the Public Service Company of New Mexico (PNM), and Turlock Irrigation District. Then the final of the current commitments is NV Energy, planning to join in 2028.

Greater efficiency is not to everyone’s benefit. New generation joining the pool can end up receiving reduced prices. So, some generators and utilities through the midwest are looking to new datacenter load as the more lucrative option.

2. Permits, taxes, and onsite power

California is a rare jurisdiction that taxes equipment purchases, while its property tax on equipment is around the national median. Santa Clara is the Loudoun county of the West, with the highest concentration of datacenters but also high taxes. The total rate is 9.75%, adding about $1.6M/MW of IT load, compared to zero in Oregon and about $0.3 million per MW in Nevada at a 2% rate. This is not likely to change in the current political climate especially as the state’s 2025 exemption bill, Senate Bill 58, lost its hearing and later became an unrelated air-quality bill.

California’s governor signed seven datacenter bills on 21st September 2026. From 1st January 2027, Senate Bill 887 bars datacenters of any size from the categorical exemptions of the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA). Every project still needs an initial study, and under CEQA many people can have standing to sue the project.

Power in California comes at a premium. In 2024 average prices were 37.7c/kWh at PG&E and 37.0c/kWh at San Diego Gas & Electric (SDG&E), against 15.4c/kWh at Silicon Valley Power (SVP), Santa Clara’s city utility. SVP’s low price is in part thanks to its datacenters, which use 55% of its power and run steadily, giving the system a 78.6% load factor.

Slow grid connections are a national phenomenon. But California makes alternatives like behind-the-meter power or bring-your-own-capacity unnecessarily hard through permit requirements and operational limits. Any thermal plant of 50 MW or more needs a CEC permit or an exemption, and that includes backup generators. This has been getting even harder. SB 887’s fast track route now requires that there can be no net rise in fossil fuel use. Then if a site does connect to the grid it is hard to sell surplus onsite power. This is due to export rules and clean-energy purchasing rules. Exporting to anyone but the utility moves a plant off California’s Rule 21 interconnection track onto the federal track, which brings its own studies and market registration. Selling power “for compensation” also risks utility status under Public Utilities Code 218. State procurement orders call for zero-emitting or Renewables Portfolio Standard (RPS) eligible resources, and Senate Bill 1368 (SB 1368) bars long contracts with high-emitting baseload plants.

On 26th May 2026 the CEC made an improvement by changing Title 20, section 1201. A datacenter served by an onsite plant is no longer part of the plant’s permit, so the operator can change the building and its equipment without reopening the plant permit.

3. Moratorium by tariff: some are worse than others

We plan to write on this in more depth soon, but large-load tariffs are being introduced with punitive terms and bad structuring that make projects unviable. Minimum charge requirements are too high, making both ramping and utilization a significant operational risk for developers that depend on tenant offtakers, which favors hyperscalers. Utilities often refuse to give any financial assurances of delivery timelines, so the datacenter bears the delivery risk, but cannot raise finances against utility assurances.

The only datacenter developer to attend the conference said its Western efforts are mainly in Arizona, thanks to its utility, although it thinks there are pockets of opportunity in California to tap into generation headroom.

We are seeing developers quickly pricing in new utility large-load tariffs. These are a bit like moratoria, because when they are poorly designed they are closing off certain areas to datacenter development. Nevada is thought of as a bad case, where minimum payment requirements are too high and inflexible. Other Berkshire Hathaway utilities are also criticized by some, especially PacifiCorp’s credit and collateral demands.

Source: Oregon Public Utility Commission; New Project Media; SRP; California Senate Bill 886

Oregon’s regulator set a 90% minimum charge on 7th May 2026 with terms of up to 30 years, even though Portland General Electric, Amazon and the datacenter industry’s trade group had asked for 80%.

NV Energy has no general datacenter tariff and uses standard 25-year contracts. One of them, for the developer Fleet, sets a 90% minimum. PacifiCorp also works contract by contract. Its Utah contract demands heavy credit security and PacifiCorp’s own credit rating faces a possible cut to junk.

Arizona’s two big utilities have gone opposite ways. APS has made no new datacenter commitments since 1 Jan 2024, and its next offer of up to 1.2 GW starts service in 2028. SRP serves any datacenter that wins local zoning. Its minimum bill is 80% of forecast demand, but SRP can cut that minimum when the customer pays upfront for the plants and wires that serve it, or for economic development.

California has not yet defined a large-load tariff, and has so far used the issue for political signaling. Senate Bill 886 (SB 886) requires the CPUC to set datacenter tariffs by 1st Jan 2028 that keep costs off other customers, and Assembly Bill 2383 (AB 2383) requires 10-year minimum payments with early termination fees and the posting of upfront collateral.

In our view, SRP’s 80% works because it has flexibility for customers who pay upfront and the process is described as collaborative. The core problem that makes certain large-load tariffs prohibitive is how one-sided the requirements and burdens can be. Utilities increasingly raise the liabilities taken on by the developer or hyperscaler, without themselves posting delay damages or firm timeline commitments.

4. Santa Clara: the exception, and its limits

Santa Clara has finished buildings still waiting for full energization. Among these are STACK’s 48 MW SVY02A and Digital Realty’s 48 MW SJC37, both of which are waiting on a new transmission line planned for April 2028.

This is because SVP depends on new transmission that others must build. A good example is STACK’s site at 1200 Memorex Drive, which steps up from 31 megavolt-amperes (MVA) in July 2026 to 77 MVA by July 2031. Even at this small scale, 46 MVA in six steps, each step is tied to a named transmission project. The main relief is LS Power’s Newark to Northern Receiving Station line at 230 kV, which LS Power expects online in 2028.

[Chart 7 — see attached PDF]

Source: City of Santa Clara file 26-330; CAISO 2025-2026 Transmission Plan

SVP and Emerald AI are running a pilot of flexible datacenters in Santa Clara, this is an interesting partnership but it is an extreme situation rather than a scalable solution. STACK’s amended agreement gives SVP Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) control of its 60 kV feeder breakers. SVP’s representatives have made clear to us that they would rather not pull that lever, because dropping a big load at once is itself a power-quality event. SVP’s flyer at the Symposium says datacenters respond “within 1 minute” by “dropping load by 30%”, and city staff plan to offer about five more customers the same kind of deal. Emerald AI is developing the controls to enable this kind of flex to be driven by software on the chip and rack rather than hardware solutions like batteries. This will be a useful capability for a range of applications. But flexing the full datacenter for the sake of power response will be a second-best solution for the foreseeable future. In our view, the revenue from the compute is worth too many times the cost of the power for this to be desirable outside of premium datacenter locations which are power starved, like Santa Clara.

5. Grid wars: state tensions, transformed by mobile demand

Upstream and downstream impacts have always been a bug of interconnected grids. But large and lucrative power demand is now highly mobile, making this into a central feature.

Santa Clara is a case in point. PG&E and San José struck a deal in July 2025 for up to 2 GW that relies on the same new LS Power lines which SVP is waiting for to further energize partially dormant datacenters. We found no public document that splits that capacity between them. In our view, on a CAISO-run network, whoever connects first takes the capacity, so the larger upstream players can muscle their way in.

The developer told us that projects can even pass PG&E’s study and then fail when CAISO finds a problem upstream. In Arizona, it said, SRP and APS sometimes run joint studies because their systems affect each other so closely.

Some want load on the wires but off the books. Amazon asked the Western Resource Adequacy Program (WRAP) in Dec 2025 to let datacenter load with onsite backup leave its forecast. WRAP is the West’s voluntary reliability pool. In our view, excluded load still sits on the wires, but nobody must buy supply for it. The task force discussion has since drifted toward counting onsite backup as demand response, and nothing is decided.

Others are pulling up the drawbridge. NV Energy will end wholesale supply to the Lake Tahoe utility after May 2027, a change it says was planned for years. It cites about 22 GW of service requests, over twice the peak of its current system. It is also suing the developer Tract, which says it has committed nearly $1 billion to NV Energy infrastructure.

At market level, EDAM against Markets+ and generators asking why they should join California are the same contest (see section 1). Two utilities’ datacenter plans that each look fine alone can be impossible together.

6. AI for energy

The opening keynote came from a Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) commissioner, who called advanced grid technologies “a must-have, not an option, not a pilot”. The examples given were artificial intelligence (AI) that runs faster power-flow studies and real-time sensors that rate power lines by the actual weather. The often debated question is whether these tools can be used in grid planning or only in daily operations.

We have strong hopes for all these technological advancements. But in many instances there are regulatory, bureaucratic, and political reasons that they will be slow to be adopted. In large part this is because reliability is sacrosanct in electricity. This is entirely reasonable, but it also means that technology that improves efficiency does not quickly replace the existing processes.

One company that is well known in this space is GridCARE, which is using modeling to find spare capacity for projects to connect to the grid. The problem is that grid studies follow strict rules, and products like GridCARE’s do not stop them from being necessary. The North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) writes the mandatory reliability rules, and its connection-study standard FAC-002-4 requires the utility’s planners to study every new connection.

More rules are coming. FERC ordered NERC on 16 Jul 2026 to file mandatory standards for “computational load” by 31 Dec 2026, and NERC’s draft would register sites of 50 MW or more that connect at 100 kV or above.
