SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion examined AI data-center technology, focusing on advanced packaging, co-packaged optics, silicon photonics, memory, cooling, and foundry competition. The expert has extensive semiconductor process and packaging experience spanning mature nodes through 2 nm, including FinFET, redistribution layers, and factory development.

AI demand continues to drive larger chips, more HBM, higher interconnect bandwidth, and stronger cooling requirements. Packaging capacity and yield remain key constraints. TSMC is developing CoPoS panel-level packaging alongside COUPE, its optical integration platform built on CoWoS and SoIC technologies.

Key Insights

CPO is viewed as the long-term destination, but adoption should progress from pluggable XPO to NPO and eventually CPO. The largest commercialization barriers are optical testing, hybrid-bonding precision, multilayer alignment, large-package warpage, thermal management, and yield. CoPoS is expected to precede broad CPO adoption because larger panels provide more room for GPUs, ASICs, HBM, and optical components. CPO becomes compelling when copper links cannot satisfy rack-to-rack bandwidth, distance, and power requirements. The expert strongly expects TSMC to remain dominant because of its process yields, packaging maturity, scale, and supply- chain management. Passive-component suppliers already validated in major AI ecosystems may have stronger long-term CPO positioning than conventional module vendors.

CL: I have worked at TSMC for 14 years. At the beginning, I was mainly responsible for front-end work, specifically FinFET. I was responsible for the FinFET area and thin-film processes such as PVD, CVD, and ALD, primarily equipment and process R&D. I am familiar with technologies from 130 nanometers through the latest 2-nanometer node.

Because of AI, demand for advanced packaging became extremely strong in 2019. We later moved to the back end, or advanced back-end packaging, to help with these designs. Because this mainly involved RDL, which has some similarities to front-end work, we also moved into back-end packaging R&D. I remained primarily responsible for the RDL-related aspects of advanced packaging.

More recently, around 2023, TSMC began establishing its factory in Arizona, so I came to Arizona with TSMC to build the factory from scratch in the desert. My main role had been in R&D in Hsinchu. AC: First, could you introduce the most important industry trends or technological breakthroughs that you observed at this year's conference?

CL: In terms of technological breakthroughs, the trend remains the same: AI demand is still very strong. In addition to chips themselves, everyone is paying the greatest attention to packaging technology because packaging remains the area with the most severe shortages. Packaging involves changes to the overall architecture.

There are several main elements. First, the core computing components, including AI GPUs and ASICs, are becoming increasingly powerful. Next is today's topic: CPO and silicon-photonic interconnects, which are mainly intended to address power consumption and bandwidth. Memory is also adopting new architectures such as HBM, HBF, and eSSD.

In advanced packaging, the hottest development recently, in addition to existing 2.5D and 3D packaging, CoWoS, and SoIC, is that TSMC is actively developing CoPoS. CoPoS development is proceeding in parallel, while CoWoS will also be used with the so-called COUPE platform.

There are also new materials for advanced packaging, such as glass substrates and, subsequently, glass interposers. The key technologies mainly revolve around substrate processes and packaging methods. These are some of the new developments in packaging.

Then there is thermal management, including liquid cooling and what we call MLD, or MicroLED chips, along with new cooling technologies and architectures. Broadly speaking, there are five major categories: GPU evolution, bandwidth, memory, advanced packaging, and thermal management and power delivery. These are approximately the five categories of new technology.

AC: High-speed optical interconnects have also become very popular recently, and they use co-packaged optics, or CPO. What is your view of this area?

CL: It is an inevitable development trend. There are currently some bottlenecks to commercialization. One is wafer- level optical testing. The industry generally knows what direction it needs to take, but there are still no particularly good testing methods. Everyone is therefore developing ways to perform the testing.

Another bottleneck is precision alignment of the FAU. The alignment must remain highly accurate under high- temperature conditions, which is currently a challenge.

The packaging itself is not a major problem. ELS, PICs, and EICs can be packaged together. However, packaging them together in a high-density space creates major challenges in temperature control and yield. Space is already insufficient, and substrates are becoming larger. As packages increase in size, warpage can occur. Warpage can create what we call voids, which then reduce yield. This is another bottleneck.

Cooling is also an issue. As I just mentioned, the routing of microchannels is another area requiring a breakthrough.

COUPE is ultimately an inevitable direction if we want to continue increasing computing power and performance. However, there are currently many bottlenecks to commercialization. TSMC is working simultaneously with various vendors, and those vendors are introducing manufacturing and technological breakthroughs.

AC: I have also noticed that TSMC is mainly promoting its COUPE platform and mass production of CPO architectures this year. At present, what do you think is the most fundamental bottleneck preventing yield from improving?

CL: I mentioned some of the bottlenecks earlier, beginning with optical testing. The first major issue to overcome is warpage because the yield of SoIC is still not improving sufficiently.

More precisely, COUPE uses a packaging architecture based on the CoWoS platform. The vertical portion is SoIC. The bottleneck in SoIC is hybrid bonding, meaning the packaging bottleneck during stacking. The precision of current equipment and certain materials still present limitations, so 3D stacked packaging has bottlenecks.

At present, 2 to 4 layers do not present a major problem. If you stack more layers, however, misalignment can occur, creating yield differences.

COUPE is essentially what we call 3.5D packaging, combining vertical and horizontal stacking. Horizontal integration requires a larger substrate. The conductors, density, every RDL layer, and the I/O ports in between become difficult to manufacture when density is too high. The line widths must become smaller, and yield falls accordingly.

Even if the package can be manufactured, heat dissipation during operation remains a major problem. If there is too much heat and thermal management is poor, the substrate will warp, which then causes yield problems.

You can therefore see that TSMC's main emphasis will initially be on CoPoS, meaning panel-level packaging, and it will subsequently extend that technology to incorporate optical packaging. Development proceeds step by step in this way.

The larger bottlenecks are the vertical stacking required for optical 3D integration and the warpage of large horizontal panels. Once these two problems are solved, testing and packaging technologies for optical components must also become mature. Broadly speaking, those are the three problems that must be overcome.

At the current stage, there is XPO. XPO is pluggable. It still uses optical modules, but they can be replaced and

AC: There has been a great deal of discussion about these three architectures. Some manufacturers are primarily promoting NPO, while others are promoting CPO. How do you view the differences among the three architectures, and which do you think will be the long-term solution?

CL: I think it will definitely begin with XPO, and the transition will proceed step by step. XPO comes first so that the industry can understand the algorithms and communication rules and confirm that the computations can communicate with one another.

Only then will we move to NPO and bring the components closer. After that, we may eliminate the connecting lines and package the optics together. CPO is definitely the most difficult because packaging everything together requires advanced packaging technology.

At present, TSMC is probably the only company with relatively mature advanced packaging technology and a leading position. Even TSMC has not yet reached mass-production standards. It is still serving only a small market, and we remain in the R&D phase.

Regarding which architectures manufacturers are promoting, they will certainly promote NPO first. However, different companies have different interpretations of how near the "N" in NPO actually is.

NPO must succeed first. We can then determine how the interconnect layout should be routed and arranged. I therefore think the transition will proceed progressively.

AC: In the long term, it will still converge on CPO. What do you think the timing and pace of this transition will be? Are you familiar with that aspect?

The first issue is that the original wafer reticle capacity is insufficient. GPUs and ASICs are becoming increasingly large, while more HBM stacks must be included. A package may originally have used 8 HBM stacks, but in the future it could use 16. There may still be insufficient space, requiring vertical stacking. GPUs may also move from 2 dies to 4 dies.

There is already insufficient space on the original wafer. If optical components must also be packaged with it, space will certainly be insufficient, and computing efficiency cannot reach its maximum.

TSMC is therefore concentrating most heavily on CoPoS because it is a relatively achievable objective. CoPoS moves from wafer to panel and increases the available reticle area, allowing more chips to be packaged.

The panels currently being promoted measure 310 by 310. Put simply, moving from the original 300-by-300 wafer to roughly 310 by 310 means the required changes to machinery and equipment are relatively limited. AC: Data centers are facing increasingly high costs related to heat generation and power consumption. What considerations will drive cloud providers to switch to CPO? For example, I understand that CPO performs better in terms of power consumption. What factors do you think will matter?

CL: Are you asking what incentives will drive them to convert entirely to CPO? Is that what you mean?

AC: Yes, although perhaps not an entire conversion to CPO. What will lead them to decide to begin using CPO? Introducing a new generation of products still requires a transition process.

CL: Correct. I think there are several motivations. For example, very large GPU systems and switches require higher optical bandwidth. CPO will be introduced when data must be exchanged very frequently.

Another factor is that a traditional scale-up network consists of racks. When GPUs need to interconnect across many racks, conventional systems use copper conductors. When pure copper wiring can no longer meet transmission- distance and power-consumption requirements, the value of CPO increases.

At that point, companies may consider packaging the GPUs together from the outset. In other words, when interconnections between racks can no longer meet requirements, customers will become highly willing to switch to CPO.

Put simply, bandwidth outside the package becomes the main limitation. When no other approach can meet requirements, the industry must use in-package integration to achieve the required I/O bandwidth and overcome the bandwidth constraint. Those are the reasons companies become more willing to transition to CPO.

Of course, the cost cannot be too high because CPO yield may not initially be good. The technology must reach what we call a sweet spot before more users are willing to switch. If initial yield is low and applications do not yet require the performance urgently, CPO adoption will proceed more slowly.

I think that is approximately the current situation. That is why everyone is still focusing first on CoWoS and then primarily on CoPoS. Everyone is conducting CPO R&D, but switching directly to CPO will not be the first priority.

The chips used in current packaging are also expensive, but we do not package everything together and then simply discard the entire package if it fails. Otherwise, all the preceding work would have been wasted.
