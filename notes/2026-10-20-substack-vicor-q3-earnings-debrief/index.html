Substack · 2026-10-20

$197.1M of revenue. Up 37.5% QoQ and 78.5% YoY.

Operating profit more than doubled.

I think this is a really strong quarter. I calculate product gross margin at approximately 52.2%. And the new AI licensee has already placed another significant licensing purchase order.

I wanted growth that actually drops through to profit. We got it.

And that follow-on license order is really important. There is repeat business here.

In my October 16 article, I focused on Vicor’s two ways of getting paid. They manufacture power-conversion hardware or license the intellectual property used by other suppliers. I wanted evidence that hardware growth and profitability were continuing to improve together. We got that this morning.

Revenue cleared the raised guidance for more than 30% sequential growth, which implied more than approximately $186.4M. Operating profit rose 107% from $34.9M to $72.1M, taking calculated operating margin from 24.3% to 36.6%.

I find the price comparison interesting too. Look at this.

Subscribed

Vicor closed ~$223 on September 18, when the published Q3 guide was nearly 10% QoQ growth. It closed ~$291 on September 29, with the guide above 20%. The next raises came September 21 and September 30, respectively, and the stock traded as high as $331 intraday on October 1. Actual growth came in ~38%. Yet the price was ~$200 at 10:02 a.m. EDT on October 20. Obviously there is a lot that comes into play here. I think the backdrop of the AI trade being punished is the obvious reason. But the reason I bring it up is because if sentiment returns to the AI trade I would think that the market would reward VICR. We shall see.

EPS was $1.33. I prefer the operating comparison because Q2 included an income-tax benefit, while Q3 included a $15.4M income-tax expense.

Product revenue grew 15.6% QoQ to $130.5M. Calculating gross margin from disclosed product revenue and costs gives 52.2%, up from 46.7%. Management cited volume, efficiency and mix. After the relocation costs and manufacturing pressures I discussed previously, more of each hardware dollar is becoming gross profit. The hardware improvement matters even with royalties grabbing the attention.

Royalties helped lift total gross margin from 58.0% to 68.3%, alongside the hardware improvement. Operating cash flow of $69.7M less $16.1M of capex gives approximately $53.6M of free cash flow. Cash reached $513.1M. I feel better about funding the expansion with that cash generation behind it for sure.

Royalties rose from $30.4M to $66.6M. The CFO said part of the increase reflected nonrecurring initial payments, without quantifying them even when asked.

Then the CEO pushed back on calling the new AI customer’s license simply one-time. That same licensee placed another significant licensing purchase order early in Q4. Its value and inclusion in the royalty minimum were not disclosed.

I think the follow-on order is an important signal. Q3 royalties included initial payments, so the quarterly number will move around. The customer has already come back with another order.

For Q4, management expects approximately 10% product-revenue growth, implying $143.6M, plus at least $43M of royalties without new license agreements. That adds up to a calculated ~$187M baseline rather than a total-revenue guide, with room for royalties above the minimum.

Management called $43M a conservative minimum that existing agreements could exceed. The baseline puts total revenue 5.4% below Q3, with hardware growing and royalties approximately 41% above Q2. The hardware business is still growing. Looking only at $197M followed by $187M misses the royalty mix. Hardware shipments can keep growing while reported revenue falls. But if there was one thing that market might dislike, I believe it’s this.

Approximately $100M of bookings in the first two weeks of Q4. Wow. That includes a large second-generation vertical power delivery order from the lead customer.

Q3 backlog reached $406M, up 7% QoQ.

Leading custom-chip and memory-test customers now represent more than $100M of annual revenue. Management described capacity-reservation discussions with these customers and, later, a major non-AI customer.

Vicor has also responded to two hyperscalers’ inquiries about all-inclusive IP licenses. If they convert, that would broaden licensing beyond what is in the existing-business Q4 baseline. Management said deals could close in Q4, but is taking a long-term approach to negotiations. The CEO also sees a larger royalty opportunity in 2027 and 2028 than he expected a year ago, without putting numbers on it.

Initial equipment and process deployment at Fab 2 is expected in Q2 2027 to relieve Fab 1 bottlenecks. Management expects Fab 1 to approach full capacity next year, with constraints concentrated in particular platforms and process steps. Early Fab 2 deployment can therefore matter before the entire facility is finished, although the incremental revenue contribution remains unquantified.

My earlier model excluded Fab 2 revenue in 2027 and allowed only an initial contribution in 2028. Earlier bottleneck relief could bring some incremental output forward, ahead of the full fab ramp.

Fab 3 is land and flexibility for now. Management is not putting up a building there for now, with Fab 2 the priority. Management expects to fund expansion without raising capital, supported by cash generation, but provided no complete capex budget.

My previous 12-month base case was around $280, using $8.07 of 2028 EPS at 35 times earnings, with a $140 downside case. Those remain the scenarios from my October 16 model.

I come away more encouraged about that work. Q3’s 36.6% operating margin is already approaching my earlier 40% assumption for 2028, helped by the initial royalty payments. Q4 margins will depend partly on the royalty mix. Management gave no numeric gross-margin or operating-margin guide. The earnings trajectory also depends on repeat royalties and the timing of new license orders. Management expects near-term legal expenses to average ~20% of royalty revenue.

The main risks are customer concentration, uneven royalties and manufacturing execution. Future license terms are negotiable. Patent disputes and competing power-delivery approaches can affect economics and adoption. A lower earnings multiple can also reduce the stock price of course.
