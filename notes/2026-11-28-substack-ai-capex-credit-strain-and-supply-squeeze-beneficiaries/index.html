  2. 五大巨头中除微软（Q3自由现金流+186亿美元）外四家最近一季自由现金流全部转负（亚马逊-334.6亿、谷歌-90.7亿、Meta上市以来首次转负至-21.2亿、甲骨文FY27 Q1为-54.0亿美元），前9个月回购大砍83%（由810亿降至136亿美元），市场预期2027年五大巨头CapEx将从今年约8000亿美元升至1.1万亿-1.4万亿美元。

  4. 折旧负担与投资回报压力凸显：五大巨头近年将服务器账面折旧年限从3年拉长至近6年（Uptime调研实际寿命约3.4年），Q3折旧同比仍飙升46%（远超18%营收增速）；高盛测算2026-2027年CapEx仅实现盈亏平衡即需3080亿美元年化AI收入。
  5. AI算力虹吸正在向传统硬件征收“AI税”：HBM单位产能消耗为普通DRAM的3-4倍，AI服务器占据约70%存储产能配额，推动标准存储合约价11个月暴涨超450%（美光毛利率87%、SK海力士营业利润率80%），直接迫使安霸（Ambarella）因边缘硬件存储短缺大幅下调下季指引。
  6. 供应链瓶颈将在未来18-36个月分三波缓解：第一波（2027 Q1-Q2）为NAND闪存（现货两周跌超20%、TrendForce预测Q1-Q2合约价转负）、老旧制程存储与铜；第二波（2027下半年）为GPU租赁（Ornn B300指数已较10月高点14.23美元/小时回落23.9%至10.83美元/小时）及新晶圆厂投产后的主流DRAM；最持久的瓶颈（2028-2030+）在变压器（交付周期2.5-3年以上）、燃气轮机（排期至2030-2032年）与PJM电网电力审批（2028/2029拍卖触及上限仍缺电近7GW）。

There is an angle to the AI trade that I haven’t seen anyone discuss yet.

Ambarella cut next-quarter guidance severely this week because memory makers pulled factory capacity away from edge hardware to feed AI servers.

I already talked about the implications for Amba.

But that got me digging into a bigger question.

Who else is taking a real profit hit purely because they sit at the back of the line behind hyperscale data centers? And what happens when those constraints go away?

This is a working thesis I am building out right now. More DD to come before I touch anything new.

But I think this is a good model to work through for 2027.

Two things are important to consider when we read late-November 2026 credit reports next to Q3 earnings calls.

First, the people questioning how the AI buildout is being financed have a real, data-backed case.

Second, that exact same spending wave is taxing a small group of genuinely great businesses outside of data centers that have strong organic demand and clean balance sheets.

Before the paywall, I will walk through the cash-flow and debt cracks showing up in AI infra right now, plus when the different supply bottlenecks break.

Behind the wall are the five names moving into my deeper DD queue for 2027.

Let’s get into it.

Giving the Skeptics Their Due

I don’t think that anybody serious thinks near-term AI demand is fake. Last quarter alone, Google Cloud grew 103%, Azure grew 48%, and AWS grew 39.3%.

Capacity is still sold out.

What HAS changed as of late is how Big Tech pays for it. For over a decade, these companies funded growth out of operating cash flow and used the leftovers for buybacks. That is no longer the case.

None of this should come as much of a surprise, and we have talked about this before. But it is relevant here so I want to quickly retouch it.

Across the Big Five (Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, and Oracle), combined operating cash flow was $198.6B against $230.0B in CapEx. They spent 115.8% of operating cash flow on CapEx.

Four of the five went free-cash-flow negative in their latest reported quarters, leaving Microsoft (+$18.6B) as the only one still self-funding.

Amazon Q3 FCF was -$33.46B (-$42.36B TTM) as gross Q3 CapEx jumped 120% YoY to $77.26B

Alphabet Q3 FCF was -$9.07B ($51.36B CapEx vs $42.29B OCF)

Meta Q3 FCF was -$2.12B (first negative FCF quarter since its 2012 IPO)

Oracle burned -$5.40B in Q1 FY27 (-$28.71B TTM) even with $11.36B of customer prepayments

To keep the buildout moving, the Big Four slashed buybacks by 83% through the first nine months of 2026 (from $81.0B down to $13.6B, all from Microsoft) and leaned hard into debt markets.

Source - Reuters / LSEG (Oct 30, 2026)

Source - T. Rowe Price (2026) And only one of them has given specific 2027 CapEx guidance (ORCL at $90B to $95B for its fiscal 2027, which ends in May).

Street consensus is already at $1.1T for the Big Five next year (Goldman is at $1.2T, Morgan Stanley up to $1.4T), up from ~$800B this year. Morgan Stanley Investment Management has combined trailing FCF (after deducting SBC) hitting -$265B by Q3 2027.

Once Big Tech stopped self-funding, lenders became the real speed limit. And recently lenders started pushing back.

In public bonds, Big Five sales hit $277B through early November (and $680B+ across the wider AI computing ecosystem per the Fed), and bond buyers quickly started charging way more for weaker balance sheets. During a $66.9B bond rush in late October, Microsoft borrowed for 20 years at 6.15%, Meta paid 7.35%, and Oracle had to pay 8.65% after walking back its June pledge not to issue more debt this year.

On top of public bonds, trillions sit off the balance sheet, with the Fed estimating $3T+ in off-balance-sheet AI obligations and both the Bank of England and ECB flagging $4T+ in circular deals and guarantees in November.

Just four companies (Microsoft $364.6B, Meta $355.7B, Oracle $288.0B, Alphabet $130.5B) sit on $1.14T in signed data center leases that have not even started yet, while Nvidia disclosed up to $118B in backstop guarantees ($105B tied to OpenAI).

That reliance on outside capital showed up fast in private credit, where zero U.S. data center debt packages priced for three straight weeks through mid-November as lenders demanded higher yields.

Around the same time, big private-credit funds (Blackstone, Ares, BlackRock, Carlyle, and Blue Owl) hit their 5% quarterly withdrawal caps as clients got nervous about AI debt and legacy software exposure.

Meanwhile, the debt-heavy AI cloud startups are already feeling it directly, with CoreWeave’s 2031 bonds dropping to ~75 cents on the dollar in late November and pushing its borrowing costs to ~17% to 18%, higher than what its contracts earn.

CoreWeave carries $47.5B in debt ($13.4B due before YE 2027), is running at $4.2B to $4.6B a year in net interest, and is seeing GPU lenders slash collateral advance rates from 80% down to 50% to 60%, while the cost to insure Oracle’s debt hit a record high the same week.

Underneath all of it sits depreciation, because between 2020 and early 2025 Big Tech stretched server lives on paper from 3 years to nearly 6 years, yet Q3 depreciation still jumped 46% YoY against 18% rev growth.

Uptime Institute’s 2026 survey puts real server life closer to 3.4 years and Nvidia drops a faster chip every year.

While Goldman Sachs estimates hyperscalers need $308B in annual AI rev just to break even on 2026 to 2027 CapEx (and $417B to $526B for a 10% to 20% return).

Source - Goldman Sachs Global Institute (2026) Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta still own the best core businesses on earth. In my opinion, long term they will be just fine. And that’s why I am heavily invested there and will continue to build over time. (I could be wrong of course).

What DOES change is the odds of a normal capex digestion phase, a credit slowdown in AI cloud orders, or a permitting pause. Those are becoming more more real concerns.

Who Is Paying the Tax (and When It Breaks)

While hyperscalers spent hundreds of billions fighting for chips and power this year, they starved everyone else of shared parts.

Memory is the cleanest (and most recent, painful, sigh AMBA) example. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) for AI servers eats 3x to 4x as much factory capacity per unit as standard memory. TrendForce and Samsung see AI memory taking 23% of total production across the top three makers in 2026 and nearing 30% in 2027.

DigiTimes estimates roughly 70% of memory allocation now goes to AI servers, leaving 30% for PCs, phones, medical scanners, networking gear, industrial hardware etc etc.

Source - TrendForce (2026) Standard memory contract prices jumped 450%+ in eleven months. Older-generation memory now costs MORE than newer memory because the big three started shutting down older lines.

Micron just printed an 87% gross margin. SK hynix printed an 80% operating margin.

Someone had to pay for those margins. It was regular hardware and enterprise tech companies.

So when does it crack? Memory makers are sitting on tens of billions in customer deposits and financial guarantees (at Micron and SanDisk) and say 2027 AI memory is sold out.

But the different bottlenecks we will discuss today do not ease together. They (potentially) break in waves over the next 18 to 36 months.

First to possibly crack (Q1 to Q2 2027) are flash storage, older-generation memory spot prices, and copper. TrendForce reported in mid-November that flash storage spot prices fell 20%+ in two weeks after Chinese cloud providers cut 2027 server storage orders, pushing TrendForce to call for negative contract pricing in Q1 and Q2 2027.

On Oct 30, Kioxia proved “100% sold out” for 2027 does not protect pricing when buyers blink, guiding Q3 operating profit ~15% below consensus.

Older-generation memory spot is already down 8% in four weeks.

Source - TrendForce (Nov 2026) Second to crack (2H 2027) might be GPU rental rates and mainstream memory contracts. Net-new memory fabs come online in the second half of 2027 (led by Micron’s Idaho fab and SK hynix expansion) right as CoreWeave’s 2027 debt wall and Nvidia’s next chip generation hit older GPU fleets. BNP Paribas Exane just downgraded Micron on Nov 24 (slashing its target 54% from $1,700 to $775), calling for mid-2027 supply to catch demand.

Tightest longest (2028 to 2030+) are transformers (2.5 to 3+ year waits per Wood Mackenzie), gas turbines (booked into 2030 to 2032), electrical labor, and local permitting.

Now remember, voters backed data center restrictions on 98% of decided 2026 local ballot measures, and Orennia estimates 191 GW of planned U.S. capacity now faces regulatory risk.

On top of that, PJM (the biggest U.S. power grid, covering Northern Virginia and the Mid-Atlantic) ran its 2028/2029 power auction, hit the legal maximum price allowed, and still came up nearly 7 GW short of the electricity it needs. Without the price cap, prices would have jumped another 70%. Even maxing out power prices cannot get new plants built fast enough, which means grid and permitting walls are going to keep slowing down data center openings well into 2028.

Source - Energy Ventures Analysis / PJM BRA (2026) Now think about this. Power, turbine, and permitting walls slow down data center openings faster than chipmakers slow down shipments.

If 2028 campuses slip past their hardware delivery dates, rigid power bottlenecks pull oversupply FORWARD into memory and cloud GPUs.

Four Live Signals for the Turn

Stocks may front-run margin turns by two to four quarters, so waiting for earnings calls is too late. Let’s keep some notes on what to watch.

1 - Flash storage spot vs contract prices on TrendForce already sit more than 20% negative in late November, and back-to-back quarterly contract drops in Q1 and Q2 2027 would confirm the turn.

2 - Mainstream memory spot on DRAMeXchange is right behind it, with older-generation memory already down 8% in four weeks and newer memory cracking below Q4 contract levels as the next tell.

3 - Executed GPU rental rates are already softening (Ornn’s B300 index fell 23.9% off its mid-October $14.23/hr peak to $10.83/hr), and tighter credit for operators like CoreWeave could force discounted rentals onto the open market.

4 - State-level data center moratoriums in 2027 legislative sessions and regional power auctions would be the quickest trigger to push back 2028 hyperscale hardware orders.

Now with all that out of the way, who could actually benefit from an easing of some of these constraints?
