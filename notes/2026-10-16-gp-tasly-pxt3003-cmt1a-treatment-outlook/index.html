SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion assessed an oral therapy designed to reduce PMP22 expression in Charcot-Marie-Tooth type 1A, a rare inherited neuropathy. Current management relies on genetic testing, electromyography, rehabilitation, symptom relief, and corrective surgery for deformities. No effective disease-modifying treatment is established, resulting in substantial unmet need and limited adherence to supportive care.

The expert considered the therapy promising, particularly for younger patients with early, mild-to-moderate disease, but noted that efficacy has not been consistently demonstrated across global trials.

Key Insights

Mild-to-moderate patients represent more than half of those seen in the expert’s practice and broadly resemble the trial population. The strongest apparent benefit involved lower-limb and distal muscle function, which is clinically relevant because CMT commonly affects the legs more severely than the arms. Upper-limb electrophysiology showed limited improvement. The expert noted that nerve-conduction changes can lag behind functional recovery. Earlier PMP22 suppression may be critical because advanced disease can progress from demyelination to largely irreversible axonal injury. A longer trial of approximately two years could better detect treatment effects in this slowly progressing disease. Differing ethnicity, baseline severity, disease duration, and multicenter quality control may help explain inconsistent regional trial outcomes. Adoption will depend heavily on price, reimbursement, and access. Oral administration is a meaningful convenience advantage.

QS: Management mainly consists of diagnosis and treatment. For severe cases involving deformities, orthopedic or rehabilitation specialists may be consulted for corrective interventions. KH: Regarding efficacy, the study reported that at Month 15, the change in ONLS score was -0.268 in the treatment group versus 0.013 in the placebo group, with a between-group difference of -0.249 and a p-value of 0.0257. How should we interpret these findings and their clinical significance?

QS: These results indicate that the treatment group experienced a decline from baseline in the score, and the reduction was greater than that seen in the placebo group. The between-group difference was 0.249. Although it did not achieve what I would consider highly convincing clinical statistical significance, the disease itself progresses relatively slowly. The drug still appears to have an effect in slowing disease progression. If statistical significance was not fully demonstrated, one possibility is that the observation period or treatment duration was not long enough. Another possibility is that the sample size was relatively small. To achieve a statistically significant p-value, a larger enrollment size is often required. Nevertheless, even without a very big between-group statistical difference, I believe the results still have some clinical significance, as the treatment group showed slight improvement relative to baseline.

QS: Statistical significance was observed in the lower-limb measures as well as ankle dorsiflexion strength. However, upper-limb electrophysiological measures, such as those involving the ulnar and radial nerves, did not show significant differences. This suggests that the drug may have some effect on improving lower-limb muscle strength, particularly distal muscle strength, whereas improvement in the upper limbs is less obvious. Because the overall composite score is influenced by upper-limb performance, the overall improvement may appear less pronounced. Since Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease most commonly affects the lower limbs, and generally affects them more severely than the upper limbs, the drug appears to provide some benefit specifically for lower-limb function.

KH: The study also discussed electrophysiological parameters, such as ulnar and radial nerve assessments, which did not show significant differences. How should this be interpreted?

QS: Electrophysiological changes, especially in peripheral nerves, often lag behind clinical improvements. We commonly observe this phenomenon in peripheral neuropathies, where patients exhibit clinical recovery, but the electromyographic findings do not improve in parallel. That may explain these results. In addition, the measurements focused on the upper limbs, where improvement appears less pronounced than in the lower limbs.

KH: The study also reported responder rates. How should those be interpreted?

QS: The responder rate refers to the proportion of patients who responded to treatment, meaning those who experienced improvement. In this study, a response was defined as an improvement of at least one point in the score. Although the responder rate was higher than in the placebo group, it did not reach statistical significance. The overall score totals 12 points, with 5 points attributed to upper-limb function and 7 points to lower-limb function. An improvement of more than one point was considered clinically meaningful. More than 80% of placebo-treated patients did not worsen, highlighting how slowly the disease progresses. If the observation period were extended, the likelihood of obtaining more favorable results might be higher.

KH: The trial duration was 15 months. What observation period do you think would be more appropriate?

QS: In many studies, following patients for around two years, or 24 months, tends to be more appropriate.

KH: This Phase 3 study was conducted in China but was also preceded by Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies in France. Those studies did not meet their primary endpoint. The publications suggested several possible reasons for this outcome. How should we interpret the inconsistency among these trial results?

QS: Patients enrolled in China were predominantly Han Chinese, whereas the French studies involved a global population. Genetic and ethnic differences among patients can affect drug responses. Pharmacogenomic differences across populations may therefore contribute to the discrepancy. Beyond that, it is difficult to draw firm conclusions. The publications also mentioned multicenter-related quality-control issues, which could have had an impact, though that is difficult to assess. Differences in disease severity may also have played a role. I have not reviewed the studies in detail, but variation in disease severity can certainly contribute to differences between trial outcomes.

From a theoretical perspective, earlier suppression of PMP22 expression should be more beneficial because it may reduce the extent of nerve damage. Once nerve damage progresses beyond a certain point, the disease may evolve from demyelination to secondary axonal damage. Recovery from axonal damage is extremely difficult. As a result, differences in disease stage at enrollment, disease duration, and baseline severity scores can all affect trial outcomes. I believe these are important factors.

KH: Are there any potential risks associated with gene-downregulation therapies such as this one?

QS: Yes, there are potential risks. If PMP22 expression is reduced excessively, published literature suggests it could lead to another condition called hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies (HNPP), or tomaculous neuropathy. In such cases, enlarged peripheral nerves may be palpable at certain anatomical locations. That risk does exist.

KH: Based on the efficacy and safety data reported so far, what is your overall impression of the drug? What proportion of patients do you think could potentially benefit from treatment with PXT3003?

QS: This drug is intended primarily for patients with CMT1A, which accounts for roughly half of all Charcot-Marie- Tooth disease cases. These patients typically have a relatively long disease course, and currently there are no particularly effective treatments available. Based on these results, the drug appears capable of improving outcomes and modifying disease progression in some patients, particularly with respect to lower-limb muscle strength. However, definitive efficacy has not yet been demonstrated globally.

I believe patient selection will be important. Patients with early-stage, mild disease are likely to benefit most. Strictly speaking, patients should be selected according to the trial’s enrollment criteria. If the drug continues to advance, it would likely target patients with CMTNS-v2 scores between 2 and 18, representing relatively mild disease, since these patients are more likely to benefit.

Whether patients ultimately use the drug will depend largely on cost-effectiveness, pricing, and accessibility. If patients can afford the treatment, I believe a substantial proportion would be willing to use it. However, because this is a rare disease, the investment required to develop the drug is significant, so the price will likely be relatively high. Access would increase considerably if the drug were included in the medical reimbursement list. Since it is an oral therapy, administration is relatively convenient. Most importantly, there have previously been no disease-targeted treatments available. This therapy directly addresses an underlying disease mechanism, so this type of gene-targeted treatment does have promising potential.
