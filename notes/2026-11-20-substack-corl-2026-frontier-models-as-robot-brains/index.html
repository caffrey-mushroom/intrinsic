CoRL 2026, a leading academic conference in robot learning, was held in Austin, Texas, from November 9 to 12. FUNDA attended throughout, including talks by NVIDIA and Google DeepMind and live demonstrations at several company booths. This report draws on CoRL presentations, exhibit-floor observations and publicly available research.

Executive Summary

  1. The robot’s brain is increasingly being supplied by general-purpose frontier models, while competition within

robotics is shifting toward action models, data and real-world deployment. By frontier models, we mean general-purpose models such as GPT, Gemini and Claude. The brain interprets instructions and plans steps; the action model generates motion in real time. Improvements in brain capabilities are coming mainly from frontier-model companies, leaving robotics companies with limited scope to build a durable advantage at that layer. They still face three questions. The action model must be fast and inexpensive, but NVIDIA plans to make its own action model available for free, limiting the scope for standalone monetization.

  1. First-person video is now inexpensive to obtain; tactile data and on-site data that can only be accumulated through

real-world deployment remain scarce. Robots have entered factories but still require human intervention. We maintain our view that humanoid robots will reach industrial scale around 2030. Compute demand should grow before robot shipments ramp, driven mainly by frontier-model inference and simulation data generation.

The brain: moving to frontier models

Asking a robot to put a cup from the table into the dishwasher requires two kinds of work. The first is thinking: understanding the instruction, recognizing the objects on the table, breaking the task into steps such as picking up the cup, opening the dishwasher and placing the cup inside, then adjusting the plan if something goes wrong. The second is moving: turning each step into specific hand and arm movements, potentially adjusted dozens or even hundreds of times per second. The industry generally calls the first capability the brain and the second the action model. Robotics companies used to train both themselves; increasingly, the brain is being supplied by frontier models. Although these models were not developed specifically for robots, their visual understanding, reasoning and code-generation capabilities can already be applied directly to robotics. They outperform many in-house robot-brain models and continue to improve rapidly.

At the conference, NVIDIA introduced GR00T 2, an action model designed to work with a frontier model that breaks tasks down, monitors execution and diagnoses failures. Jim Fan, one of NVIDIA’s robotics research leads, said complex tasks cannot be handled by OpenAI’s Astra alone or NVIDIA’s own GR00T alone; the two need to work together. This reflects the industry’s growing acceptance of frontier models for task understanding and planning. On NVIDIA’s figures, the success rate on the same set of tasks rose from 40% without a frontier model to 92% with one (vendor-reported).

At Google’s booth, Apptronik’s humanoid robot Apollo took instructions directly from visitors. A screen beside it showed how the robot interpreted each instruction and the action it planned to take. One visitor asked whether it could high-five itself. The screen first showed the robot preparing to clap, then displayed its reply: “I can definitely give myself a high five, which basically looks like a clap.” The frontier model generated the response on the spot. It was one of the more engaging live demonstrations we observed at the conference.

OpenAI and Anthropic had no product demonstrations or talks at the conference, yet many research projects used their models. We saw no booth demonstrations or presentations from either company. OpenAI was a conference sponsor; according to Fortune, the booth registered in its name stood empty during the event. Anthropic was not a sponsor. Meanwhile, the workshop on controlling robots with frontier models was among the conference’s most closely watched sessions, with 39 accepted papers. Research from NVIDIA, Berkeley, ByteDance and others used Astra or Claude as the robot’s brain or as a research tool. Progress in the robot’s brain is increasingly coming from frontier-model companies outside the robotics industry.

Subscribed

Fortune reported on November 13 that OpenAI had moved its robotics researchers into the main research team developing general-purpose models. An OpenAI spokesperson said the company had initially believed robots would require a purpose-built model, but later found that a general-purpose model such as Astra could be applied directly. OpenAI said robotics remains a long-term priority and that it still plans to publicly demonstrate its own humanoid robot in 2027. The report also noted that OpenAI is scaling back non-core projects and preparing for a possible IPO, making cost control one factor behind the move. The organizational change suggests that OpenAI now views robotics as an application of its general-purpose models rather than a reason to maintain a separate robotics foundation-model team. NVIDIA also tested Astra writing its own programs to control a robot arm across 120 pick-and-place tasks, with a 74.8% success rate. The best specialized robot model on the same tasks had achieved 51%, more than 20 percentage points lower. The test has a limitation: it was conducted in computer simulation and has not been validated on a physical robot. It is still informative, however, because the models Astra outperformed included NVIDIA’s own robot model.

Frontier models are also being used to train action models and accelerate robotics research. In training, ByteDance and other teams first had a frontier model complete tasks in simulation, then used its actions as demonstrations for a smaller model; in one study, the smaller model’s success rate rose from 18% to 69%. In R&D, NVIDIA and other teams had a frontier model manage eight robots at once, rewrite training programs and schedule retries autonomously. One peg-insertion task reached near-perfect success in about 40 minutes. Google said its action model took 103 days to move from the previous version to the current one, compared with 308 days for the preceding iteration, largely because frontier models assisted its robotics research. The pace of action-model development is therefore increasingly tied to frontier-model capabilities.

It is still too early to determine which company has the strongest robot brain. OpenAI’s models rank near the top in most third-party tests, but the company currently provides the brain rather than its own action model. Google is the only company that develops both the brain and the action model in-house and has already put robots into factory trials. When Anthropic introduced Fable 5.2 on November 9, it reported a robot-simulation score of 64.9%, compared with 27.6% for the previous generation. On the same results, OpenAI’s Astra scored 29.8%, less than half of Fable 5.2’s score. The results came from a test organized and published by Anthropic itself and have not been independently verified, so they should be treated as indicative.

On November 17, OpenAI released its next-generation model, GPT-6.2 Astra. Artificial Analysis subsequently updated its physical robot-arm benchmark: GPT-6.2 Astra scored 79 and ranked first, while Fable 5.2 scored 75, marking its first result in a third-party robotics test. In the same test in mid-October, the previous Astra scored 69.4 and the previous Fable scored 46.3. OpenAI’s score rose by about 10 points in roughly a month. Anthropic’s progress was also confirmed by a third-party test, although the improvement was less pronounced than its own results had suggested.

Frontier models still have two clear limitations: response time and inference cost. Astra takes more than ten seconds to make a decision, compared with less than 0.05 seconds for a specialized action model. The high scores cited above were obtained under a special test condition: the test pauses while the frontier model thinks and resumes after it returns an answer. A real-world task cannot be paused to wait for the model. A Tsinghua University researcher presented a comparison at the conference: on the same household tasks, a frontier model completed more than 60% with no time limit, but only 15% to 20% when required to finish within 50 seconds. Cost is the other constraint. In NVIDIA’s test, the frontier-model inference charges for Astra to complete one pick-and-place task were about $36; on a third-party physical-robot leaderboard, a specialized model cost about $0.15 per task. The figures come from different tests and the tasks are not identical, so they should not be compared directly. Even so, the gap shows that direct robot control with a frontier model is currently far more expensive than using a specialized model.

We expect the robot’s brain to be supplied mainly by a handful of frontier-model companies, leaving robotics companies with limited scope to build a durable advantage at that layer. The ranking among the leading providers may change, but it does not alter this conclusion. Because frontier models remain slow and expensive, specialized action models will still be needed for real-time motion.

The action model: speed and low cost are essential, but difficult to monetize on their own

For real-time motion control, specialized action models still outperform frontier models and cost much less. The third-party RoboCurve leaderboard tests different models on the same tasks using a physical robot arm. Dream-1, a dedicated model developed by robotics startup Dream Labs, ranks first, with a 59.3% success rate and a cost of about $0.15 per task. Google’s and OpenAI’s frontier models rank lower, at 48.2% and 42.5%, with per-task costs of about $2.30 to $3.50—more than ten times the cost of the specialized model. In the near term, frontier models are unlikely to replace action models for real-time motion control.

NVIDIA has made a substantial investment in action models and plans to release GR00T 2 for free. It was among the most active companies at the conference, with several prominent papers from its research team. In his keynote, Jim Fan said robotics research was “no longer an elitist sport.” NVIDIA presented three sets of self-test results for GR00T 2. First, across 50 new tasks it had never encountered during training, the model achieved an average success rate of 40% without additional training. Second, with just five human demonstrations per task and half an hour of training on a standard gaming GPU, the success rate rose to 95%. Third, NVIDIA has an accelerated version that can run directly on a robot and output an action about every 0.12 seconds. The speed improvement comes at the expense of accuracy: on fine-manipulation tasks requiring a dexterous hand, the success rate falls from 68.6% to 34.3%. NVIDIA plans to make the model freely available in December. The specific license terms have not been published, so commercial use remains to be confirmed. The accompanying 100,000 hours of human video data and simulation-training tools are already available, and the video-data license permits commercial use.

GR00T 2 follows a technical approach known as the world action model, which differs from conventional action models. A conventional action model takes the current frame and outputs an action directly. It is relatively fast, producing an output about every 0.05 seconds, but relies mainly on robot-demonstration data, which is limited in volume and expensive to collect. A world action model is built on a video-generation model: it first predicts how the scene will change over the next few seconds, then uses that prediction to decide what action to take. This allows it to learn how objects move from large volumes of human video. GR00T 2 used about 400,000 hours of such video. The trade-off is slower inference: in NVIDIA’s tests, a world action model took about 0.7 seconds to generate each output.

NVIDIA’s paper reports that, on tasks not seen during training, the world action model achieved 39.5% task progress, compared with 16.3% for the best-performing conventional action model. In independent tests conducted by the University of Hong Kong, Berkeley and other institutions, however, conventional action models performed better, with a success rate of 8.8%; all world action models scored below 4%. The advantage of world action models has therefore not yet been established by independent testing, and the strongest supporting evidence still comes from NVIDIA’s own results.

On the final day of the conference, an NVIDIA researcher and a professor from TU Darmstadt debated with Chelsea Finn, co-founder of Physical Intelligence, and Vincent Sitzmann of MIT on whether robots need world models. Before the debate, most audience members voted that robots do need world models; after the debate, the result shifted to the opposing side. The opposing argument was that an action model only needs to learn the actions a robot will actually perform, while a world model must predict the consequences of many possible actions, making it harder to train. Finn concluded that world models are worth exploring and may help evaluate robot performance, but are not essential.

NVIDIA and Google take different approaches to action models, while independent model companies sit between them. NVIDIA is opening up its action model and allowing customers to choose their own brain. Its business model does not depend on charging for the model; instead, it aims to drive sales of chips and simulation-training tools. Google takes the opposite approach: it charges for its frontier models and keeps its action model mainly for partners. Developers can pay to use Google’s two models for robotics, while Gemini Robotics 2.5 is available only to a small group of partner robotics companies; others must join a waitlist. Google argues that different systems can sit downstream from its frontier model, including Google’s own action model, a robot maker’s control system, or programs written directly by the frontier model. This makes the action model relatively replaceable. Robot makers are also developing their own action models. Figure previously worked with OpenAI; after ending the partnership in 2025, it shifted to training its own models and signed a $3.5 billion compute contract. NVIDIA’s open model, Google’s partner-led approach and robot makers’ in-house development all put pressure on independent action-model vendors. Fewer customers may be willing to pay separately for a general-purpose action model.

Our observations at CoRL suggested that robotics foundation-model startups had a lower profile this year, while deployment, on-site data and customer stickiness featured more prominently in public sessions and company presentations. Capital is still entering the sector: Bloomberg reported that Physical Intelligence was in talks to raise at least $1 billion at a valuation of about $20 billion, while Dream Labs raised a $90 million seed round.

The technical direction for action models has not yet converged. NVIDIA is currently betting on the world action model and plans to release GR00T 2 for free. The outcome of the technical competition remains uncertain, and companies that choose the wrong approach may need to reinvest. Free access to general-purpose action models would also put pressure on standalone model pricing. Robotics-model startups still have room to develop, but their business models may diverge into three groups: companies with real deployment sites and a steady flow of on-site data; companies that turn action models into standard interfaces that frontier models can call, building customer data in the process; and companies that are acquired by larger businesses. AMD completed its acquisition of World Labs on November 16. This is one example of the third path.

Data: first-person video is already inexpensive; tactile and deployment data remain scarce

Publicly available CoRL presentations and demonstrations highlight three main categories of robot-training data: first-person video, handheld-gripper data and tactile data. For comparison, NVIDIA used about 10,000 hours of handheld-gripper data to train GR00T 2, while public tactile datasets contain only a few thousand hours.

Teleoperation data is difficult to scale because each hour of data requires one operator and one physical robot for an hour. NVIDIA has released 100,000 hours of human video data, providing a public reference point for the scale of its data release.

First-person video has become the largest source of data for robot training. GR00T 2 was trained on about 440,000 hours of data, including roughly 400,000 hours of first-person video, or about 92% of the total; robot teleoperation data accounted for about 5%. Forbes reported that Figure disclosed at a recent all-hands meeting that its in-house collection platform had received 37.5 million human-video clips and that weekly data spending was about $8.1 million. Chief executive Brett Adcock summarized the strategy by saying, “Pre-training is what will win.” Media reports also said that first-person video prices had fallen significantly this year, while orders in the latest two weeks exceeded the total for the previous two months.

Handheld-gripper data is collected with a camera-equipped gripper that records an operator’s grasping motions. Compared with first-person video, it provides more accurate information about grasping actions. First-person video only allows algorithms to infer hand movements from images, with limited precision, making fine manipulation difficult to learn. NVIDIA has also released hardware designs for two handheld grippers. Because collection uses a handheld device rather than tying up a physical robot, it may be easier to scale than teleoperation.

Tactile data is another scarce resource, and it is still collected mainly through hour-by-hour human demonstrations. At Sharpa’s booth, an operator wearing a headset and exoskeleton gloves remotely controlled a five-fingered robot hand to pick up a plush toy. Apollo 2, used in Google’s Mercedes-Benz pilot, is also equipped with Sharpa’s 22-degree-of-freedom five-fingered hand. Fine operations such as inserting connectors, driving screws and grasping soft objects are difficult to perform using vision alone, making tactile feedback necessary. GR00T 2 does not yet support tactile input. One CoRL study used 100 hours of tactile data and achieved more than 30% better performance than the strongest baseline across 12 tasks requiring force sensing or involving deformable objects. In another third-party test, Astra failed all 11 attempts to insert a plug using a physical robot. Tactile input remains a capability that action models need to develop. The study results underscore its value for tasks that require force sensing or involve deformable objects.

Data generated during robot deployment cannot be obtained through procurement alone. In his conference talk, Russ Tedrake, MIT professor and chief executive of robotics startup Walden Robotics, divided data into two categories (as reported by industry media). The first can be purchased: human video, handheld-gripper data and outsourced teleoperation data. These are available in the market and are difficult to use as a differentiator. The second category is generated when robots perform tasks at customer sites. It cannot be bought directly; it accumulates as robots are deployed in factories and operate in real conditions. Tedrake showed that more capable foundation models lead to fewer human interventions on site, while the data generated by each intervention can be more valuable for subsequent model improvements. He also expects large-scale model training to become concentrated among a small number of well-funded companies, as has happened with language models.

The data bottleneck is shifting from raw video to tactile input and real-world deployment data. First-person video still has room to become cheaper and more abundant. Handheld-gripper and tactile data remain expensive, and large-scale procurement is only beginning. Teleoperation data is the most expensive and is constrained by the availability of people and equipment. Deployment data can only be accumulated at customer sites. The next stage of differentiation will depend more on which companies get robots into factories and keep them operating; purchasing data alone is no longer enough to establish an advantage.

Real-world deployment: robots are in factories but still need people

Google’s robots have entered trials at Mercedes-Benz factories with a reported success rate of 96%, but the work remains at the proof-of-concept stage. Google says Apptronik’s humanoid robot Apollo 2 is performing parts-kitting tasks at two Mercedes-Benz plants, selecting the parts needed for a vehicle and assembling them into a set, with a 96% success rate. At the site, we saw Google describe the work as a “proof of concept” and explain that the robot retries after an error. A task that fails on the first attempt but succeeds after a retry is therefore counted as a success. Google has not disclosed the number of trials, the length of continuous operation or the frequency of human intervention.

A 96% success rate is still below what factories require. If a workstation performs 1,000 operations a day, a 4% failure rate would mean about 40 cases requiring human intervention each day. Common problems include parts shifting out of position, changes in lighting and objects slipping during grasping. Each individual issue may be uncommon, but the range of possible exceptions is wide. A Google robotics lead told the media that the remaining 4% is a “steep road.” Based on the approaches presented at CoRL, there are three main ways to improve the remaining success rate. First, robots can practice repeatedly on site. NVIDIA’s self-test results show success rates rising from 40%–76% to 88%–98% on some fine-manipulation tasks. Second, robots can accumulate data while working and use it to update the model. Third, a remote human operator can take over when the robot cannot handle a task.

Compute: investment comes ahead of volume

Our first report argued that an individual robot-model training run is much smaller than one for a frontier model, and that incremental compute demand comes mainly from data processing, simulation, evaluation and repeated iterations. The findings from this trip support that view and point to an additional source of demand: frontier-model inference.

Action-model training remains relatively limited in scale, and its cost is declining. NVIDIA trained GR00T 2 using 1,024 GB300 chips, a much smaller scale than a frontier-model training cluster. According to industry media, one paper shortlisted for this year’s best-paper award trained a humanoid robot’s whole-body controller on a single H100 chip in just three hours. Comparable work by NVIDIA previously required about 21,000 GPU-hours.

Frontier-model inference is another source of incremental compute demand. In NVIDIA’s test, a frontier model wrote its own program to complete a simulated task, consuming about 30.9 million tokens. More than 95% of the task’s total time was spent on frontier-model inference. This type of compute use is already occurring in R&D, including the model-assisted training and development described above. If frontier models are deployed as the brain of robots, each robot task may generate ongoing cloud-inference costs, depending on how frequently the frontier model is called. Google’s system offers one reference point: the brain is called once per second, while the action model also runs on a server.

Inference costs for action models are also rising. Each inference step by a world action model is substantially more computationally expensive than one by a conventional action model. In NVIDIA’s tests, the former took about 0.7 seconds per output, compared with about 0.05 seconds for the latter. Compressing a world action model so that it can run in real time on a chip inside the robot remains an important technical challenge.

Simulation data generation is the third source of compute demand. Google said its system uses a frontier model to generate digital twins from real-world images and then runs thousands of simulations for training. After training entirely on simulated data, NVIDIA’s humanoid robot completed a popcorn-making task successfully 48 times in a row (vendor-reported). A study published by Apple researchers in early November trained a humanoid robot’s whole-body movements entirely in simulation. The robot completed 360 tests across six previously unseen real-world environments, with a 91% success rate. The tasks included whole-body movements such as sitting, crawling and pushing boxes, but not dexterous manipulation. Simulation data can scale with compute and is not directly constrained by human data-collection capacity.

Chip companies are also preparing for this demand. On the third day of the conference, World Labs founder Fei-Fei Li delivered a keynote, followed by a discussion with AMD chief executive Lisa Su. It was their first public appearance together since AMD completed its acquisition of World Labs on November 16. According to media reports, Li introduced Atlas, a world model that can reconstruct or generate three-dimensional scenes with freely changeable viewpoints from a small number of photographs. The team is testing Atlas for robot simulation training but has not disclosed any robotics customers or application results. Su said robotics will become an important source of compute demand in the next phase. AMD has built a sizable robotics research team, although the effort remains at an early stage; she did not announce a new robotics chip.

Our assessment is that near-term compute demand from robotics will come mainly from frontier-model inference and simulation data generation, while action-model training remains relatively limited in scale. These demands arise during R&D and do not need to wait for large-scale robot shipments, so they may grow ahead of end-market volume. Once robots are deployed at scale, continuous frontier-model inference and chips inside the robots will become the next sources of demand.

CoRL 2026 shows that frontier models are taking on more of the work involved in understanding instructions and planning tasks for robots. The robotics industry still needs to solve three practical problems: making action models fast and inexpensive, securing tactile and real-world deployment data, and reducing the frequency of human intervention on factory floors. As frontier models improve, spending on data purchases, inference and simulation compute during R&D may increase before end-market shipments ramp. As deployment expands, cloud inference and on-device chips should become the next sources of demand.
