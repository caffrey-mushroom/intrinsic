Anthropic and the AI spending question

They reportedly generated $16.8B of revenue in Q3, up about 45% QoQ. That’s an additional $5.2B of quarterly revenue. The previous quarter added about $6.9B, so there is a real slowdown in the pace of growth to discuss. I understand why people are watching that closely when so much infrastructure spending depends on these businesses continuing to expand.

But at the same investor meeting, Anthropic reportedly said it expects to reach a $110B annualized revenue run rate by year-end (this is cited from someone attending but it is up for debate whether or not this is true). If it is true I think that deserves a lot of weight. They’re saying this in the middle of October, with a much better view of their customers’ spending than any of us have.

The $100B debate has been about the annualized pace they reach at year-end. At $110B, they would be bringing in roughly $9.2B a month if that figure is based on December revenue. September’s actual pace would tell us how much more growth they need to get there. We don’t have that number.

If September was strong and they deliver that year-end expectation, I think the argument that demand is falling apart becomes much harder to make. It would give us more confidence that a major buyer of compute is growing into the capacity it has committed to.

We also got encouraging information on profitability. The Information reports an 11% adjusted operating margin, excluding costs including stock compensation. The FT reports that Anthropic told investors it was cash flow positive in Q3. The cash-flow amount and definition weren’t disclosed in that report, so we’ll need the financial statements to understand it properly.

I think competition is absolutely worth watching. OpenAI has been gaining momentum in OpenRouter’s data, and cheaper models give customers more options. Ramp saw token spending flatten in August and September even as usage increased, followed by a new spending peak in early October.

I can definitely see cheaper AI making a lot more applications worthwhile. Businesses might use it for tasks they would never have paid to automate at the old price. Providers are also getting more efficient, which can help them make money while charging less. We need to see how those changes play out together rather than assume a lower price automatically means worse economics.

Where I think we deserve to be more cautious is the amount of spending already committed. Reuters reported roughly $518B of Anthropic compute commitments over a decade, with about 80% noncancelable or payable regardless of usage. Many of its customers have more flexibility to change their spending. That puts pressure on Anthropic to keep attracting enough business to use the capacity it’s paying for. The payment schedule stretches over years, and its timing will be important.

That’s why I want to see both continued growth and evidence that the growth is producing cash. A strong September exit rate followed by the $110B year-end pace would be a meaningful step toward answering the demand question. The financial statements will help us understand how much money they retain as that demand grows.

CoreWeave

A lot of the attention has been on CoreWeave’s bond yield rising toward 15%. That tells us investors are demanding a much higher return to own that debt, which is a warning about confidence and the potential cost of future borrowing. The bond’s existing coupon is fixed at 9.625%, so their payments on that bond haven’t suddenly jumped to 15%.

They need substantial outside funding because they’re building so quickly. In Q2, operating cash flow was about $679M while cash spending on property and equipment was roughly $6.4B. They’ve raised additional financing since then, but those figures show how dependent this stage of expansion is on continued access to capital.

Some of their projects have stronger protection than that bond yield might suggest. Their Meta-linked financing announced in March included fixed-rate funding initially priced around 5.9%, with a structure designed to repay the loan during the customer contract. An August facility had a five-year loan against customer contracts averaging about three years, which leaves more of a renewal question. The customer agreement behind each project can make a substantial difference to how comfortably the debt gets repaid.

For the semiconductor businesses we follow, we should always be watching whether funding pressure starts changing deployment schedules or equipment purchases. That would give us a much more direct reason to revisit supplier earnings expectations. Until then, the financing stress tells us the market is becoming less comfortable paying for the next stage of growth. Which in itself could significantly suppress our returns.

I think that warrants patience when it comes to declaring this trade over or whatever. I want to see that these projects can keep getting funded on terms that leave worthwhile returns for shareholders. Getting a project built and earning an attractive return on it are both important when we’re deciding what these businesses are worth.

What else I’m watching this week

Today’s inflation report didn’t make the funding conversation any easier. Core CPI rose 0.3% in September against expectations for 0.2%. Persistent inflation makes it harder to rely on lower interest rates to improve the economics of expensive projects. And even if Treasury yields ease, lenders can still demand a bigger premium from a borrower they’re becoming more worried about.

We get PPI and retail sales tomorrow, October 15, at 5.30 a.m. Pacific. Those will give us more information on inflation and how well consumer spending is holding up. Kevin Warsh’s IMF conversation follows Thursday evening at 8.30 p.m. Pacific, ahead of the Fed’s October 28 decision. I’ll be watching how the data and commentary affect expectations for rates, because financing costs are becoming a larger part of this discussion.

ASML gave us some encouraging evidence from the equipment side today. Management said its expected capacity for 2027 and 2028 remains below customer demand and is considering further expansion. That’s a useful indication of what customers are planning while the market debates the financing of those plans.

TSMC’s earnings conference is at 11 p.m. Pacific. Just a couple hours from when I am writign this. I want to hear whether customer demand and capacity plans still support its growth outlook.

Further out, Meta reports on October 21 and Microsoft on October 28. Their commentary should help us understand whether spending plans are changing and how the additional infrastructure is contributing to their businesses.

Semtech tomorrow

Semtech’s data-center analyst day is tomorrow, October 15, starting at 9 a.m. Pacific. I want to hear the CFO’s financial framework at 1 p.m. and get a better sense of what the next few years could look like financially.

FY28 adjusted-EPS consensus has risen from $3.92 roughly two months ago to $5.86, about a 50% increase, with revenue expectations around $1.93B. Most of that revision was already in place a month ago. So there’s already a lot more growth in the estimates than there was before August’s earnings.

I want to hear how the next wave of products and customers builds on that. More hyperscalers moving copper designs into production would broaden the business, while the 1.6T ramp and CW lasers create additional opportunities. Management previously expected CW lasers for transceivers to begin contributing in the first half of FY28, so updates on qualifications and shipment timing would help put those opportunities into the earnings model.

The new Tower agreement adds dedicated SiGe capacity after management warned that secured supply might fall short in the second half of FY28. Tomorrow should give us a better sense of how much growth that capacity can support and the investment required. I’m looking for a framework connecting those revenue ramps, margins and R&D spending, with enough detail to see what could move FY28 estimates higher and what comes through in FY29 and beyond.

Hopefully its a good catalyst but I would expect the macro backdrop to be the deciding factor when it comes to stock movement in the short term.

What we learned from GFS

GFS Just Got a New Way to Grow

Gaetano

·

Oct 14

Read full story

The GFS update is especially interesting in a market so focused on the cost of financing new capacity. Japan would provide up to $21B for two new fabs at Malta, with approximately $10B going toward the first facility, which is expected to begin producing in 2029. GFS would supply the technology and run the operation. That gives the company a way to grow earnings while the governments fund the construction.

A special-purpose vehicle owned by the U.S. and Japanese governments would own the fabs and fund them through construction and ramp-up. GFS would receive technology and construction-management fees, followed by operating-management fees and a share of EBITDA as production builds. Sharing infrastructure with its existing Malta operation should also spread costs across a larger base. There are several ways for GFS to earn money here, and some begin well before the first wafers ship.

Technology fees are expected to start in 2027 and continue for at least 20 years. Management expects roughly $50M of revenue contribution in 2027, with annual diluted EPS accretion from the first fab of $0.10 to $0.15 that year, rising to $0.50 to $0.75 over the longer term. It also projects approximately $3B of cumulative adjusted free cash flow over the first ten years. Those numbers give us a much clearer sense of what the arrangement could mean for shareholders.

Management said profit per wafer over the first decade should be comparable to what GFS would earn if it funded the capacity itself. The returns would also be additive to the long-term model presented in May. I think that could be a very attractive way for GFS to expand. They can earn more from their technology and operating experience while keeping capital available for other opportunities.

There are still details to settle around the final agreements, accounting and shareholder impact. Commerce would receive approximately 4% of GFS equity, and $955M of previously awarded CHIPS funding is being redirected toward silicon photonics, silicon-germanium and advanced packaging. The EPS targets leave the share-count assumptions unclear, so that belongs in our assessment of the final economics. I like the direction of the arrangement, especially with the market becoming more sensitive to how semiconductor expansion gets funded.

Seeing GFS hold up so well today makes me wonder how much it would’ve ran if it was able to breakout of that $50 level. Regardless, if I am bullish on any networking-adjacent company right now for the next few quarters, GFS is up there.
