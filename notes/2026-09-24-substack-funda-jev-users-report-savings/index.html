原标题：Deep|LLM: Jev Users Report 10× Faster and 54.5× Cheaper Than the Models They Replaced; Only 3.7% in Production

#llm

Substack · FUNDA · 2026-09-24

Jev is a “decision model” from TypeSafe AI, released September 15, 2026 and opened to all users on September 20. It does not generate text. It answers questions with a fixed answer set: pick an option, score on a scale, or judge true/false, and attaches a confidence score. The launch quickly gathered industry interests, and some investors were asking whether it’s a significant negative to compute demand. As we addressed in our report earlier, we disagree with that concern and believes Jev is more of an interesting trial with limited impact on LLM.

1:14 AM

On September 22, Anthropic launched Claude Opus 5.5 at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens, 20% below Opus 5. Anthropic estimates that a typical customer workload costs 40% less to run. OpenAI launched GPT-6 Sol ($2/$10) and GPT-6 Luna ($0.10/$0.50) the same day, at prices 50–58% below the prevailing rates for the corresponding GPT-5.6 models and 60–92% below their July 9 launch prices. GPT-6 Luna is the first U.S. model since DeepSeek launched its V4 series to undercut DeepSeek’s cheapest model on both input and output list prices: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash costs $0.15/$0.60 even at off-peak rates. In the text, paired prices such as $4/$20 denote input/output charges per million tokens.

To analyze Jev further, we decided to have a deep dive into what Jev use cases are really about. This note covers 6,277 public discussions and use cases from the first 7 days; 2,153 are from people who actually used or tested it.

  • Demand sits on fast decisions with a fixed answer set. No single use clears 20%. Of the 1,284 cases with an identifiable use, the largest groups are real-time control in games, robots and simulations (18.8%), agent control decisions (16.1%) and content classification (15.7%).
  • Indie developers dominate the conversation; big-company engineers barely show up. Of the 2,140 authors whose role we could identify, 35.9% are indie developers, 23.4% are AI creators and KOLs, and just 2.7% are engineers at large companies.
  • Speed: 10× faster than the model it replaced or was tested against. Median user-reported speed-up is 10× (n=72): 10× vs frontier models, 5× vs small models. In the 16 cases with latency for both Jev and the prior system, Jev’s median is 300 ms vs 2,924 ms. The vendor’s 193.6× is a peak against the most expensive model.
  • Cost: 54.5× cheaper than the comparison model; the saving depends on what it replaced. Median user-reported cost multiple is 54.5× (n=56): 188× vs frontier models, 17× vs small models. The vendor’s own comparison with GPT-5.6 Terra is about 76×; the 444.6× in marketing is a peak against the most expensive model.
  • Accuracy: Jev and the systems it replaced each win some head-to-heads; gaps are small. In the 17 cases with accuracy for both, Jev is ahead in 10 and behind in 7; median gap is 1.6 percentage points. Of 241 cases that assessed accuracy, 83 rated Jev better and 65 worse.
  • Jev’s confidence scores miss by about 10 percentage points on average, and run clearly high on unfamiliar rating questions. Median user-measured ECE (expected calibration error: average gap between stated confidence and actual accuracy; 0 is perfect) is 0.097 (n=27). An independent test on unfamiliar tasks found 0.107 overall, but 0.325 on rating questions, where Jev was right only 44.7% of the time.
  • Developers put cheap small models next to Jev almost as often as the strongest large ones. Of the 432 cases that name a comparison model, 48.6% mention open or small models and 59.7% mention frontier models.
  • Criticism is common. Abandonment after trying Jev is not. 25.1% of all 6,277 cases contain criticism and 40.6% contain praise, but only 1 of the 2,153 hands-on cases ended with Jev being dropped.
  • Production use is still rare. Most activity is experimental. 80 of 2,153 hands-on cases (3.7%) are in production; prototypes, side projects and trial demos make up 62.4%. Seven days of data: treat this as a baseline, not a run-rate.

2. What Jev replaces

Jev does the deciding inside a program, not the writing. Classification and routing is the largest group among the 1,284 hands-on cases with a clear purpose (27.0%), then real-time and games, then search and ranking (Figure 4). These steps choose from preset answers and run many times; text generation does not matter there.

Figure 4 The 1,284 hands-on cases with a specific use, by broader group

Most of what Jev replaces is frontier models, though more than six in ten hands-on cases never say what came before. Among the 2,153 hands-on cases, prior approach is unknown in 62.1% and a frontier model in 15.1%. Rules or heuristics (keyword matching and similar) are 6.6%; human review is 0.7% (Figure 5). When Jev’s confidence is low, the most common fallback is a person (7.4%); 7.8% have no fallback at all (Figure 6).

In one tax-filing workflow, Jev took over page classification from Claude Sonnet. One call per page, pick from 261 IRS form types and 7 page types, return a confidence score; below 0.95 counts as error. On a 314-page test file the strict error rate was 0.00%, at about $0.00115 per page; the system now runs in production. “Jev classifies 100% of our tax document corpus at $0.001 per page — 34× cheaper and 6× faster than that LLM setup. This is the classifier, open sourced”

Developers compare Jev with cheap small models almost as often as with the strongest large ones. That is a clearer read on the moat than the volume of criticism. Of the 432 cases that name a comparison model, 48.6% mention a small or open model and 59.7% a frontier model (Figure 5). Only 17 cases have moved a live service to Jev; the models they replaced are a mixed group (Table 2).

A product that routes coding tasks across AI tools moved that routing decision in production from a large model to Jev. Jev now picks the tool, the model and the reasoning effort in about 200 ms. It matched Claude Fable 5.1’s choices on all 25 test tasks and cut routing cost 71% and time 90%.

4. Calibration: how reliable is Jev’s confidence

On common tasks developers tested themselves, Jev’s confidence is off actual accuracy by about 10 percentage points on average. ECE (expected calibration error: average gap between stated confidence and actual accuracy; 0 is perfect) is the usual measure. It matters for routing: auto-run high-confidence answers, send low-confidence ones to a person. Median user ECE is 0.097 (n=27); the middle half sits between 0.0588 and 0.154 (Figure 10).

and an independent test on unfamiliar tasks")

Figure 10 User-measured calibration error (ECE, lower is better) and an independent test on unfamiliar tasks

Source: FUNDA

On unfamiliar tasks unlike the training data, the overall gap is similar, but Jev is clearly overconfident on rating questions and errors point different ways by question type. One independent test measured overall ECE 0.107, close to the users’ median 0.097. Score questions (4-level priority) hit ECE 0.325; Choice leaned overconfident; Noul understated confidence. One confidence threshold for handoff will not work; set it per question type.

One independent test used 900 rule-generated support tickets (300 each Choice, Score, Noul) to check whether Jev’s probabilities hold. On three public benchmarks the probabilities needed almost no correction. On the 900 tickets with unfamiliar rules, overall ECE was 0.107: 4.4× the smallest error the test can detect (0.024). Score questions were right only 44.7% of the time.

“Calibration is proven” and “calibration is unproven” both overreach. Average gap is about 10 percentage points; on some question types in unfamiliar tasks Jev is clearly overconfident. Across all discussion, 7.4% of cases cite calibrated probabilities as a strength and 2.2% call calibration unproven or the method undisclosed. Of 64 evaluation cases that assessed calibration, 28 found Jev better and 21 worse. Task and question type decide the answer.

One replica pairs a small open model with a decision component, supports Choice, Noul and Score, and publishes training process, weights and data. On a shooting-game task it scored 128/128 vs Jev’s 56/128, but it was trained on those tasks and Jev was not. Scores are not directly comparable.

Another open-source project copies the same three question types and trains for probability output. Author-measured: 33 ms single question, 7.2 ms per question in batches. Model is available for download.

7. Risks and limitations

First: week one is prototypes and trial demos, almost no production. Treat the numbers as a tracking baseline. Prototypes and trial demos are 62.4% of 2,153 hands-on cases; production plus migrated cases are 3.7% (Figure 15). Seven days is too short to know whether prototypes stick.

Second: criticism clusters on hype, moat, accuracy and vendor self-testing. All four point to missing independent checks on capability and method. Across all discussion, top complaints are overheated promotion (7.0%) and “retrained open model, thin moat” (3.8%). In the same set, 40.6% of cases contain praise, mostly for speed and low cost (Figure 16).

One skeptical write-up argues Jev is not a substitute for general LLMs: at most 255 preset options, no code or natural-language generation, no shown reasoning. As the author puts it, “jev is just a really smart switch statement”

Third: criticism is about positioning and method. It has almost never led to abandonment. Criticism appears in 25.1% of all discussion, but only 1 of 2,153 hands-on cases ended with Jev dropped after a trial. One week is too short to judge abandonment.

Fourth: among cases with a known outcome, failures are limited. Main causes are low accuracy and unsuitable tasks, not the speed or output format the vendor advertises. Of 1,581 cases with a known outcome, 14.2% did not succeed (partial, failed, or claimed but doubtful). Main causes: low accuracy (23.1%) and tasks outside Jev’s scope (13.8%); latency worse than claimed is 4.0% (Figure 17).

8. Conclusions

Jev’s opening is fast, low-value decisions inside existing systems where the answer comes from a preset list. It is not competing for text-generation work. Of 1,284 cases with a clear use: classification and routing 27.0%, real-time and games 18.8%, agent step decisions 16.1%. Of 2,153 hands-on cases, 30.9% are code projects developers published. Vendor claims of 444.6× cheaper, 193.6× faster and zero output-format errors drew attention. Users report median gains of 10× speed and 54.5× cost. Of 432 cases that name a comparison model, 48.6% put Jev against a small or open model.

Still unproven: whether production use lasts, whether confidence scores can be trusted, and how much independent evaluation exists. Of 2,153 hands-on cases, only 80 (3.7%) are in production, 62.4% are prototypes or trial demos, 19.5% are independent evaluations. Of all 6,277 cases: 1.0% offer calibration evidence, 0.3% record a migration, 3.0% are open-source replica attempts. Among 1,581 cases with a known outcome, 14.2% did not succeed. With 82.5%–88.8% agreement between the two automatic classifiers on 80 short comments, every share here is a first-week baseline, not a settled result.

9. Metrics to track

Is trial turning into production use? Watch the production share, now 3.7%. First-week use was mostly trial: prototypes and trial demos 62.4% combined. A rising production share as the sample grows, and production spreading from single decision points to linked steps, would mean users are moving from trying Jev to relying on it.

Are outsiders checking the vendor’s numbers? Independent evaluation is 19.5% of hands-on cases; calibration evidence is

  1. 0% of all discussion. A clean positive signal: independent re-tests on out-of-distribution data that publish

confidence against actual accuracy, not a single accuracy headline.

Is negative talk becoming real churn? Track criticism share (25.1%), abandonment after trial (1 case), and open-source replicas (3.0%) together. The moat holds only if criticism falls, abandonment stays low and replicas stop growing. Table 7 has current values and the direction to watch.
