If you own AI infrastructure stocks, these developments are something worth tracking.

A lot of the growth we’re expecting obviously depends on data centers getting built and connected to power.

If approvals take longer, projects cost more or companies have to find another location, that can change when the revenue shows up.

But how much of the buildout is actually at risk?

In my article after the Texas election, I covered the initial implications.

Since then, I’ve been going through the interviews, local decisions and company calls to get a better sense of what could change.

There’s still a lot being built of course.

Some developers are already addressing the concerns.

Others could face a much harder approval process.

I think we need to understand those differences so we can sift through the different downstream impacts.

That means looking at what communities are asking for, what companies are agreeing to, and how far these projects have actually progressed.

Let’s get into it.

Local tradeoffs

The WSJ brought together officials from Loudoun, Cedar Rapids and Gary.

I think this is a great place to start.

Buddy Rizer, Loudoun County’s economic-development director, said data centers bring in roughly $1.2B a year in local taxes. That helps pay for the services people use every day.

But when the discussion turned to more development he said:

“I don’t think we should have any more.”

Think about that. He’s defending what the county gained while saying the next project may not be worth it. Those positions can both make sense.

A successful first wave doesn’t give developers unlimited room to keep expanding. Rizer acknowledged inadequate setbacks near homes. He also explained that continuous equipment noise can bother residents without breaking a decibel limit.

If the company can say it complies with the rules and the neighbor still can’t enjoy their backyard, telling everyone the project is compliant won’t settle the argument.

The rules may need to deal with the actual experience of living beside it.

Designing that in before construction seems a lot more workable than arguing over it afterward.

Cedar Rapids Mayor Tiffany O’Donnell described housing and labor pressure during construction, but defended negotiating benefits. She cited roughly $56M in community-betterment commitments. Rizer recommended negotiating early because tax receipts can take years to arrive.

So when we think about timing, the disruption starts while construction is underway. If the benefit is mostly tax revenue years from now, residents are being asked to accept the cost first and trust that the payoff comes later.

An agreement that funds something useful earlier can change that conversation. It still needs to be clear who receives it and when.

Gary Mayor Eddie Melton explained his city’s moratorium differently:

“I’m not closing the door.”

Gary had recently endured a weeks-long outage. Melton wanted residents involved and favored brownfield sites over residential areas.

It’s different than the argument Rizer is making because one community is deciding how much more it wants after years of development and another is trying to establish what it will accept in the first place. A pause can be part of that negotiation.

But as investors we still need to know whether it leads to a workable process or leaves developers waiting indefinitely.

The next question is harder. Even if the town likes the deal, how do we know the rest of the utility’s customers aren’t paying for it?

Who pays?

Say a utility builds new infrastructure because a developer says it needs a huge amount of power.

Then the project gets delayed, shrinks or never opens.

The utility has already spent the money, so who pays it back?

Duke CFO Brian Savoy laid out the tools in his November 5 Bloomberg interview:

“minimum billing demands, with capital advances, termination provisions, credit support.”

Minimum bills help cover infrastructure built for demand that might arrive late or come in lower.

Upfront contributions reduce the amount the utility has to finance.

Exit payments and credit support are intended to protect it if the customer leaves or cannot pay.

But I’d still want to know how much of the cost is covered, how long the obligation lasts, and who stands behind it. A promise from a well-funded parent company and a promise from an isolated project subsidiary can have very different value.

Savoy said Duke is moving protections from confidential agreements into public tariffs. Residents and policymakers can inspect the terms rather than take management’s word for them.

Transparency won’t make every objection disappear, but it allows a more useful argument about who pays.

He also pointed to more than 5 GW under construction, expected online next year, and said he didn’t expect a slowdown in Duke’s territories.

That’s a useful counterpoint to the idea that this new blue wave is going to drown out all the data center dev.

The interviewer pushed him on a rejected North Carolina gas turbine. Savoy said Duke would return with its justification after the resource-plan decision. So even his confident outlook includes a regulatory process still to work through.

On November 3, Florida’s Public Service Commission rejected Duke Energy Florida’s proposed large-load tariffs.

It found that the proposal left out some customers covered by the law and hadn’t sufficiently demonstrated that each qualifying customer would pay the full cost of service.

Duke needs to come back with a compliant proposal.

But it is a meaningful challenge to the idea that having minimum bills and financial guarantees automatically settles the question.

North Carolina’s attorney general made a related objection to Duke’s proposal in October. The state welcomed protections but wanted more, including treatment of new generation costs.

So we have management arguing it can deliver growth without burdening households, and regulators asking it to prove the cost allocation.

That’s one useful example of the types of disagreements to follow and how they ultimately resolve.

The answer affects the price and terms on which the next facility can get power.

Community deals

Hut 8’s November 4 call got into this.

At River Bend in Louisiana, it is funding roughly $16M for a new well and water infrastructure for parish residents.

The infrastructure is intended to transfer to the parish at no cost, and the water is not for cooling the data center. Hut 8 also said it paid $10M in lieu of taxes a year early.

To be clear, the water project was announced in May, well before the election.

Residents would get infrastructure for their own water supply. That is a much more concrete benefit than hearing how important AI will be someday.

IREN’s November 5 call focused on regional locations, local hiring and closed-loop cooling. Management said it prioritizes bringing the limited water needed from off-site. Location and design can avoid some problems before construction starts.

Rizer put it this way in the WSJ discussion:

“water is an engineering choice and noise is a design issue.”

“Closed-loop” only answers part of the water question. Heat still has to leave the building. We need to know how the whole facility is cooled.

The same applies to bringing your own power. Residents might welcome less pressure on the grid and still object to a generating plant nearby. Its emissions, noise and fuel supply need their own answers.

Google’s November 5 announcement gives us an example of how much goes into that. It plans to invest $17B in its first Wyoming data center, with air cooling to reduce pressure on local water resources. The power arrangements include a contracted 702 MW natural-gas fuel-cell project with carbon capture, alongside other gas generation. Google says it will pay for the electricity it uses and the infrastructure directly required by its growth.

So they are committing to a major new US campus while putting more of the power supply and community arrangements into the development plan. I think that’s important. The response to these concerns can involve more investment in getting a project built, rather than abandoning it.

OpenAI CFO Sarah Friar’s November 6 CNBC interview brings us back to why this matters for the companies trying to secure the capacity. Friar’s argument starts with demand. She reported 70% QoQ business growth and a doubling of enterprise business in Q3. In her view, customers keep finding uses for AI, so OpenAI needs to keep securing compute and raising capital. Obviously this is one of the main critical theses for these investments.

That explains why she wants to keep building but it doesn’t, by itself, explain why a particular town should want to host the buildout. She kept pushing on that gap.

“we, OpenAI, have promised we will never raise your electricity bill.”

The interviewer then pushed back and said that it seemed very reactive what OpenAI was putting into place now.

Friar pushed back on the idea that this was all a post-election response. She said the community work had been going on for years and pointed to local programs alongside the electricity commitment

Things like investing in the grid, funding school lunches, hosting AI summits, building berms to reduce noise etc etc.

I think both sides of the exchange are worth sitting with because a company can have been doing useful work and still have failed to earn enough trust.

The question is whether the response came early enough to matter. Friar’s answer is that the work continues regardless.

The missing piece for me is the project-level agreement.

If the promise is that residents won’t pay more, how is that defined?

Does it cover the infrastructure built for the project?

What happens if plans change?

Who can enforce it?

That interview doesn’t settle those questions.

New(ish) rules

It’s not going to be the case where every town will be won over by a better financial package.

San Diego’s October 27 ordinance imposed an initial 45-day pause covering pending applications and expansions, while preserving legally vested rights.

Brisbane extended its pause on November 5 through September 2027 while considering restrictions or prohibition. But the city reported no existing data centers or pending applications. This is a constraint on potential future development.

Pennsylvania’s August 18 order removed data centers from fast-track permitting and tightened review for new environmental applications above 25 MW.

But look at the dates.

So much of this stuff was underway before the midterms.

And we know that Texas was already moving before the election, too.

Abbott’s September 21 direction to TCEQ called for a pause on data-center permits pending an ERCOT review.

Hinojosa’s proposed protections still need to become adopted rules. Her victory didn’t itself settle which projects can proceed.

For a developer, losing a tax break raises costs.

Waiting for new rules can push back an opening date. ‘

An outright prohibition can mean finding another site.

IREN

IREN already has Horizon 1 running for Microsoft, and it just signed another five-year agreement where the customer is prepaying 90% of the associated GPU costs.

That’s a big commitment from a customer, and it takes a meaningful piece of the equipment bill off IREN’s plate.

But even with customers committing that kind of money, getting capacity online can take longer than expected.

IREN moved its target for more than $4B in annualized revenue from December to January, potentially February, citing a customer-requested design change and supplier timing. That delay wasn’t attributed to the election just to be clear.

Microsoft’s agreement allows it to terminate an affected tranche and recover its advance after certain delivery delays. There are also extensions for specified delays caused by Microsoft and qualifying NVIDIA supply issues.

IREN says the program supporting that $4B-plus run rate is funded, subject to its stated conditions. Its broader FY27 plan still calls for roughly $15B in remaining capex, with $9B in cash, committed financing and prepayments, and another $6B targeted.

Management can slow some construction if financing conditions worsen, but it also has non-cancelable Dell equipment commitments. So there’s a limit to how much spending it can simply push out once orders are placed.

I think IREN shows both sides of this.

Customers are committing real money and capacity is coming online.

Getting the rest built, powered and delivered on time still takes a lot of capital and coordination.

The buildout

There’s still a lot of capacity being built, with customers already waiting for it.

JLL’s August report estimated 66 GW under construction across North America, with 95% pre-committed, including facilities being built for their owners’ own use. That covers the broader data-center market and several years of construction, rather than 66 GW of AI capacity opening next year. Even so, it gives us a substantial amount of development already underway. JLL’s report

Keep in mind that a customer can commit to a facility before the developer has everything it needs to open on schedule. Construction progress, power delivery and final approvals can move at different speeds.

Core Scientific’s November 5 call helps explain that. Management said stormwater permits had been secured for Pecos and Hunt, while applications for proposed on-site power generation had yet to be filed. It also discussed a permit-by-rule route for backup generators. Those are separate approvals for different equipment and activities. Securing permission to begin site work leaves further steps before a proposed power plant can operate.

For those of us that might be tracking data center development, that’s how an encouraging construction update can coexist with uncertainty about the eventual opening date. We need to know what has been approved and what work those approvals actually allow. The same applies when a developer says it will bring its own power. It still needs to build and obtain approval for the generation.

Could the prospect of tighter restrictions make companies spend sooner?

Morgan Stanley’s Ariana Salvatore discussed that possibility in July, before the midterms. Her team considered whether hyperscalers might start projects earlier ahead of greater political uncertainty around the 2028 election. Longer development timelines also give companies a reason to begin securing future capacity now. Listen to the discussion and read the transcript

I can see why a company would want to get moving while it has an opportunity. But this was an analyst’s scenario. It wasn’t a hyperscaler announcing that it had raised its budget because of the election. Ordering equipment sooner can bring spending forward while the electricity connection still arrives on the original schedule. And starting construction offers no blanket exemption from future rules.

So there are reasons for companies to accelerate the projects they can advance. There are also projects that will have to wait. The permitting and power arrangements help determine which response is possible.

And we need to include what’s happening outside the US.

On November 4, Google announced $15B of investment in Malaysia for 2027 and 2028. Its first campus at Elmina is already operational. Additional campuses in Selangor and Negeri Sembilan are planned, alongside an upcoming Google Cloud region. Google says the capacity will serve growing local demand and its services across Asia-Pacific.

The same announcement includes plans for more than 1 GW of additional clean-energy capacity, water projects and payment of the direct infrastructure costs of connecting its campuses. Google chose water cooling there after assessing local watersheds. In Wyoming, it chose air cooling. The design depends on the location and the resources available.

There’s a lot happening in India as well. Google broke ground on its Visakhapatnam AI hub in April, part of a $15B program for 2026–2030 first announced in October 2025. It is building gigawatt-scale compute for Indian enterprises and startups, with new transmission, generation and storage planned alongside it.

Google is also developing new subsea connections linking India with Singapore, South Africa and Australia. That helps explain why these projects take years of planning. A new campus needs the network that connects it to customers and other facilities, as well as somewhere to plug in the servers.

In Finland, Google announced at least €13B for 2027–2028 in September. It worked with the grid operator to locate new campuses in the north, near available electricity and existing grid capacity. The arrangements include a 22-year nuclear power agreement and a contracted battery expected online in late 2027.

Meta broke ground on a planned 1 GW campus in Alberta in July, representing more than CAD $13B of investment. It says it will fully fund new generation and grid infrastructure, and use dry cooling with no operational water consumption in the cooling system.

The reason I bring up all these example is to show that there’s real investment behind the international buildout.

These companies are serving customers abroad and developing sites that fit their power and network needs.

If US approvals become harder, the next investment could go somewhere else. But the examples above show how much preparation that requires.

Companies still have to secure power, connect the campus and reach an agreement with the people who live nearby.

Closing Thoughts

The buildout will go on. If it gets cancelled or delayed in certain counties, they will find other places to build them. If not in the U.S., then abroad.

As long as demand for AI will continue to grow and the investment is worth it (which I do believe it will be, even if capex plateus 2028/2029) then these data centers will continue to be built, and the infra companies will continue to grow.

But as we are seeing, company record earnings and the movement in the stocks are not directly correlated. The highest quality companies will continue to exceed earnings with many quarters to come. But if the market is pricing in a peak, then the multiples that these companies will trade at will get crushed. And that’s what we are seeing today.
