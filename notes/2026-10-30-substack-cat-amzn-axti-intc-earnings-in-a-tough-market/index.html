Caterpillar

Physical AI initially got me interested in CAT. When I dug into it / presented it last week, I also liked how much else was going on across power, construction, mining, and the parts and service attached to those machines. For this report, I wanted to know whether power orders were holding up and whether the equipment entering dealer rental fleets was getting used.

The reported results were strong, with sales and revenues of $20.682B, up 17%, with $7.20 of adjusted EPS.

Power & Energy earned $2.143B of segment profit, up 31%, and Construction Industries earned $1.831B, up 33%.

Having several businesses contributing earnings, with different customers and replacement cycles behind them, is part of the appeal for me.

Power-generation revenue grew 30%, and sales to users rose 75%.

Backlog reached $78.1B, with 58% scheduled within twelve months.

They are taking some engine orders into 2029 and turbine orders into 2030, with slots still available.

The backlog in my earlier article was $72.1B. They are shipping equipment and still adding to the order book, which supports the runway, although capacity still limits the pace of Power & Energy deliveries and expansion costs, depreciation, and freight are weighing on margin.

Now lets talk about the service opportunity that goes along with these delivieries. A primary-power engine accumulates far more operating hours than a backup unit waiting for an emergency. Those hours eventually mean parts and maintenance. Much of the incremental service-revenue benefit discussed in my earlier article comes after 2030 so that time frame definitely needs to be kept in mind.

On construction, sales to users rose 20% globally and 27% in North America. Management said equipment entering rental fleets was moving promptly onto projects, with North American dealer rental revenue increasing.

That rental activity is important to the question I raised earlier because putting a machine in a dealer fleet creates revenue for CAT, while getting it onto a job and earning money from it gives the dealer a reason to buy another one.

The Q4 setup is more awkward though honestly. Construction dealers are expected to reduce inventory by roughly $1.6B, versus about $0.6B a year earlier.

Management expects a larger-than-normal sequential construction-margin decline, while keeping full-year revenue-growth guidance unchanged.

Dealers can meet customer demand with equipment they already own. That means fewer shipments and worse fixed-cost absorption for the manufacturer, even while customers have work.

The earnings hit is important, but the end-user commentary gives me more reason to treat this as a channel adjustment than as customers demand issues.

Resource Industries sales grew 15% to $4.615B, while margin was 13.5%, versus 13.6% a year earlier. Manufacturing costs, including tariffs, absorbed much of the benefit.

Retail sales rose only 4%, with shipping and commissioning delays and a Q4 catch-up expected, while dealer-inventory changes contributed substantially to the stronger reported sales growth.

Q3 also included $128M of tariff recoveries, worth an estimated $0.21 per share after tax. That belongs outside the repeatable earnings assumption obviously.

Enterprise operating cash flow was $4.4B, giving the expansion a substantial existing cash-producing business to lean on.

My earlier valuation used a 2028 EPS estimate of $39.21.

Roughly 21–25 times gave an illustrative $825–980 range.

At 15 times, it was about $588, or $590.

As of today the sub $600 level is my area of interest to build out my position. Depending on the overall market movement. I won’t catch a falling knife here. But I do like the valuation in the high 500s given where we are at today.

Amazon

Let’s start with the bad and then get to the good.

Amazon spent $75.9B in cash capex in Q3. Over the last twelve months, they generated $168.3B in operating cash flow and spent $210.7B on property and equipment, net of sales and incentives.

Free cash flow fell to negative $42.4B, from negative $7.6B at the end of Q2.

The approximately $220B cash-capex plan for 2026 is unchanged, and management expects meaningfully higher spending in 2027.

Payback periods are improving, according to management, with the 2027 budget and the timing of positive free cash flow still open.

A lot of the cash coming in is going straight back into data centers and the chips to fill them. Great for AI infra. For Amazon, the market clearly doesn’t like it.

AWS margins were 38.7%, down from 39.4% in Q2.

AI margins are improving from a lower level than the mature core business, so the mix will influence profitability as AI becomes a larger share of revenue. Custom silicon and efficiency help but component costs and depreciation add pressure. Management left the margin outlook open. For the growth scenario below, I am using 37.5%.

The AWS growth itself was very impressive. Revenue was about $46B, up 39.3%, with $17.8B of operating income. That is a $184B annualized revenue run rate growing nearly 40%.

Across Amazon, revenue was $201.9B and operating income was $28.1B.

Then Jassy said they believe AWS can grow above 40% in both 2027 and 2028. At this size, that is a remarkable outlook.

Here is how I am thinking about the scale. AWS generated $125.803B in the first nine months of 2026.

Holding Q4 at Q3’s $45.984B gives us a $171.787B full-year base.

My scenario uses 40% growth in each of the next two years, just below management’s outlook of above 40%. $171.787B × 1.4 × 1.4 gets us to about $336.7B of AWS revenue in 2028.

Applying a 37.5% operating margin gives about $126.3B of AWS operating income.

That is enormous earning power.

The AI mix will shape the margin, and the pace of investment will determine how quickly those earnings turn into cash for shareholders of course.

The Morgan Stanley forecasts I discussed on October 20 were 47% AWS growth in 2027, 42% in 2028, and roughly $16.50 of Amazon EPS in 2028.

Their earnings estimate takes the analysis through to Amazon as a whole, where retail, advertising, margins, and spending all contribute.

Morgan Stanley named Amazon a Top Pick on October 19, keeping Overweight and raising its target from $335 to $350.

Backlog reached $578B, up from $496B in Q2, and inference already accounts for well over half of AI revenue.

We have substantial future commitments alongside customers paying to use the capacity now.

When we think about the broader AWS relationship, an agent doing work every day needs compute, storage, memory, and access to the customer’s systems.

When the data and applications are already on AWS, running the AI work nearby makes sense. An AI application can also bring more of the customer’s existing business onto AWS. Amazon can earn from that activity while somebody else’s model does the work.

That was an important part of my earlier thesis, and it becomes more valuable as agents handle more work.

External Trainium sales are planned for late 2027, giving Amazon another way to earn from its chip investment. They can sell a chip and get paid sooner or keep it inside AWS and earn cloud revenue from it over several years. Foundry allocation limits the available supply. Keeping the chip also means providing the data center, power, and surrounding infrastructure, with utilization and pricing driving the return on that additional investment.

Now when we think about retail, small margin improvements across that much revenue can add a lot to earnings. They doubled the robotic-arm fleet and expect a fourth consecutive annual reduction in cost to serve. This is a Physical AI application I really like where an enormous existing network where technology can improve the cost of moving each item.

Amazon can use those savings to improve margins, deliver faster, and offer better prices. In Q4, fuel and linehaul will create a bigger headwind, with the seller surcharge recovering only part of the increase.

Advertising was $19.6B, up 11% as reported and 12% excluding currency effects. Management pointed to Prime Day moving into Q2, NBA timing, and an advertising comparison change.

Outside shopping agents are having no meaningful impact yet, according to management. I previously raised the risk of an outside assistant owning the customer relationship while Amazon handles fulfillment, which could change the advertising economics over time. Purchase history, payments, and delivery convenience give Amazon a strong position with shoppers, and the timing changes explain part of this quarter’s growth rate.

Q4 guidance is $240–247B of sales and $28.5–33.5B of operating income. RBC called the revenue outlook, and especially profit, below expectations while keeping Outperform and a $330 target.

Overall I came away more confident in a substantially larger AWS business, with retail improving alongside it.

The cash-flow timing is the main issue I think. Amazon can rerate before capex peaks as the market becomes more confident in the returns, and this call gives us a much stronger demand outlook to build that case around. AWS growing above 40% for another two years would change the scale of the whole company.

Again, I want to keep building my hyperscaler positions. Like most things, I think I’m just early. But that’s why I am DCA’ing into them. Patience on my end.

AXT

Wow, well done AXT!

If you have followed my AXT coverage, you know I was quite critical for a while. I questioned the valuation, China exposure, and how necessary they specifically were to the laser makers.

By the July pre-earnings article, I was changing my mind as customers worked harder to source material, shipping routes improved, and agreements with Coherent, Lumentum, and Casela made the demand more tangible.

I don’t currently have a position but this quarter is exceptionally impressive, and the Q4 outlook takes another large step up from it.

Revenue was $73.4M, up 54% sequentially and 162% year over year, with $54.3M coming from InP.

Adjusted gross margin reached 56.1%, versus 45.0% in Q2, and adjusted operating profit was $29.5M at a 40.2% margin.

Adjusted EPS went from $0.19 to $0.41.

GAAP gross margin was 56.0% and EPS was $0.39, so the strength is evident on that basis too.

In April, I was discussing roughly 35% gross margin as an external planning assumption with room for upside.

They have moved so far beyond that discussion that the earnings assumptions need to be rebuilt around what they are now delivering.

And then they give us approximately $90M of permit-backed Q4 revenue, another roughly 23% sequential increase, with adjusted EPS of $0.57–0.59. The midpoint is about 41% above Q3.

The CFO discussed gross margin moving into the low 60s, and management expects the incremental revenue to come from InP.

That is a very strong setup after a quarter that already more than doubled adjusted EPS sequentially.

The permits are an important part of it as well, because the revenue base is supported by permits already secured or shipments that do not need them.

Insane.

The production progress helps explain it. They surpassed the original capacity-doubling goal in August, expect to exit 2026 at roughly 2.75–3 times the end-2025 level, and still plan to double again in 2027.

Four-inch products are expected to lead the next stage, while six-inch remains earlier in its ramp (sigh, Coherent).

Volume, pricing, richer InP mix, and furnace productivity are all contributing.

For demanding lasers, the material also needs the right defect level and customer qualification. Announcing equipment is only the start of producing something the customer can use consistently at good yields, and those requirements give the existing process knowledge real value.

In my latest weekly review, I discussed sources suggesting the constraint could ease in 2027 and wondered how the stock would react if competitors narrowed the gap.

Management’s assessment is very different.

Customers remain on allocation and demand exceeds planned output into 2027 and 2028, even with the expansion underway. I take that seriously. Competitor qualifications and laser yields still affect how long the tightness lasts, but the call gives me more confidence in the near-term scarcity.

They can also grow revenue by selling more valuable material and getting more useful output from the equipment, which is why a revenue opportunity and physical wafer capacity need separate assumptions.

Backlog exceeds $500M across multi-year agreements, shorter contracts, and spot orders, with LTAs still a relatively small portion.

They want pricing and capacity flexibility, which is interesting because those initial agreements helped change my view by demonstrating commitment and funding expansion.

Now they can preserve more of the upside if the product becomes more valuable rather than deliver most future output at an old price. That also leaves more exposure to the market when the wafers are ready.

The longer-term attraction would be customers continuing to pay a premium for qualified material as supply becomes less desperate. Delivery timing matters too, since some backlog stretches across years and long queues can encourage earlier orders. The Lumentum agreement discussed on the call was the existing one.

The proposed Tongmei financing could bring in up to about $545M externally, with the parent retaining around 73.4% at the maximum investment. The separate $400M parent contribution is an internal transfer, and the agreement includes investor preferences and conditional redemption provisions. This could help them expand faster, while a completed transaction would leave AXTI shareholders with a smaller share of the manufacturing business. The stronger operating earnings therefore have to pass through that ownership math before we decide what belongs to the listed parent.

The $43.5M Lumentum payment received in Q3 is another source of cash before shipment, carried as a liability until the wafers are delivered.

For the rest of optics, this is encouraging demand evidence and a reminder that the laser and module makers still need enough qualified material to hit their own plans.

Capturing more value from scarce substrates is great for the supplier, while its customers have their own production and margin problems to solve.

I am much more impressed with the operating business after these results.

The Q3 margin, Q4 earnings outlook, and capacity progress together make this a much stronger earnings story than the one I was questioning earlier in the year, with the valuation now needing to reflect both that improvement and the financing terms.

I want in. I would expect with any kind of semis recovery that AXTI would out perform. I don’t know yet when I’ll get in. I want some of this volatility to die down first.

Intel

Wow. Not well done.

The outlook was poor, even though Q3 beat the prior company guide.

Revenue was $16.513B, adjusted EPS was $0.52, and adjusted gross margin was 44.1%.

Q4 guidance is $15–16B of revenue, with $0.25 of adjusted EPS and 38% gross margin at the midpoint.

That is a substantial deterioration in earnings and margin, and the strength in servers has to be considered alongside it.

DCAI produced $7.318B of revenue and $3.039B of operating income in Q3, while the foundry segment lost $1.853B.

There is a profitable product business here, with a manufacturing operation still absorbing a lot of it.

There is also a tragedy behind part of the outlook. The October 14 incident at the Kiryat Gat facility involved a gas-cylinder explosion and fire, killing two people and seriously injuring another.

Production was temporarily paused for about two weeks, and management estimates approximately $300M of lost Q4 revenue and a two-percentage-point gross-margin impact. It also said Q1 could see a similar revenue impact, with the assessment still preliminary.

Adding the two margin points back to the 38% outlook gets roughly 40%, still 4.1 points below Q3.

That is my arithmetic but it shows that the accident explains only part of the weakness.

DCAI revenue is expected to grow in the mid-teens sequentially while the client business falls more than 20%.

Management described a PC inventory correction concentrated in Q4 and Q1, potentially extending into Q2, while separately expecting no improvement in memory conditions during 2027.

They are moving some Oregon capacity from 18A client production toward Intel 3 servers, with output expected in the second half of 2027.

Substrates remain a constraint.

Granite Rapids is margin-accretive, while Panther Lake remains below the corporate average, so shifting resources toward the stronger opportunity makes sense.

The extra output still arrives later and needs the other components, which limits how much today’s demand can help the immediate earnings result. The production response and the customer opportunity have to line up closely enough to earn a worthwhile return.

External foundry revenue was $411M, with the sequential increase primarily coming from a nonrecurring program.

Manufacturing costs improved on Intel 3 and 18A, which is encouraging, but recurring foundry business needs customers qualifying designs, committing volume, and returning with the next product. Purpose-built silicon can also grow gross-profit dollars at a below-average margin percentage.

I am comfortable with that trade if the returns justify the investment. Filling capacity already paid for can be attractive, whereas building another expensive facility to earn those dollars requires a very different calculation.

The balance sheet has more resources after an approximately $23B equity raise, with cash and short-term investments around $54B. That funding helps them pursue the expansion, but it also spreads the eventual earnings across more shares, with Q4 estimated diluted shares of 5.44B. The return needs to make sense on that per-share basis as well as for the manufacturing business overall.

2027 capex is expected to rise strongly from the approximately $20B gross-capex level in 2026, and management said 2027 probably would not produce positive free cash flow.

They need capacity to serve the demand while it is there, but spending can be necessary to compete and still take a long time to reward shareholders.

Better process costs, product mix, and utilization need to work together, with enough profitable volume to carry the manufacturing costs.

There is progress worth following here, alongside a weak near-term outlook and another substantial investment year before the cash outcome becomes more convincing.

Down 25% after hours. Bloodbath. No position for me.

Where this leaves me

The AI trade has been hurt. If you’ve been following along you know that my goal is to trim back meaningfully on rips and add back on dips. More trimming size and frequency than I typically would. I sold a bit yesterday. I don’t yet know when I will add more AI exposure. Like I mentioned earlier, I want to see macro conditions improve first.

I am also continuing to look outside the trade as well. There are other businesses worth spending time on, especially when so much attention is concentrated in the same names. I will have a few ready to discuss this weekend.

I also want more hyperscaler exposure. It still feels early to me, and I plan to build that over time. The spending makes the near-term cash picture difficult, but the potential earning power as that capacity gets used is what interests me. I want room to work through the numbers and add gradually as the opportunities make sense.
