Substack · 2026-10-16

It’s obvious that there is a lot of time spent on how much electricity all of the data centers that we are building need.

But getting power into the building is only part of it. Delivering it to an increasingly powerful processor, within a very small space and without wasting too much of it as heat, is becoming a serious engineering problem. (If you have been following along the robotics research, this should sound familiar as well).

And this is where Vicor comes in. They have two main ways to get paid. They sell the power-conversion hardware, and they license technology that other suppliers can use.

I like that they can benefit from growing demand through both routes. The hardware business is already growing, and the recent licensing announcements have pushed revenue expectations considerably higher. We’ll get to that in a sec.

Getting power closer to the processor

Vicor makes modules that convert electricity into the voltages different components need. They sell into computing, industrial equipment and aerospace and defense, among other markets.

For AI processors, one big challenge is delivering a lot of power in very little space. Sending high current across a circuit board wastes energy as heat. Bringing the final conversion closer to the processor can reduce those losses and leave more room for other components.

Vicor makes thin modules that can sit underneath the processor. That is the basic idea behind vertical power delivery. What we really want to know is whether their approach helps customers build more powerful systems efficiently, and whether those customers choose to buy their modules or license the technology.

Google has published research on moving power delivery underneath increasingly demanding processors. Vicor has also worked with Cerebras, whose wafer-scale computers require substantial power in a tightly integrated system.

Orders are growing

Product backlog grew from approximately $177M at the end of 2025 to $380M at the end of June. Q2 product revenue increased approximately 32% YoY, and Advanced Products revenue excluding royalties grew about 28% QoQ.

Calculated from the separately disclosed product costs, first-half product gross margin improved from approximately 41.7% to 47.5% YoY. Q2 was lower than Q1, though, at roughly 46.7% versus 48.4%. The CFO described equipment relocation expenses as they made room for new tools, alongside higher freight and tariff costs.

Their operating margin is improving pretty quickly too. It increased from 14.9% in Q1 to 24.3% in Q2. Revenue grew 26.9% QoQ while operating expenses increased just 6.1%, which allowed operating profit to more than double from $16.9M to $34.9M.

The licensing business is great for this. Royalties increased from roughly $15M to $30.4M over that period and carry very little direct cost. That helped lift overall gross margin from 55.2% to 58%, even with the manufacturing costs I mentioned above.

I like that we’re already seeing more of the revenue growth turn into profit. If they can keep growing hardware shipments without expenses rising at the same pace, alongside a sustained licensing contribution, there is room for further operating-margin expansion.

In September, they acquired sites to support additional chip fabs, with initial Fab 2 deployment expected roughly a year later. Management has discussed broader next-generation customer ramps toward late 2027. I’ve kept Fab 2 revenue out of 2027 in my model and included only an initial contribution in 2028 to be conservative.

A connection to Physical AI too

This also fits into the power-and-energy layer of my Physical AI research. Robots need efficient conversion between their batteries and the different voltages used by onboard computers, sensors and other electronics. Saving space and reducing losses can help them operate longer between charges.

Vicor has documented warehouse-robot applications, and Knightscope selected them for its fifth-generation security robots in 2021. They have also described supporting Dusty Robotics’ construction robot through electronics partner OLogic. This is not a major part of my thesis at all but I like having that additional avenue for adoption as more computing moves into machines working in the physical world. And I want to make the connection as I cover both Physical AI and DC infra.

Why the stock moved so much

The stock made a parabolic move at the end of September.

They had several great updates at the right time just as semis were picking up steam as well.

The September agreement with a leading AI OEM allows that customer to source covered vertical-power modules from other suppliers. Vicor can receive licensing income from that arrangement, and direct purchases from Vicor can qualify for royalty discounts. Their technology can generate income beyond the hardware they manufacture themselves. It also means that a transition toward direct hardware purchases needs to account for the associated royalty credits.

After the September license, they raised Q3 sequential revenue-growth guidance from nearly 10% to more than 20% on September 21. Then, on September 30, they raised it again to more than 30%. Both increases were tied to the same license. That’s wild. And with earnings coming up next week, and the stock having reset to the levels pre-announcement, I wonder how much is baked in at this point. If the stock was still trading >$300, I would have concerns that it was all priced in. But I don’t feel that way today. I guess we will see what Tuesday brings.

Using Q2 revenue, that moved the implied Q3 outlook from roughly $158M to more than $186M in a few weeks. You can see why the market cared. The licensing opportunity was showing up in near-term numbers.

Needham’s May target used a $7 estimate for 2028 EPS at 50×. By October, the estimate was $8.37 at 42×, still producing a $350 target. The earnings estimate had risen about 20%, while the multiple had come down. In that analyst’s framework, stronger expected earnings were supporting the valuation. We have seen this play out and I think we will see more of it as analysts reprice AI stocks. Earnings can continue to grow, but the multiple that the market will pay for them is going to be heavily contested. So I want to find companies that don’t need outlandish multiples.

The stock closed at $191.50 today, trading at roughly 23.7× my modeled 2028 EPS. That leaves us looking at a business with improved revenue expectations at a much lower share price than it had earlier this month.

Royalty income can vary substantially from quarter to quarter of course. The earlier May agreement, for example, had $60M of consideration recognized unevenly over several quarters, with cash collected on a different schedule. I want more visibility into how the September agreement contributes beyond this initial increase.

Shorter agreements can give Vicor opportunities to renegotiate as customer usage grows. They also leave future earnings dependent on terms that still have to be agreed. My model includes future royalty income beyond the amounts publicly scheduled today.

Where I could be wrong / Risks

The biggest risk is that customer programs ramp more slowly or royalties come in below my assumptions. Factories require spending before they generate sales, and future license terms still need to be negotiated. Some customer identities and component values remain undisclosed, which limits visibility. A delay at a major customer could affect both earnings and cash flow.

Competition is there, with MPS already discussing vertical-power shipments and ADI investing through Empower. Patent disputes and customer redesigns can also affect Vicor’s licensing position. My model needs stronger manufacturing margins and a premium valuation.

And of course, the AI trade is being absolutely pummeled right now. Even if the company is growing aggressively, we don’t yet know what the market will be willing to pay for it, or how low the stock could go in the meantime. But I know where I feel comfortable owning it, given what we know today.
