原标题：Why Robot Hands Are So Hard to Get Right

#robotics #semis

核心摘要

  1. 文章认为人形机器人能否从少量测试走向规模化工作，很大程度取决于机械手能否抓取物体并长时间完成任务，而灵巧操作要求物体在手中移动调整，比两指夹爪的简单抓放复杂得多。
  2. 手指驱动方式存在取舍：腱驱动可把电机后置以减轻手指，但腱会拉伸、磨损并需重新调张力；把齿轮和连杆放进手内则增加手指重量、发热和摩擦，欠驱动方案可用更少执行器实现抓握但牺牲手内操控灵活性。
  3. 美蓓亚三美在 CES 2026 展示的机械手原型内含 85 个微型与小型轴承和 11 个执行器，驱动单元与 Harmonic Drive Systems 合作开发。
  4. NVE（NVEC）的 ALT022 磁传感器封装仅 0.55×0.55 毫米，管理层在 10 月财报电话会上表示面向自动化和人形机器人应用的产量正在增加。
  5. Allegro（ALGM）的电机控制器、传感器和电源芯片被 Sarcomere Dynamics 用于 Scorpion 夹爪并计划扩展到 ARTUS Dex 手，其集成 TMR 磁传感器与智能电机驱动的芯片计划在财年第三季度送样。
  6. VPG 第二个人形机器人客户在 3 月下了约 150 万美元订单，主要用于手指触觉传感，第一个客户的关节传感量产已推迟至第四季度，VPG 第三季度人形机器人销售额为 30 万美元。
  7. 触觉方案还包括电容式、软垫内磁铁以及 GelSight 等指尖内置摄像头的光学传感，Meta 与 GelSight 合作制造 DIGIT 360 指尖并与 Wonik Robotics 合作开发机械手，Mobileye 于 2026 年 2 月完成收购的 Mentee Robotics 采用基于电机的触觉感知和完全可反向驱动的手。
  8. Lattice（LSCC）与 Inova Semiconductors 及德国航空航天中心合作开发 18 个电机的研究手，用 FPGA 就近控制电机，并通过调度网络共享传输指令和传感数据以减少线束。
  9. Google DeepMind 的 Gemini Robotics 2 在配 Sharpa 手的 Apptronik Apollo 上拧下灯泡成功率为 92%，拧上仅 36%；NVIDIA GR00T 2 在 Dexter-50 任务上零示范平均成功率 40%，1 次示范升至 71%，5 次示范达 95%，但任务是预先筛选过的。
  10. Figure 02 在宝马累计运行超过 1250 小时、装载超过 9 万个零件，前臂是主要硬件故障区，宝马确认其参与了超过 3 万辆车的生产，文章强调维修、重新标定和备件等可维护性将直接影响客户经济性。

How the fingers move, how they feel, and what it takes to get useful work out of them.

Gaetano

Nov 10

READ IN APP

With this packed earnings period we’ve spent so much time going through company results.

Let’s take a step back for a bit and dig into some humanoid research.

I want to spend this one on the hands.

In the earlier humanoid article we went through what could get these robots from a few units being tested to hundreds of them actually working.

And a lot of it comes back to something that seems pretty basic. Can the robot pick something up and do the job with it? Keep doing it, all day, with someone coming over to help only occasionally?

The hands are so intricate. Think about it. Pick up a pen and turn it around in your hand. You probably moved a few fingers, loosened your grip in one spot and tightened it somewhere else. It takes almost no thought. Now try to describe all of that to someone building a hand from scratch. Where do the motors go? How does it feel the pen starting to slide? How hard should it squeeze? There is a lot going on in this tiny space, and the decisions start affecting each other pretty quickly.

Some of the companies we’ve been talking about are already working on pieces of this. ALGM, NVEC, VPG. We have named integrations, customer orders and sensor development to look at.

First, look at this. A Shadow hand holding a light bulb, photographed in 2007. All that hardware coming through the wrist and these fingers wrapped around thin glass. People have been at this for a long time! Just getting the fingers to close around something took a lot of work. Then you start asking the hand to use it.

Picking it up, then doing something with it

Back to the pen. Picking it up gets it off the table. To write with it, you have to settle it between your fingers, turn the point toward the paper and hold it there as you move. The object moves around inside your grip. Some fingers let it slide while others keep it from falling. Your thumb comes across from another direction and helps hold the whole thing together.

‘*Dexterous manipulation’. *

For plenty of factory jobs, the part shows up in the same spot and just needs to be moved somewhere else. Look at Baxter’s two-finger gripper below. Close around the part, move it, release it. If you can do the whole job that way, you have fewer moving pieces to buy and keep working. A customer can get quite a lot out of a setup like this.

But say that same robot is going to open a drawer, take a tool out and then use the tool. Now the grip changes as the job changes. A hand with more movement has more ways to get through that sequence. And we already have drawers, handles, tools and workstations everywhere that were built around people using them. Getting a robot to work with what is already there would save a lot of redesigning around the robot. I think it’s quite easy to see the appeal, but you can also see how quickly the hand itself gets complicated.

How the fingers move

Let’s start with how a finger bends. Some of the muscles moving your own fingers sit back in your forearm, and tendons carry the force through the wrist. Follow them in the drawing below. They have quite a distance to travel before they reach the finger.

So you can put the motors farther back and run tendons out to the fingers, much like our own hands. Lighter fingers, more room at the tips. Makes sense.

But now you have this tendon running from the motor out to the joint and it stretches a bit. It rubs against the guides. The tension changes as the finger bends, and you keep doing that over and over. Eventually it wears and someone has to replace it. Then get the tension right again. Even the decision to move a motor farther back follows you all the way through to the person fixing the hand later.

Or you can fit more of the machinery into the hand itself, using gears and rigid links to move the joints. We’ve talked about actuators before. For an electric hand, think of the motor and the mechanism carrying its movement out to the joint. Put that assembly close to the finger and the finger carries more hardware. Move it farther back and you have to get the movement out there somehow.

There are different movements to account for too. Curl your index finger. Then spread it sideways. Your thumb can come across your palm and meet the other fingers, which is especially helpful for holding little things. These independent ways of moving are called degrees of freedom (DoF). A hand might have a big DoF number, but the placement of those movements tells you a lot more about what it can do. Having a thumb that can get around an object changes the grip available to the whole hand.

You can also use a single motor to bend several joints together. Pull a tendon, the finger closes, and when one section hits the object the other joints can keep wrapping around it. That’s called underactuation. Fewer actuators than degrees of freedom. It’s a way to get a useful grasp while saving some hardware. For moving the pen around inside the hand, separately controlled joints give you more options. Then the software has to coordinate those options.

Here’s an example from a 2021 research paper. The researchers built the motors, links and sensing into the hand, which they call ILDA. You can see the whole assembly on its own and attached to an arm.

And here is one finger opened up. Look at the little links connecting the joints. All of that has to fit inside something narrow enough to work alongside the other fingers.

Want a stronger grip? A bigger motor can help, and gears can turn fast motor rotation into slower, stronger movement. Then you have the weight of that motor, the heat it makes and the friction in the gears. There’s a little play in the mechanism too. Add the bearings, wires and sensors, then put a cover around everything. Even if you get it all to fit, the arm still has to lift it. So a change that looks pretty straightforward at the motor can end up affecting the whole robot.

MinebeaMitsumi showed a hand prototype at CES 2026 with 85 miniature and small bearings and 11 actuators. It developed the drive unit with Harmonic Drive Systems.

85 bearings in one hand.

That makes the mechanical side of this easier to picture. Every joint has to move smoothly in a small space, over and over. This is a prototype, so it gives us an example of the hardware being developed. As these designs get into production, the particular parts that survive all that testing are the ones a customer would keep ordering.

Even the stiffness is something you can design around. Let a finger give a little when it bumps into an object and it can absorb some of that unexpected contact.

‘Compliance’.

Researchers at the German Aerospace Center have a tendon-driven hand with adjustable stiffness.

It can be firmer for one task and more forgiving for another. Think about first reaching for the bulb, then holding it while the arm moves. Those are different demands on the same fingers.

Getting the finger to the right place

Now say we ask the finger to bend halfway. The motor turns. Did the finger actually get there?

This is where the electronics come in. A motor driver switches power into the motor, and sensors send readings back as it moves. The controller compares the movement it asked for with the readings coming back, adjusts the drive and reads again. It keeps doing that. That’s the control loop, and the sensor is giving it something to correct against each time.

If the sensor is at the motor, it can follow the motor turning. Then there’s a tendon between that motor and the joint. The tendon stretches a little, so the finger ends up somewhere slightly different from where the motor movement would suggest. Put a sensor at the joint and you get a reading farther along that path. The placement is part of the design, just like the placement of the motor was.

Look at this exposed NASA Robonaut hand. There’s quite a bit between the drive coming in and the fingertips reaching the object.

NVE, NVEC, has a little demo with a Lego figure in a gripper. A magnet sits on one jaw and a sensor on the other. As the jaws close, the field at the sensor changes. The controller follows that reading and stops at a set threshold. So you have a magnetic reading helping control the gap, with the Lego figure sitting in between.

Then if we look at the size of NVE’s ALT022, a separate sensor in a package just 0.55 by 0.55 millimeters, you can see why small magnetic sensors would be useful with everything already crammed into a finger. The magnet and its placement still have to fit into the design, and the whole arrangement has to be calibrated. On the October earnings call, management described increasing production into automation and humanoid applications. There is customer work happening here already. The eventual size of the humanoid business will depend on how those designs progress.

ALGM has a named example too. Sarcomere Dynamics says it is putting Allegro’s motion controllers, sensors and power chips into its Scorpion gripper, with plans to extend the work to its next-generation ARTUS Dex hand.

And Allegro is working on combining some of those functions. On its October call, it said samples of a chip combining a magnetic sensor and an intelligent motor driver were planned for its fiscal third quarter. The sensor uses TMR, short for tunneling magnetoresistance. A magnetic field changes its electrical resistance, which gives the electronics a reading to use. Put that sensing together with the driver and the customer has fewer separate pieces to fit around the motor. Think back to that opened-up finger. Saving a bit of space there can leave room for something else.

Feeling what is happening

Let’s have the hand pick up a connector and push it into a socket. It lines up, moves forward and meets resistance. Maybe the connector is going in. Maybe it’s crooked.

The position reading tells the robot where the finger is. Touch gives it more to work with as the connector meets the socket. That becomes especially useful when the hand itself is blocking the camera’s view. It can feel the resistance changing as it pushes, then adjust the movement.

There are a few places to measure it. At the wrist you can measure forces and twisting loads, (torques) passing through the whole hand. At a joint you can measure the load there. And a tactile surface on the fingertip can give a more local picture of where the object is pressing. Imagine the connector sliding across one finger pad as another finger tightens. Those individual readings give the controller a way to follow what is happening inside the grip.

VPG’s force-sensing guide has a ton of great information. One way this works is a small structure bends under a load, and a strain gauge attached to it changes resistance as it stretches or compresses. Measure the change, calibrate it against known loads and you have a force measurement. The gauge is working with the structure it is attached to. Move it somewhere else in the hand and you change what it is measuring.

Which is why a customer can spend quite a while working with a sensor supplier. Build the hand, load the fingers and try different grips. Change the hand and you may have to change some of the sensing along with it. Orders can be coming in through that whole development process.

In March VPG’s second humanoid customer had recently placed around $1.5 million of orders, primarily for tactile sensing in the fingers. That customer was evaluating prototypes. So there was money being spent on getting the fingers to feel, while the customer was still working through the hand design. VPG expected a clearer view of volume after those evaluations.

Its earlier humanoid customer is working on joint sensing. That production start has moved around, with the November update putting it in Q4, later than previously expected. Across VPG, humanoid sales were $300,000 in Q3.

There are other ways to turn contact into a reading. Compress a material and you can change its capacitance (ability to store electrical charge). Put a magnet inside a soft pad and it moves as the pad deforms, changing the field at a sensor underneath.

You can even put a camera inside the fingertip.

With GelSight, the object pushes into a soft, reflective surface. Light shines on the inside and a camera watches the shape change. So a little camera behind the pad gets a view of the contact from underneath. Those images can help estimate force or detect slipping. The robot is using vision to get information about something happening right against its finger.

Here’s C-Sight, another optical sensor. Follow the layers from the surface that touches the object down to the light and camera. In this research, changes in the image are used to estimate how deeply the surface is being pressed.

Meta has been working on both the fingertip and what the robot does with all those readings. It partnered with GelSight to manufacture its DIGIT 360 tactile fingertip and with Wonik Robotics on a hand using its tactile platform.

Once several fingers are sending data, that data has to travel through the hand and the software has to interpret it. Meta’s Digit Plexus handles the shared hardware and software connections, while its Sparsh work trains software to learn from raw touch readings across sensors and tasks.

ALGM also has research on a flexible, strain-sensitive material for uses including finger pads.

So its development work reaches into touch as well as the position sensing and motor drivers we looked at earlier. A finger needs all of those functions, and companies are trying different ways of putting them together.

Mobileye, MBLY, owns Mentee Robotics after completing the acquisition in February 2026. Mentee builds its own hands and actuators, and Mobileye describes motor-based tactile sensing. It also describes the hands as fully backdrivable. Push on a finger and the force can drive movement back through the mechanism. That gives the developer a way to build contact feedback around the actuation system itself. Go back to the first decision about how to move a finger. Mentee is designing that mechanism and its feedback together, so the way it moves also helps determine how it feels contact.

For the soft pads, wear comes into this pretty quickly. Rub one against objects all day and the surface changes. It picks up dirt or gets cut. Eventually someone replaces it, and now the readings may be a little different. That can mean calibrating again before the hand gets back to work. Every decision the robot makes from touch relies on those readings staying useful through all of that.

Keeping the whole grip together

Picture the robot carrying a cup and feeling it start to slip. One finger tightens, which changes how the cup presses against the others. They adjust too. The arm is still moving while this is happening.

All those corrections have to happen fast enough to keep the grip together. A late reading tells the controller what the cup was doing a moment ago. And the readings have to get through the wires we already squeezed around the joints.

Lattice, LSCC, worked with Inova Semiconductors and the German Aerospace Center on a research hand with 18 motors.

Lattice’s chips run control close to those motors. They’re FPGAs, chips whose digital circuitry can be configured for the task, so the team can set them up around the control work this hand needs.

Inova’s network carries the commands and the joint and touch measurements. It schedules the traffic so the more time-sensitive motor messages get through when they should. That lets the team bring the sensor traffic onto a shared network as well, reducing separate bundles of wiring. Think about the number of wires 18 motors and their sensors could otherwise leave you with. Fitting and bending that wiring is part of making the hand work. This project gives the researchers a physical hand to test the arrangement on.

Below is an earlier robot from the German Aerospace Center, SpaceJustin, photographed in 2009. You can see the multi-fingered hands at the ends of those arms. Whatever the arm does next changes the job the fingers are doing too.

Cameras can move closer to the grip as well. Figure describes cameras in the palms of Figure 03, alongside touch sensing. The camera helps with the approach, then the touch readings add information when the fingers meet the object.

OUST has an example through its Stereolabs camera business. In the Trossen setup, two small cameras are at the wrists and another watches the scene.

The wrist cameras move with the arms, keeping a close view of the work. So the system has that view changing with the movement, along with whatever it is measuring inside the grip.

Teaching the hand to use all of this

Getting this hardware together is a lot of work. Then somebody has to teach it to use a pair of scissors.

Developers train the robot from demonstrations, practice and simulation. The software that turns what it observes into actions is called the control policy. In simulation, a virtual hand can try something over and over. It can drop the object, try another grip and keep going without wearing out the real hand. Eventually that behavior goes onto actual hardware, where the friction is slightly different or a tendon stretches more than expected. A grip that worked in the simulation can need more work on the real machine.

And on the real machine, repeated failures can turn into repairs.

That helps explain the newer hand Shadow developed with Google DeepMind.

They designed it around surviving repeated learning experiments. It has three fingers, with 15 Maxon motors across them. Compare that with the older five-finger hand holding the bulb at the beginning. The team has a particular job for this hand, which is spending a lot of time trying things. If it survives more of those attempts, the researchers can keep collecting experience instead of stopping to fix it.

The cost of the hand affects who gets to do that work too. Projects like LEAP Hand aim to make capable hardware more affordable. More labs can get one, and more people can try their ideas on an actual hand.

Google DeepMind’s Gemini Robotics 2 results show how uneven the learning can be from one task to another. The same model checkpoint controls particular configurations of Apptronik’s Apollo with different hands, plus a Franka setup with a gripper. On Apollo with Sharpa hands, it unscrewed a bulb successfully 92% of the time. Screwing it in was 36%.

Same bulb. Now you have to get the threads to catch while controlling the contact, and the success rate changes a lot. A small-looking change in the job can give the hand a much harder problem.

So when a model already has a lot of training behind it, how much more teaching does it take to get a task working on a particular robot? That robot has its own hand, its own movement and its own readings coming back. A demonstration on that machine gives the model something specific to learn from.

NVIDIA tested this in GR00T 2 just recently.

Across its Dexter-50 tasks, the already-trained model averaged 40% success with no demonstrations on the target robot. Give it one demonstration per task for further training and success reached 71%. With five, it reached 95%. That is a big change from a relatively small amount of teaching on the actual robot.

The task selection in the paper is part of those numbers. Researchers first chose tasks the model could learn to at least 80% success with five demonstrations, then assembled the set of 50. Within that selected group, you can see how much the extra examples helped. Every demonstration still takes work to collect. If a developer can get a task running with a handful of examples, that reduces the teaching involved as it adds more tasks and machines.

There’s also the next attempt. The robot misses a grasp, recognizes what happened and tries a slightly different position. Getting that little recovery to work would save someone walking over every time the first attempt went wrong. With enough robots, those interruptions add up.

Putting the hands to work

Here is the research hand from earlier using scissors and tweezers. The fingers hold the tool in place while moving the part that does the work. Once a robot can use something like this, it can start taking on jobs people already do with that tool.

Then run the job for a full shift. The grip has to hold up, the movement has to be fast enough and the cables keep bending. Put people nearby and you also have fingers closing around objects close to their hands. The amount of force and the response to unexpected contact become part of whether the machine can work safely in that space.

Figure’s BMW work gives us actual operating experience here. In its production report, Figure says Figure 02 logged more than 1,250 operating hours and loaded over 90,000 parts. The forearm was the leading hardware failure area. Figure described changes to the wrist electronics and cabling for the next generation.

There’s that crowded wrist again.

BMW confirms Figure 02 contributed to the production of more than 30,000 vehicles. The robot was doing a specific production assignment, putting parts into an actual manufacturing process. That gives the developers a lot to bring back to the next design, including the failures.

Say the repair is a finger that can be swapped by the technician already working there. The robot is down for a bit, the part gets changed and it goes back to work. If the repair takes much longer, or someone has to keep rescuing it during the shift, the customer loses more of the benefit. That’s where the little mechanical decisions from the beginning show up in the economics. How the hand comes apart, how it is calibrated again, whether spares are available. As more machines go in, the same support team has to be able to keep up with them.
