0. Everything you need to know about scale-out and scale-up

Before we start, let’s go over a few basics. For details, I recommend reading the articles I mentioned above. If you are short on time, I hope the short explanation below is enough.

To understand how AI data centers are connected, the two concepts you should know first are scale-up and scale-out.

Scale-up is the connection over which accelerators in the same compute group exchange data directly. Nvidia’s NVLink is the best-known example, and until now these links have mostly run over copper inside a single rack.

Scale-out is that the connection over different compute groups through a server’s network interface card (NIC) and Ethernet or InfiniBand switches.

The figure below shows where the optical engine, which turns electrical signals into light, attaches in each of the two connections.

There are four places where the electrical signal from the switch chip can be converted into light.

From farthest to closest to the chip,

they are the pluggable at the front of the equipment,

OBO (On-Board Optics) on the main board,

NPO (Near Packaged Optics) right next to the chip package,

and CPO (Co Packaged Optics) inside the package.

The closer to the chip, the shorter the distance the electrical signal travels and the lower the power, but the optics have to be assembled and tested together with the chip.

So where does the industry stand today on scale-up and scale-out?

Pluggable optical modules are still the mainstay in scale-out, but CPO has already entered volume production and real deployment. Switch CPO, which puts the optical engines next to the switch chip, has been shipping from Broadcom since 2024 and from Nvidia since 2026, and volumes are expected to grow sharply through 2028.

Scale-up has now started to move from copper to optics. Once a group grows beyond one rack, copper cannot cover the distance, so optics will come in first at the switches between two racks in 2027, and accelerator CPO, which puts the optical engine next to the accelerator chip, is expected to be adopted in earnest around 2028.

Most of the talk in the news and from many experts about CPO being delayed applies to this accelerator side, scale-up CPO.

And they expect that in early scale-up optics, pluggables or NPO plugged into a socket next to the package will grow before CPO does.

A regular switch converts light into electricity so that a chip can read where the data is going, then converts it back into light, but an OCS only changes the path of the light by moving mirrors. 1. Switch optics have already entered the CPO stage

Nvidia and Broadcom lead volume production

Nvidia has two scale-out CPO switches. For InfiniBand, which Nvidia effectively supplies on its own, it is the 115.2T Quantum-X Photonics, and for standard Ethernet it is the 102.4T Spectrum-X Photonics. Spectrum-X Photonics entered full production in August this year, and Nvidia said on its November earnings call that scale-out CPO is in volume production.

Broadcom was the first in the industry to put a CPO switch chip into volume production with the 51.2T Bailly and deployed it to hyperscaler customers including Meta, and its follow-on, the 102.4T Davisson, is reported to be in customer testing and qualification as of 2026. According to Broadcom’s August roadmap, it plans to reach Davisson’s volume and cost targets in 2027 and to launch a fourth-generation 204.8T product using 400G lanes the same year.

Industry Insight

As I said in my CPO technology article earlier this year, the benefit of CPO that customers report most clearly during development for volume production is power. According to industry experts, both Nvidia’s and Broadcom’s products cut power consumption by at least 44% and as much as 58%.

I am also hearing meaningful things about reliability.

For CPO switch reliability, the most important question is optical link reliability. According to reliability validation engineers, CPO switches have reportedly recorded a 0% failure rate over a meaningful period of operation in real field data.

In short, I’m very pleased with these numbers for such an early stage of bringup. As production ramps up, I’ll be watching for any reliability issues that emerge at scale and tracking whether yields stay high enough to support volume production.

Nvidia and Broadcom designed their optical engines differently

FYI

An optical engine: a small chip that converts the electrical signals into light

A lnae: electrical signal line running between the chip and the engine.

Both Nvidia and Broadcom run 200Gb/s per lane, but Nvidia attaches 32 engines of 3.2T, each bundling 16 lanes, while Broadcom attaches 16 engines of 6.4T, each bundling 32 lanes, and their modulators and fiber connections also differ.

So, what explains these design differences?

Nvidia appears to have chosen microring modulators for density at the edge of the chip.

Why does density matter?

Because, they are already considering this technology extends to scale-up. That is, they are considering the situation where it makes it easier to fit optical engines into a GPU package whose edges are already taken up by HBM. So if Nvidia ramps microring engines in switches first and gets temperature control and yield down, it can move the same devices and process straight to optical engines next to the GPU.

Broadcom, on the other hand, chose to keep using a proven process. They has already demonstrated the reliability of Mach–Zehnder modulators in Bailly, one of its early CPO products. These modulators tolerate temperature swings well, which gives Broadcom an advantage in reliability. The tradeoff is that the more lanes an engine contains, the more likely it is that at least one will be defective. If Broadcom has to scrap an engine, it loses all 32 lanes, twice as many as it would lose with a single NVIDIA engine.

I’ll go into more detail in the supply chain section, but I think Broadcom’s decision to put 32 lanes in each engine may reflect its confidence in both yield and reliability.

Btw, technically, one thing to keep in mind is the weaknesses of both choices grow as lane speed rises to 400G. Going forward, when analyzing scale-out CPO, it will likely be important to track technical changes from two angles:

the modulator choice for 400G lanes and engine attach yield.

Industry Insight

In particular, TSMC’s microring roadmap puts 400G in 2030, so for Nvidia’s 2028 Feynman to move to 400G lanes, it would have to pull that roadmap in by two years, use a different modulator, or add more lanes. From this angle, when and how Nvidia solves this technical problem looks set to settle the Feynman delay rumors.

Scale-out CPO and pluggables will coexist, and optical revenue will shift

A common misconception is that once CPO is used, pluggables will no longer be used.

I’ve been saying this for a long time, but it’s worth emphasizing again.

Even as CPO gains adoption, pluggable optics will still be necessary, and the two will continue to coexist. Here’s why.

  1. First, there are four optical connection points along the path from the server through the switches. Even if every

switch moves to CPO, the connection at the server’s NIC still needs a pluggable module.

  1. Second, CPO is unlikely to replace pluggables at all three connection points on the switch side. That’s because the

optical engines in a CPO switch are integrated into the switch chip package, which can introduce physical constraints in deployment depending on the equipment. For example, NVIDIA’s CPO switches require liquid cooling, so data centers without that infrastructure cannot deploy them.

  1. Third, the operational flexibility of pluggable optics gives them a lasting economic advantage.

In addition, pluggable optics are already narrowing the gap with CPO. LPO can cut power consumption in an 800G optical module by 50% by eliminating the DSP. USFP addresses bandwidth limits by supporting 6.4 Tbps in a single module.

In the model I’ll discuss in Section 4, pluggable optics still account for 72% of all scaleout ports in 2030. Even as CPO captures a share of switch ports, I expect revenue from scaleout pluggables to nearly double, from $28.8 billion in 2026 to $55.6 billion in 2030.

That means we need to track both the optical revenue shifting to CPO and the revenue optical module suppliers continue to earn from NIC ports and from switch ports that still use pluggables.

2. Accelerator optics expand beyond the rack

Scale-up beyond the rack moves from copper to optics

Scale-up connections are now moving to optics, and as I said in my previous article, six companies, AMD, Broadcom, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and OpenAI, formed the OCI (Optical Compute Interconnect) MSA in March to standardize this signal and are defining the signal spec.

The first generation of OCI carries eight different wavelengths of light in both directions over a single fiber to send 200G per direction up to about 500m, and the second generation targets 400G per direction. OCI is starting out in pluggables, and each company is trying to take it to the CPO level. Let’s look at the details in the next section.

3. The CPO supply chain splits into Nvidia-style division of labor and Broadcom-style vertical integration

Nvidia controls its supply chain, Broadcom built its own

You can think of the CPO supply chain in two types: the division-of-labor model, like Nvidia’s, which designs only the chips and optical engines and splits the rest among specialist companies, and the vertically integrated model, like Broadcom’s, which makes even its own lasers. Once you understand this, you can also see why Marvell recently went to such lengths to acquire Lumentum.

Nvidia’s division-of-labor model picks the best company for each layer, so it can ramp volume quickly without building its own factories. The downside is that capacity and yield are split across many companies. The yield of a single Nvidia CPO switch is the product of the yields of TSMC’s optical engines, SPIL’s packaging, Lumentum’s lasers and Foxconn’s assembly, and when a defect shows up, the cause can span several companies, so finding it takes time. As a result, Nvidia’s CPO volume is set by the capacity of the slowest layer, and the revenue is spread across many companies in the supply chain instead of concentrating in one. This also appears to be why rumors of delays in Nvidia’s Feynman CPO shipments keep coming up.

Broadcom’s vertically integrated model works the other way. It makes the chip, the optical engine and even the CW laser itself, which gives it the advantage of controlling its own supply of InP lasers, the hardest part to get. To that end, Broadcom has in fact been steadily expanding its laser fabs. The difference in optical engine technology between the two companies described above is easier to understand when you read it again through this supply chain structure. For Nvidia, which has to design around the yields of many companies, it ends up being economically better to split the optical engines into small units and test them all before attaching them. Broadcom, on the other hand, has built its supply chain by developing its own chips, optical engines and lasers generation after generation, so it is more economical for it to keep using the optical engines and the packaging and fiber attach processes it has already proven. It is a cost and time advantage that only a vertically integrated company can have.

So the most important pieces here are manufacturing, packaging and test. Whether it is Nvidia’s model or Broadcom’s, to actually manufacture optical products, both companies have to go through TSMC and SPIL. This means that no matter whose CPO sells more, the volume goes to these two companies, and no CPO shipments, from anyone, can grow faster than their capacity.

With lasers, on the other hand, Broadcom makes its own, so outside laser companies’ switch CPO revenue has to depend heavily on how many Nvidia CPO switches sell. If Marvell, which had no laser fab, acquires Lumentum, it becomes a vertically integrated company like Broadcom that supplies everything from chips to lasers, and it also takes the laser revenue that goes into Nvidia’s CPO. Coherent’s move to supply both CPO and NPO with PhotonLink, which bundles optical engines, lasers and fiber components, is also a strategic step to avoid relying only on one chip company’s volume.

As scale-out CPO is deployed and matures, pluggable optical module companies and DSP companies will see their share of each port shrink. That is because there is no DSP in a CPO port, and the switch chip company supplies the optical engines along with the chip. On the scale-up accelerator side, by contrast, OCI is standardizing the signal and the Open CPX MSA is standardizing the connection for socketed NPO engines, so suppliers can be spread across more companies than on the switch side, where chip companies sell engines as a bundle, and the market can also be larger. In particular, as mentioned earlier, accelerator optics are only now starting with OCI pluggables, so a new market can open up for optical module companies too. So when looking at optical component and module companies, it will become more important to watch the size of the new revenue on the accelerator side than the share they lose on the switch side.

Optical component generations change with lane speed and laser power

To understand optical interconnect components, you mainly need to look at three: the laser that creates light, the modulator that puts the signal onto the light, and the PD (photodiode) that turns light back into electricity. For details, I recommend my previous optics articles.

Among optical components, the most important is probably the laser. There is talk of alternative lasers such as GaAs-based VCSELs, but CPO and NPO basically use InP lasers, so InP laser companies capture that market demand directly.

One thing to watch in scale-out optics in particular is that every time lane speed changes, pluggable EMLs and CPO are about one to two years apart. If you understand this sequence, you can guess that companies making 400G EMLs and PDs will see pluggable upgrade demand for one to two years before CPO comes in, and I expect that window to fall in 2027 to 2028, when optical module companies can grow fastest.

Another thing to watch in scale-up optics is that it needs a different kind of laser. Switch CPO in scale-out splits one high-power single-wavelength laser across multiple lanes, but scale-up optics like OCI carry eight wavelengths on a single fiber, so they need a multi-wavelength light source with precisely set wavelength spacing. Fewer companies can make this kind of light source than high-power single-wavelength lasers, so as accelerator optics grow, laser companies’ pricing power looks set to grow more on the accelerator side than on the switch side.

Industry Insight

Personally, now that the optics war has started, I see InP laser fab capacity as the key to the supply chain.

InP laser fab expansions are concentrated in 2027 and 2028, and many field experts point to one thing the market is still overlooking: Broadcom’s expansion of its own InP capacity.

In fact, according to Broadcom’s announcements, it is building a sizable InP fab of its own in Singapore, and I am told experts expect its capacity to reach nearly 10 times today’s level by 2030.

My personal view is that if this is right, Nvidia’s aggressive investments in laser companies and Marvell’s overreaching attempt to acquire Lumentum make a bit more sense. After 2028, when Broadcom’s own capacity grows sharply, a cost and supply gap can open up between companies that make their own lasers and companies that buy them.

For Nvidia and Marvell, which do not make lasers, there is no way to close this gap other than securing laser capacity in advance or owning a laser company outright.

4. AI optical interconnect revenue grows to about $100 billion in 2030

The most important part of this forecast is that revenue depends more on the optical bandwidth each accelerator uses than on the number of accelerators. You should not approach it with the usual calculation used in earlier analyses.

In the model I use, accelerator shipments grow 1.9x by 2030, but scale-out bandwidth per accelerator triples. The share of accelerators that use optics for scale-up beyond the rack also rises from 10% to 26%, so even if the price per port falls by nearly half, I think the optical interconnect content per accelerator can still rise from $2,211 to $3,857.

One more thing to note is that about 40% of the $69.3 billion increase comes from scale-up. Scale-out is already connected with optics, so it grows only as fast as bandwidth, while scale-up creates a new market as copper is replaced with optics. Optical module companies also need to be analyzed with this point in mind, and I see their fastest growth coming in 2027 to 2028.

My model rests on the following premises.

Around 2028, 400G lanes need to become mainstream so that the EMLs and PDs in a 1.6T port are cut in half, and for the high-power lasers that CPO uses, InP fabs and the InP substrates upstream of them have to keep up with 2027 to 2028 volumes.

Under these premises, when inputs such as bandwidth per accelerator and the share of accelerators using optics for scale-up beyond the rack are lowered together, the bear scenario comes to $73.8 billion in 2030, and when they are raised together, the bull scenario comes to $135.4 billion.

Even the bear scenario is 2.4 times the 2026 level, so the direction of growth is clear. Going forward, we will need to keep watching these points in the optical supply chain and analyze how fast scale-up moves from copper to optics and which companies see that revenue first.

In particular, along the way, the companies that hold the tightest supplies, InP lasers and optical engine packaging, look set to gain a competitive edge and capture volume first.
