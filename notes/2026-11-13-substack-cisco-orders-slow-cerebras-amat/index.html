CSCO -3%: FY27 raise and $9B AI guide clear the bogeys, but orders slow to +21% (vs. low-30s bogey) and mgmt stopped quantifying hyperscale orders

Stock initially popped 10% last night on the beat and raise but faded as investors realized mgmt didn’t release the orders number in the release like they usually do and there was a reason why: only 21% vs bogeys of low 30s.

Despite the big headline numbers, product orders slowed to +21% from +35% (ex-hyperscale +14% from +25%), and mgmt stopped quantifying hyperscale orders. Robbins only said hyperscale orders grew nicely y/y vs. “$1 billion and some change” a year ago. Rough math (total +21% vs. ex-hyperscale +14%, on a ~$1.3B year-ago base) implies ~$2.2–2.4B, vs. $4B in FQ4 (down ~40%+ q/q). The guide also implies 2H GM steps down to ~64–64.5%. Mgmt’s message is a multi-year supercycle with five drivers, a campus refresh <10% through, and early post-Mythos security orders.

Bulls will point to a 4th straight acceleration (+24.4% y/y on a flat comp; Networking +43%), a record 37.8% OM, an F2Q GM guide ~110bps above Street’s 64.4% (August’s was ~87bps under) and an implied 2H of ~$38.7B vs. Street’s $37.03B.

Bears will lean on slower product orders and weaker implied hyperscaler orders, , F1Q GM missing the bogeys with product GM down 355bps y/y to 63.7%, and an F2Q guide of -2.3% q/q at the mid vs. +3.1% and +1.1% in the last two Jan quarters.

Details:

F1Q revenue of $18.52B (+24.4% y/y, CSCO’s strongest revenue growth “in over 15 years”) beat Street ($18.07B) and the ~$18.3-18.45B bogeys, a 2.3% beat on the guide mid (2.7% in F4Q) and a 5th straight quarter above the top. GM of 65.6% was in line with Street (65.6%) and missed the ~65.8-66% bogeys, but OM hit a record 37.8% (Street 36.2%) and EPS of $1.40 beat Street ($1.32) and the ~$1.35-1.37 bogeys.

The F2Q revenue guide of $18.0-18.2B (mid $18.1B, +17.9% y/y) beat Street ($17.80B) and was in line with the ~$18.0-18.3B mid bogeys; GM of 65-66% beat Street (64.4%) and the ~64.5-65% mid bogeys; EPS of $1.34-1.36 ($1.35 mid; implied OI ~$6.61B vs. Street $6.18B) beat Street ($1.23)

FY27 revenue guide went to $74.8-75.8B (mid $75.3B, +$2.5B), beating Street ($72.92B) and the ~$73.2-73.7B mid bogeys, and EPS to $5.47-5.53 ($5.50 mid), beating Street ($5.10) and the ~$5.14-5.20 mid bogeys, and the FY27 hyperscale AI revenue view went to $9B and beat the ~$8.0-8.5B bogeys. Key for call will be product orders, which weren’t in the release (~31-35% bogeys; +35% in F4Q).

Key Takeaways

  • Callback. The most important post-call message was a data-driven rebuttal of pull-forward. Software activations are at an all-time low (they spiked in past supply crunches when customers stockpiled), pipeline conversion and distributor inventory are normal, and the forward pipeline is still growing q/q, which it wouldn’t be if demand had been pulled in. Mgmt also said four other things. The couple-of-points campus decel reflects tough comps after consecutive years of double-digit growth, not saturation. The 2H GM step-down comes in FQ3, with FQ4 at a similar level, and is driven by mix rather than a worsening memory backdrop. FY27 opex should grow mid-single digits. And CSCO will participate in scale-up, with wins to be announced as engagements mature. Net, the callback de-risked the GM path and gave bulls a pull-forward rebuttal, but it didn’t put a number back on hyperscale orders.
  • Products. Silicon One is now in parts of the enterprise portfolio, and full conversion by FY29 is on track, which over time removes the margin CSCO pays to third-party chip suppliers. Multi-rail optical systems arrive in early CY27, and the USFP optical module with NVDA is a few years out. Cisco IQ has 17,000+ customers (nearly doubled q/q from ~8,600 in August), and Cloud Control has 6,000+ sign-ups (from ~4,500). Together they are effectively the funnel for finding end-of-support and vulnerable gear ahead of the refresh. Strategic additions this quarter include a Supermicro partnership for the Secure AI Factory with NVDA, AMD GPU/Silicon One clusters in production at HUMAIN, sovereign critical infrastructure expanded to Canada (India pre-announced), Mythos integrated into AI Defense for adaptive red teaming, and the WideField acquisition for the agentic security operations center.
  • AI. FY27 hyperscale AI revenue was raised to ~$9B from ~$7.5B (vs. buyside ~$8.0–8.5B). That is +~125% y/y and ~12% of revenue (vs. ~6% in FY26 and <2% in FY25), and it drove $1.5B of the $2.5B revenue raise. Mgmt credited 90 more days of visibility and order flow. Post-call, it added that existing customers asked for earlier ship dates on backlog alongside new bookings, so part of the raise is timing. Three new design wins bring the count to 18 in five quarters: two P200 systems for DCI/scale-across, both with one “leading technology company,” and a first client-side 1.6T optics win at a hyperscaler. Scale-across is now in production, G300 has begun shipping into labs, and mgmt sees line-of-sight to more wins over the next six months across G300/G200/P200/A100. The $9B is ~50/50 systems/optics and, outside optics, 100% Silicon One; P200 is not yet a material part of it. Optics remain strong, with record FQ1 Acacia AI orders after a $1B FQ4, and 800G-ZR revenue now exceeds 400G-ZR. Outside hyperscalers, AI orders from neocloud, sovereign and enterprise customers accelerated (AI-tagged Nexus +47%). Neocloud exposure is “relatively immaterial” given credit conservatism and is excluded from the $9B. Customer concentration rises as a handful of hyperscalers become ~12% of sales with little services attached.
  • Campus. Campus orders grew 14% (from +20%), a second straight decel. Mgmt says the next-gen portfolio is ramping faster than prior launches and is only ~9% of campus switching sold (<10% of orders), against a $100B+ refresh opportunity. Wireless orders are up 20%+, and Wi-Fi 7 is now >60% of AP orders (from >50% of wireless orders in FQ4), which pulls through multi-gig switch upgrades. Industrial IoT orders were ~+50%, the 10th straight double-digit quarter, helped by data center powered-shell builds. Post-Mythos demand moved from pipeline (August) to early orders this quarter, though customers are still in the planning phase.
  • Security. Revenue grew 7% y/y, in line with Street ~$2.11B; last q’s +14% was boosted by large Splunk on-prem deals. Mgmt called 7% at or above plan, but its August framing for FQ1 was mid-to-high single digits, so this was in line rather than a beat. Leading indicators were better. Ex-Splunk security orders grew 15%, the best in ~3 years, and firewall orders grew >30% for a third straight quarter. Zero Trust Secure Access reached 5,400 customers (~800 added in FQ1), and AI Defense is nearing 500 customers and protecting 2T tokens/month. Splunk turned positive, with growth expected to improve through FY27. Observability grew 2% and slightly missed Street ~$283M.
  • Pricing. Mgmt still assumes ~5 pts of FY27 revenue growth from price (vs. “four to five points” in August), weighted to 1H as the hardware increases anniversary. Post-call, mgmt said more recent increases skew to software and security, have a smaller revenue impact and did not drive the raise. Customers are absorbing single-digit, memory-driven hardware hikes. The math matters for FY28: ~5 pts of price inside ~11–12% core growth means core volume growth is ~6–7%, and the price tailwind fades through 2H.

Bull vs Bear Debate

Bulls see CSCO as the broadest beneficiary of an AI-driven networking supercycle, not a single-product AI trade. It is one of the few vendors covering the full stack a distributed AI build needs: Silicon One chips, 8000/Nexus systems, Acacia coherent optics, open line systems, security and observability. That breadth is why hyperscale AI revenue has gone from <2% of sales in FY25 to ~6% in FY26 and ~12% ($9B) in FY27, with 18 design wins in five quarters. Scale-across is the next leg. As power limits push GPU clusters across multiple sites, CSCO estimates data center interconnect bandwidth rises ~14x and coherent ports per deployment go from 1–2K to 12–32K. It is a category where CSCO can sell silicon, systems, pluggables and line systems together. Bulls also see compute diversification as a structural tailwind, because each additional accelerator platform that reaches production (AMD, Maia, MTIA and others) enlarges the slice of AI networking that independent Ethernet suppliers can compete for. NVDA’s abrupt CEO transition (Huang resigned this week, with CFO Colette Kress named interim CEO) could, at the margin, reinforce hyperscalers’ multi-vendor strategies, which mgmt says expands CSCO’s TAM.

On the enterprise side, bulls think the refresh has years to run. The next-gen campus portfolio is only ~9% of switching sold, against a $100B+ refresh opportunity. Wi-Fi 7 (>60% of AP orders) forces multi-gig switch upgrades. Gear past its last day of support can’t be patched, which becomes a liability as AI-driven vulnerability discovery spreads. Customers are now attributing orders to post-Mythos risk, so the refresh starts to behave like required security spend rather than a discretionary IT line item. On top of that come sovereign infrastructure (Europe, Canada, India, HUMAIN, G42) and enterprise private AI: data center switching orders have grown double digits in 9 of the last 11 quarters, and AI-tagged Nexus orders are +47%. Security finally has order momentum too, with ex-Splunk orders +15% and firewall orders >30% for three straight quarters.

This quarter ticked the bull boxes on the P&L: +24% growth, an AI raise above every bogey, a $1.0B core raise, a better-than-feared FQ2 GM, and an EPS raise ~2.5x the revenue raise. That last point shows hyperscale revenue adds to OM because it needs little extra selling cost. The post-call operating data (record-low software activations, normal channel inventory, a growing pipeline) also gives bulls a direct rebuttal to pull-forward. Bulls think estimates can keep rising. CSCO has beaten its EPS guide midpoint by ~3.7% on average, and the 2H guide explicitly builds in lower visibility. P200 isn’t yet material to the $9B. Converting the enterprise portfolio to Silicon One by FY29 should cut what CSCO pays third-party chip suppliers.

Bears argue CSCO is a cyclical hardware story at peak growth, and that FY27 is flattered by three things that won’t repeat. The first is ~5 pts of memory-driven price increases that anniversary in 2H. The second is a lumpy and concentrated hyperscale ramp. The third is enterprise orders pulled forward ahead of price hikes and tight supply: channel checks earlier this year pointed to customers ordering early to lock in CY26 projects, and more recent checks suggest customers aren’t waiting because they expect further hikes. The order data now looks like a peak. Total orders slowed from +35% to +21%, and ex-hyperscale from +25% to +14% (roughly high-single digits ex-price). Campus and public sector decelerated again, product RPO slowed, and total RPO fell q/q. And mgmt chose this quarter to drop orders from the release and stop quantifying hyperscale orders, which likely fell ~40%+ q/q. Part of the AI raise reflects customers pulling in ship dates on existing backlog, which borrows from FY28 rather than creating new demand, and the two new P200 wins came from a single customer.

Meanwhile the mix is deteriorating. Product GM is down 350 bps y/y, the guide implies 2H GM of ~64–64.5%, memory has climbed to ~20% of switch BOM, and subscription revenue has slipped to 44% of sales. CSCO has also locked in $21.9B of purchase commitments (2.6x a year ago) and $6.4B of inventory at elevated component prices, a classic late-cycle exposure if demand or memory prices roll over. FCF already missed (~$3.9B vs. ~$4.7B). The bull assumptions most at risk are FY28 growth and the multiple. Against a +19% FY27 comp, losing ~4–5 pts of price, working off pulled-forward demand and lapping lumpy hyperscale shipments could leave FY28 growth in the low-to-mid single digits, not the ~8% the Street models. Competition is intensifying on both fronts. NVDA’s Spectrum-X Ethernet has won hyperscale fabrics at Meta and Oracle, CSCO’s AI back-end gains skew to lower-value leaf/ToR switching, and Arista expects campus revenue to grow ~60% to ~$2B in 2027, a direct attack on CSCO’s ~50% campus share. The bear case strengthened on durability, with a second straight campus and enterprise decel, slowing product RPO, an FQ2 guide below the last two Januaries’ seasonality, an AI raise partly built on schedule pull-ins, an FCF miss and sharply higher purchase commitments.

CBRS +12%: Hardware-led beat/raise and two new hyperscalers reset a washed-out bar; OpenAI cloud ramp is now a 2H27 story but some overhangs lifted. Signed MSFT, 3rd hyperscaler after ORCL and AWS.

Positioning tilted negative to say the least as stock as has steadily declined from IPO earlier this year to under $100 so this print and OAI commentary good enough to clear a pretty washed out bar. Q3 core revs of $231M beat street at $215M driven by hardware although cloud missed and guided flat bc OAI capacity running late. Q4 rev guide of $320M was ~$50M above street. Neither the MSFT and ORCL deals are in RPO yet. The 2027 “triple” (~$2.85B) was only reiterated (although previous language was “more than triple”), roughly in line with Street.

The CFO sees “flattish” 4Q cloud, “limited entirely by our data center capacity.” If cloud holds at 3Q’s ~$145M, the $320M 4Q guide mid needs ~$175M of hardware, double 3Q’s $86M. OpenAI was the worry going and grew 27% q/q. Mgmt blamed the OpenAI delays solely on site construction, said under 5% of the 750MW deal could be cancelled this year and that CBRS expects to catch up by 2H27, but conceded credits already deducted from RPO and didn’t size it; Feldman expects OpenAI to use “a range of hardware solutions” but also said that labs brining up new chips in days erodes incumbent’s sw moat, which is good for CBRS.

Key Takeaways

  • OpenAI. OpenAI revenue grew to $72M of GAAP revenue (+27% q/q, 34% of the total). But CBRS is behind on the initial tranches of the 750MW take-or-pay because land, power and shell (LPS) arrived late. Mgmt conceded it lacked the capacity for OpenAI’s broad Ultrafast rollout, including Astra, which NVDA publicly said runs on its GPUs. It called claims of a technical shortfall false. The contract mechanics are new:
  • OpenAI earns delay credits, which are already estimated and deducted from RPO; mgmt called its estimate “very

conservative.”

  • Credits can escalate up to 100% of a delayed installment.
  • After 90+ days either side can cancel only the delayed tranche.
  • <5% of committed MW (~37MW, roughly $1–1.4B of contract value at most) is exposed this year, nothing has been

cancelled, and mgmt projects being back on schedule in 2H27.

Also new: OpenAI can elect hardware instead of cloud under the contract, though its own DCs are also late. Mgmt said it has “not even begun to discuss” the 1.25GW option, pushing back on intra-quarter reports that OpenAI had passed. In August, mgmt said the RPO set up “a massive 2027, 2028, and 2029,” and OpenAI drove +60% q/q cloud growth. The contracted ramp has since shifted roughly two quarters to the right.

  • Mix. The beat and the guide are hardware, not cloud. Core hardware of $86.0M (-11% y/y, +5% q/q) beat Street ~$61–67M by ~$20M. Core cloud of $144.9M (+272% y/y; +13.5% q/q after +60% in Q2) missed Street ~$148–153M. Q4 cloud is guided ~flat, so hardware more than doubles q/q to ~$175M on new deals, including ORCL, that ship from Q4 through at least 1H27. That takes hardware “closer to 50%” of revenue in Q4 and 1Q27, from 37% in Q3. Mgmt reframed CBRS as “an AI hardware company with two channels to market” and said “We are not a neocloud.” That is a clear shift from August’s private-cloud emphasis, likely aimed at the neocloud de-rating. Mgmt also positioned CBRS as model-agnostic across frontier, open-weight (Kimi K3.1) and agentic workloads. Hardware turns into cash quickly because customers pay before shipment and CBRS spends no capex, but it is lumpy and depends on customers’ DCs, which mgmt said are also running late.
  • Margins. Core GM of 41.0% beat Street 39.2% and the 38–40% guide, and mgmt said the low point was actually Q2 rather than the guided Q3. Q4 is guided up ~700bps to 47–49% vs. Street ~42%, with GM above 50% “in the near term” and moving toward the 60% LT target in 2027. Hardware GM fell to 31% (Q2: 38.8%) on the final shipments under a legacy low-priced agreement. New orders ship at “significantly higher current prices,” and mgmt pointed to a 50% LT hardware GM target. Cloud GM rose 510bps q/q to 46.9% on higher-priced new business. The catch: in August the Q4 step-up was supposed to come from CBRS-owned DC capacity replacing costlier systems it rents back from a customer. It now comes from hardware pricing and mix, and the rent-back lasts until at least mid-2027, so cloud GM durability is unproven. The FY26 GM and OM ranges (42–44%, (10%)–(8%)) also sit below what the Q4 guide implies for the year (~44.4%, ~(7.4%)), which builds in cushion.
  • Profitability. Core OM of (14.3%) beat Street ~(24%) on opex of $128M vs. Street ~$136M. Q4 is guided to ~breakeven ((1%)–1%) vs. Street ~(25%), with “significant progress” toward the 40% LT OM target during 2027. The first positive core EPS ($0.12 vs. Street $(0.14)–(0.15)) is mostly interest income, though. Core operating loss was flat q/q at $(33)M, while other income was $64M ($82M of interest less $19M of non-cash interest on the OpenAI loan). Roughly three-quarters of the EPS beat came below the line. GAAP remains deeply negative (GM 20%, OM (106%), EPS $(0.67)), driven by $155M of SBC and $36M of customer-warrant contra-revenue. That contra-revenue equals 16% of core revenue, and the $1.1B warrant asset is charged against revenue through October 2031.
  • Guide. Q4 core revenue of $310–330M (+87% y/y, +39% q/q) compares with Street ~$271–275M. FY26 was raised to $940–960M (+86% y/y) from $880–890M vs. Street ~$887M. 2027 was reiterated, not raised. The CEO and the release now say “triple,” vs. “more than triple” in August, although the CFO still called “more than triple” a target CBRS is “well-positioned to achieve.” Three times the new FY26 midpoint is ~$2.85B vs. Street ~$2.86–2.98B (sources vary), so the Q4 raise did not carry into 2027. Mgmt deferred a full 2027 outlook, including mix, to the Q4 call, noting hardware timing is “very reliant” on customers having their DCs ready.
  • Customers. MSFT signed a cloud deal in October, with revenue starting in 1H27 and details to follow then. ORCL is buying hardware for OCI data centers in the US, with service launching in summer 2027 and described as “the start of a large partnership.” With AWS (Bedrock targeted for 1Q27 as of August), that makes three hyperscalers in six months. In August mgmt said hyperscaler revenue would start mid-2027; MSFT now starts in 1H27. MSFT signed in the same month it laid out multi-GW Maia 300 deployment plans from 2027. That suggests hyperscalers want CBRS for the ultra-low-latency tier their own silicon does not serve. Mgmt framed demand as limited only by supply (“demand... continues to outpace supply by a wide margin”), and the pipeline grew “much more than 10x.” Neocloud buyers now include Fireworks, and Bilt adds a consumer-scale app. Concentration is still extreme near-term: the largest customer (historically G42) was 38% of Q3 GAAP revenue (70% a year ago) and OpenAI 34%.

Manufacturing exits 2026 at more than 10x the start of the year, with more factories planned for 2027, and CBRS avoids HBM and CoWoS. Contracted DC capacity rose to ~700 gross MW for delivery by YE27, from 600MW in August. It starts ramping in December, with “the bulk” arriving from mid-2027. More than 75% of 2027 capacity is outside the US: Canada, France and Norway come online in the next few months, Norway and Finland are signed, and São Paulo is near-final. New this quarter, mgmt flagged the “evolving U.S. policy landscape” as a growing obstacle to US DC development. This matters for all of AI infrastructure, since mgmt said “everybody’s data centers are late,” including OpenAI’s and those being built for hyperscalers.
  • RPO. RPO is $26.1B (+$0.7B, +3% q/q) after deducting estimated delay credits, which sell-side math puts at ~$0.6–0.9B, and before ORCL and MSFT. Conversion moved forward: ~33% (~$8.6B) is now scheduled for the next 24 months vs. 22% (~$5.6B) last quarter. Part of that is the 24-month window rolling forward into a ramp weighted to later years. With 2027 framed at ~$2.85B, the schedule implies more than $5B of contracted revenue lands in 1Q–3Q28 if delivery holds to the new timeline.
  • Competition. Mgmt put numbers on where CBRS beats low-batch GPUs on cost for the first time (see quote #3). It called CBRS “the fastest inference solution, bar none,” despite low-batch GPUs, mega kernels and hyperscaler custom silicon; an analyst question also flagged OpenAI’s own Jalapeño chip. Disaggregated inference, with AMD GPUs handling prefill and CS-3 handling decode, is now in production as promised in August, delivering up to 5x more fast tokens per MW. The CEO argued that labs moving models to new silicon in days (Anthropic on AMD in ~3 days) weakens incumbents’ software moats and shifts value to differentiated hardware. Pricing is “healthy,” and cloud customers say CBRS tokens are “the most profitable tokens they sell.”
  • Roadmap. CS-4 is shipping, and CS-4 overtakes CS-3 in shipments in 1Q27, with 2x performance and throughput and higher ASPs. WSE-5 taped out June 15 and is already running models in system test. CS-5 production is set for early summer 2027, vs. 2H27 on the August call, at more than 2x CS-4 speed on TSMC 5nm, targeting up to 10,000 tok/s per user. Mgmt spent unusual time rebutting “persistent falsehoods” on model size and on yields. It described fab and in-spec yields as high, citing spare tiles that route around defects, cutting off the wafer edges, and voltage tuning across 84 wafer sections. It did not directly address the short report’s claim that the chips last 1.5–2.5 years vs. the 3–5 years CBRS has cited, beyond citing “strong in-field reliability.” That matters for cloud depreciation and resale value. Inventory charges tied to the product transition were $4.3M in Q3 ($23.5M YTD).

Bull vs. Bear Debate

Bulls see CBRS as the only proven, at-scale alternative chip architecture in AI inference, and the clear leader in its fastest-growing, highest-value slice. Its wafer-scale chip keeps memory on-chip (SRAM), which delivers per-user speeds GPUs cannot match. The fastest-growing workloads, such as agentic coding, multi-step agents, voice and real-time apps, are exactly the ones where speed decides product quality. Mgmt’s math says CBRS is the cheaper way to serve anything above ~250–300 tok/s per user, and its cloud customers call CBRS tokens their most profitable.

The moat goes beyond the chip design. CBRS needs no HBM or advanced packaging (CoWoS) at a time when mgmt expects memory shortages and price increases to last through 2028. Its 5nm wafers are cheaper, TSMC supply is secured for 2027, and it can sell either systems or tokens while paying only build cost rather than a GPU vendor’s margin. The roadmap compounds the lead: CS-4 at 2x, then CS-5 at more than 2x again from early summer 2027. Disaggregation turns the installed base of GPUs into a sales channel rather than a competitor, with up to 5x more fast tokens per MW and markedly more revenue per data center. Bulls also like the CEO’s argument that labs moving models across silicon in days weakens incumbents’ software moats, shifting value toward differentiated hardware.

This quarter gave bulls evidence on the two concerns stacked on top of OpenAI:

Bull vs. Bear Case

The bull thesis is that AI has turned WFE from a cyclical capex line into a multi-year capacity build, and that AMAT owns the part of it with the most leverage. Mgmt says leading-edge logic, DRAM and AP are >85% of the silicon in an AI data center and ~75% of WFE growth through 2030. AMAT addresses ~55% of WFE, with #1 positions at the leading edge in deposition, CMP, implant, epitaxy, thermal, eBeam and conductor etch. Equipment spending per wafer rises with each technology step: WFE dollars per wafer start go up ~1.6x from 5nm to 1.4nm in logic and ~2x in advanced DRAM, and 4F² adds >40% to SAM vs. 6F². So AMAT grows faster than wafer starts even at constant share. Bulls expect share gains at gate-all-around, backside power, 4F²/3D DRAM and hybrid bonding to deliver the >2-point WFE share gain targeted for 2030. On top of the core, bulls see three businesses the market undervalues: AP (~90% growth, >60% share of serviceable HBM packaging), process control (>50% growth, eBeam 2x its nearest competitor) and AGS. AGS is a ~$7.3B business growing mid-teens, with more customers moving to subscription contracts and >90% renewal rates. Bulls view these as lasting advantages that make AMAT’s earnings larger and steadier than in past cycles.

This quarter gave bulls most of what they needed. Customers are asking to speed up deliveries rather than delay them, and the fab tracker added >10 projects for a third straight quarter, many set for 2029–30. Customer deposits rose ~40% y/y. Bulls read mgmt’s first CY27 call (>30% WFE, AMAT growing faster) as a floor, since CY26 began at >20% and is ending at ~42%. The CY27 mix also moves AMAT’s way: NAND, its weakest share position, slows, leading-edge logic, DRAM and AP grow at similar rates, and ICAPS turns from a drag into a modest tailwind.

The bear thesis is that this is a late-cycle setup and CY27 is the peak. WFE is on track to nearly double from ~$110B in 2025 to >$200B in 2027, memory capex is set to grow ~75–85% a year in 2026–27, and the industry, AMAT included, is adding capacity faster than ever. Bears argue three things. AI infrastructure spending is increasingly debt-financed and under ROI scrutiny. The gap between powered data-center shells and chip supply could force a pause. And DRAM, now AMAT’s biggest growth driver at a third of systems revenue, has historically had the most violent capex swings in semis.

AMAT-specific risks add to this. Its DRAM growth trails LRCX’s this year just as DRAM spending shifts to greenfield. Its foundry/logic business lags peers, its NAND share is structurally lower, and China is still 25% of revenue with both export-control and local-substitution risk. FY26 FCF grew only 9% against 21% revenue growth, and the cost base would hit margins hard if volumes fall.

Bears think the bull case relies too much on mgmt’s tracker and capacity plans. Tracker projects are not orders. CY26’s upside came from one-time cleanroom pull-ins and fab upgrades. And by mgmt’s own wording, >30% is a demand figure limited by cleanroom, components and raw materials, so actual CY27 growth could fall short of it. This quarter gave bears specific evidence: Foundry/logic fell 2% q/q and missed the Street by ~10% while peers grew logic 8–40%; The ICAPS recovery mgmt expected in 2026 is now a 2027 event; JanQ was already limited by component pinch points, with inventory days at a multi-year low; and WFE fell short of bogeys

Moonshot AI: Restructuring advances potential $5B Hong Kong IPO – WSJ

Moonshot is moving investors from its Cayman holding company into its Chinese operating entity ahead of a possible early-2027 Hong Kong listing. The IPO could raise up to $5B following a roughly $50B private valuation. Timing and size remain subject to market conditions.

Cyber / AI Policy: White House task force weighs incident-reporting standards – Reuters

The administration’s AI task force discussed national safety-incident reporting standards and ways to encourage critical-infrastructure operators to disclose AI-driven attacks without legal exposure. The discussion comes as the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act, which provides protections for defensive information sharing, approaches expiration next month.

MU / China Memory: CXMT broadens server DRAM and LPDDR6 offering – DigiTimes

CXMT detailed 32Gb DDR5 dies, server modules up to 192GB and mass-produced LPDDR6 already used in Xiaomi’s 18 Fold. It says its G5 process increases gross dies per wafer by at least 50% versus its predecessor, while it negotiates long-term supply agreements. Performance and manufacturing gains are company claims.

DELL / AMD: Dell adds next-generation EPYC servers – ETNews

Dell introduced PowerEdge R9825 and R9815 systems using AMD’s sixth-generation EPYC processors. The platforms support existing air-cooled facilities, optional direct liquid cooling and up to 512 cores per dual-socket server. Dell pitches consolidation and lower operating costs without wholesale data-center redesign.

TSM: New venture arm could invest in suppliers – Nikkei Asia

TSMC’s deputy co-COO told Nikkei its newly announced $500M venture fund could back suppliers and upstream partners as well as chip startups. The new detail is the potential expansion into supplier equity investments; the fund and separate OpenAI/Anthropic investments were announced earlier this week.
