SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion assessed a renewable-energy developer’s data center joint ventures, battery investments, wind expansion, and international project pipeline. It focused on what tenants and lenders require, project-delivery risks, and how policy changes could affect revenue and financing.

Key Insights

Tenants are likely to prioritize proof of ownership and secured power, followed by permits, construction arrangements, and delivery dates. They may seek to transfer grid delays, cost overruns, power-price exposure, and operating-performance risk to the developer. Operating solar assets were viewed as a major advantage over new builds because they reduce execution risk and are more financeable. The expert expects this advantage to remain even if regulation changes. Data center rent was identified as the hardest earnings-plan component to deliver on time. Tenant contracting and permitting were highlighted as early warning signs. Battery investment risks include uncertain payback, capital-cost overruns, and pressure on liquidity. Lenders may become more receptive as customer demand and contracted revenues become clearer. Wind additions face lengthy environmental and grid studies, as well as possible local opposition. Uruguay’s grid capacity was considered the most likely early bottleneck among the international projects discussed.

Competitive Landscape

Comparison

Market position and considerations

Hyperscalers vs. data center operators

Hyperscalers may have greater negotiating leverage, seek longer contracts and stronger guarantees, and push construction risk toward the joint venture. Operators may sign sooner because they are considered more agile; risk sharing may be likelier if they invest in the project.

Operating solar vs. new-build solar

Existing operating plants offer tenants lower delivery risk and stronger bankability. The expert considered this a significant advantage.

Solar-plus-battery vs. standalone battery

A solar revenue base and stronger debt-service coverage may allow lenders to accept a lower fixed share of battery income than for a merchant-only battery.
