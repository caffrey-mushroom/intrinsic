1. SpaceX and Tesla: Elon Musk on the cost of building 10GW of compute, xAI’s software rebuild, orbital compute and TerraFab’s 2028 target

Speaker: Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX and Tesla.

Summary: Musk said his team is adding roughly 500MW of terrestrial AI compute per month and expects to exceed 1GW per month by the end of 2027. He estimated that a 10GW build would cost about 60% of the figure he sees in broader industry estimates. He also described the engineering behind xAI’s software rebuild, how SpaceX would power and cool a computer in orbit, and the milestones he expects from TerraFab, Optimus and Cybercab.

1. The cost and pace of a 10GW build

Musk said some estimates for building 10GW are at least twice what he expects to spend. Against the broader industry estimate, he put his expected cost closer to 60%. He attributed much of the difference to doing the physical work in-house. In Memphis, the team breaks installation into tasks that new hires can learn in days and runs shifts around the clock. Musk said he now sees comparable productivity on the site at 2 a.m. and 10 a.m.

His estimate of 500MW added per month is measured at the power plant. It covers the GPUs, CPUs, networking and cooling, with room for turbine outages and peak cooling loads. Tesla Megapacks smooth the sharp changes in GPU power draw that can interrupt training runs and damage turbines. Musk expects monthly additions to exceed 1GW by the end of 2027.

2. Former Starlink and Starship software leaders are rebuilding xAI’s stack

Musk said xAI, now part of SpaceX, spent roughly six to eight months rewriting the software it uses for training, reinforcement learning and inference. The team wrote primarily in C, with some C++, to get more performance from the hardware and more control over how it operates.

At several hundred thousand GPUs, Musk said, training becomes a networking problem. Small losses in how efficiently chips exchange data matter across the cluster. Engineers who previously led software for Starlink and Starship are leading the work, drawing on their experience coordinating a large distributed network.

3. Grok 4.8 is in reinforcement learning

Musk said xAI is testing successive versions of Grok through Grokbot and judging them by whether they complete the user’s task. When a model fails, the team works backward to find missing examples, an imbalanced training mix or an incorrect answer in the training data.

He gave the example of a model repeatedly arriving at the correct answer while its reinforcement-learning system marks it wrong. The model starts searching for an incorrect response that will earn the reward. Musk said correcting those examples and acquiring data for unfamiliar tasks are major parts of the work on Grok 4.8.

4. TerraFab targets a first test chip around June 2027

Musk expects TerraFab’s research facility to produce its first test chip around June 2027. If development goes to plan, he is targeting phase-one volume production toward the end of 2028.

He expects to start with standard semiconductor equipment, then ask suppliers to modify tools as the team develops its process. TerraFab may build some equipment itself where Musk sees an opportunity to improve production. He put the facility’s ultimate footprint at approximately 100 million square feet.

5. Orbital compute depends on Starship, solar manufacturing and heat rejection

Musk described the recovery of Starship’s upper stage as a milestone toward flying both stages repeatedly. He expects full, rapid reuse to reduce the cost of reaching orbit by roughly 100 times. For orbital compute, SpaceX is targeting manufacturing capacity of 200GW per year of solar photovoltaics for use in space.

He described the planned Starmind satellite as a computer connected to solar panels, a double-sided radiator and laser links to Starlink. His design estimates were approximately 250 watts of solar generation per square meter of panel and 1,200 watts of heat rejection per square meter of radiator. That implies roughly five times as much panel area as radiator area.

Musk said the computer would occupy about one-quarter the physical volume of a Vera Rubin NVL72 rack while offering comparable computing capability. Starlink would relay its communications to the ground.

6. Optimus economics favor round-the-clock industrial work

Musk identified the hands and forearms as the hardest parts of Optimus to engineer. One test he likes is whether the robot can open a toy car, unscrew the battery cover, insert the batteries, replace the cover and operate the car. That sequence requires dexterity and the ability to adapt when an object is in an unfamiliar position.

He expects Optimus to be capable of household tasks such as washing and putting away dishes in 12–18 months. He sees the strongest initial economics in mining, refining and other industrial settings where the robot could work around the clock.

7. Cybercab expansion, Neuralink and Jensen Huang

Musk said Tesla plans to expand Cybercab from Austin into other major Texas cities and then Florida. He thought California approval could come in early 2027. With no steering wheel or pedals, he said Tesla is pacing the rollout around safety performance and regulatory approvals.

At Neuralink, Musk said more than 30 people had received implants and that some participants were using robotic arms to feed themselves. He described Blindsight as a project to send signals from a camera directly to the visual cortex. Looking further ahead, he wants Neuralink to bridge damaged neural pathways and improve communication between people and AI. On AI safety, he emphasized training models to seek accurate answers and to care about human welfare.

Asked about Jensen Huang, Musk called his departure a major blow to NVIDIA. He compared losing Huang to the Chicago Bulls replacing Michael Jordan in the middle of a winning streak.

2. Intel: 14A timing, 70% yields on 18A, packaging wins and the CPU recovery

Speaker: Lip-Bu Tan, CEO, Intel.

Summary: Tan said Intel had signed its first external 18A customer and improved yields to 70%. He laid out separate milestones for Intel’s own 14A products and external customers, described new EMIB packaging business, and explained why Intel struggled to redirect capacity from weaker PCs toward stronger server demand.

1. The 14A schedule differs for internal products and external customers

Tan is targeting the first internal 14A product tapeout in 2027 and volume production in 2028. He put external-customer volume production in 2028–29. Asked whether Intel’s own products could reach process parity with TSMC in late 2027 or 2028, he agreed. He described the external-customer timeline as roughly comparable with TSMC’s or potentially slightly behind.

Tan said customers need to see the required performance, SRAM yields and defect density, along with a complete portfolio of IP. Intel released its 0.9 process design kit in October and is preparing the next update for the first quarter. Low-power IP is among the requirements for serving mobile customers.

2. 18A is in volume production, with a first external customer signed

Tan said 18A yields had reached 70% after a poor start when he became CEO. Intel is using the process for its own products and has signed its first outside customer. He described foundry as a service business in which customers depend on Intel’s ability to manufacture on schedule because their own revenue is at stake.

3. Microsoft and another customer are using Intel packaging

Tan described advanced packaging as an industry bottleneck. He pointed to a newly announced Microsoft data-center product using Intel’s EMIB packaging.

Gavin Baker said a company in their shared investment portfolios had selected Intel’s EMIB-T over the TSMC alternative. Tan said its CEO had visited him two days earlier and described the order as substantial. He said hyperscalers want Intel to respond quickly to design requirements and deliver packaging on their schedules.

4. PC weakness exposed limited flexibility between client and server

Tan said customers began flagging excess consumer-PC inventory in September. Memory shortages and sharply higher memory prices were weighing on phones and lower-end PCs. Intel tried to shift toward data-center servers but ran into supply constraints on Intel 3.

He said Intel’s PC and server chips use different architectures, which limits its ability to move capacity between the two markets as quickly as AMD. He also called out memory and substrate requirements. Following a review of the quarter, he increased the frequency of leadership meetings and pushed for closer coordination between product and manufacturing teams.

5. A 141-person CPU team and a return to tick-tock development

Tan called CPU leadership his top product priority. He highlighted Diamond Rapids, Coral Rapids and simultaneous multithreading as parts of the plan to close the performance gap with AMD and eventually move ahead.

He has also assembled a separate CPU team that reached 141 people in two months. He wants a faster development rhythm alongside the existing organization and a return to Intel’s tick-tock cadence.

6. Tan sees a $200B CPU opportunity over five years

Tan said frontier-model customers are using CPUs for orchestration, reinforcement learning and agentic workloads. He sees the CPU market potentially increasing from roughly $40B to $200B over the next five years.

He said the softness he was seeing was concentrated in client PCs. Data-center, cloud and frontier-model customers wanted more than Intel could supply. He expects AI capex and data-center semiconductor spending to be higher in 2028 than in 2027, although financing could change which companies and projects account for the spending.

7. Memory, substrates and power can slow deployment

Tan expects memory and substrate constraints to take another two to three years to work through. He also identified agent security and the possibility of a slower power build-out after the midterms as risks.

Inside Intel, he is encouraging teams to use Claude, Perplexity and Gemini across functions. He also pointed to healthcare as an area where AI could improve productivity, access and affordability.

3. AI Credit: Chip financing, neocloud borrowing costs and the risk of excess compute

Speakers: Scott Goodwin, Co-founder & Managing Partner, Diameter Capital Partners, alongside fellow AI credit panelists.

Summary: The panel explained why a neocloud can face a 15% unsecured borrowing cost while secured loans against the underlying GPUs clear at much lower rates. Goodwin favored short, amortizing chip loans backed by large companies and described his cautious positioning toward operators that need both expensive financing and a sustained compute shortage.

1. What the 15% unsecured yield actually finances

One panelist compared a GPU loan to a mortgage covering approximately 80% of a property’s purchase cost. The lender can underwrite the customer contracted to buy the compute, and the GPU loan can repay over roughly three years. The operator still has to fund the equivalent of the down payment.

Operators can use their own cash, borrow unsecured or get customers to prepay. The panel put the cost of unsecured borrowing at roughly 15%, up from about 7% a year earlier. Some customers are paying one or two years in advance to secure capacity, giving operators another way to fund their share.

2. The borrowing-cost ladder runs from 7.5–8% to 15% plus warrants

For comparable chip collateral, Goodwin cited roughly 7.5–8% on short, amortizing loans backed by NVIDIA or Broadcom, about 8.5% for SpaceX, and approximately 12% for stronger neocloud borrowers. Smaller inference providers and model developers can face 15% or more, sometimes with warrants and cash collateral.

Goodwin cited an estimate that a neocloud could need 30–40% more revenue per gigawatt than a hyperscaler using the same chips because of the difference in funding costs. He added that customer prepayments and guarantees come at an economic cost to the operator. Panelists said current token margins leave some room to absorb those costs, with future economics dependent on token pricing and output per watt.

3. Goodwin expects more acquisitions among capital-constrained companies

Goodwin said larger companies are securing both capital and compute. He expects some smaller businesses with valuable technology to sell because they cannot afford enough chips to scale independently. For a late-stage investor, he illustrated the difference with a $5B company that might sell for $10B instead of growing into a $50B standalone business.

Panelists said companies that use cash generated during a shortage to pay down debt will have more flexibility if supply catches up. They also expect public financial statements, observable equity valuations and credit ratings to broaden the lender base available to frontier labs.

4. A gigawatt of new chips can produce substantially more tokens

The panel said investors tracking new gigawatts also need to track which accelerators occupy them. A gigawatt of newer GPUs can generate substantially more tokens than a gigawatt of older hardware, with the improvement varying by workload.

Panelists estimated that roughly 70% of tokens are used internally and remain unmonetized, versus 30% served to paying customers. Labs can change that allocation, affecting how much token supply reaches the market even before another facility comes online.

5. What would change Goodwin’s positioning

Goodwin said nearly everyone he spoke with at the conference expected the compute shortage to persist. He read equity and credit prices as assigning a greater probability to excess supply. His fund had been positioned cautiously toward companies with high funding costs, particularly those borrowing long term to own chips and selling capacity into the spot market.

He is watching how much compute actually comes online and how quickly lab inference revenue grows. An application that accelerates revenue above its current trajectory could change his view. He cited agentic workers, healthcare and cybersecurity as possibilities and expects the industry to experience periods of both scarcity and oversupply.

6. Convertibles at 50–70 cents and chip loans at 300 basis points over cash

Goodwin highlighted some neocloud and powered-shell convertible bonds trading around 50–70 cents on the dollar. Where a company has several years of financing runway, he said an investor can sometimes buy close to estimated recovery value while retaining exposure to a rebound in the equity.

Panelists described different forms of downside protection, including energized and permitted land, a completed building, or a lease with an investment-grade customer. Goodwin also favored short chip loans backed by highly rated companies, citing approximately 300 basis points over cash for two-year risk. He stressed the need to limit portfolio concentration in a new asset class.

7. Operating facilities and unfinished projects carry different risks

The panel cited yields around 8% for some operating facilities with major counterparties, versus roughly 10–12% for projects with a customer contract but outstanding permitting, construction or power-delivery risk. Goodwin said he preferred simpler exposures where he could earn a comparable return.

One panelist described a West Virginia data-center project beside a former coal plant. He said it was bringing employment back to the area and was expected to increase the town’s tax revenue fivefold.

4. Blackstone: AI demand, infrastructure financing and adoption across the portfolio

Speaker: Jonathan Gray, President & COO, Blackstone.

Summary: Gray said spending on models had increased twentyfold across Blackstone’s companies. He described how Blackstone commits capital against 15- to 20-year data-center leases, why lender concentration limits may shift more funding toward hyperscaler cash flow and equity, and where portfolio companies are already generating revenue from AI.

1. Gray’s three tests for a bubble

Gray looks for excessive valuations, speculative supply and excessive leverage. He said large technology companies are valued more reasonably than at the dot-com peak. He contrasted the 1990s fiber build, when companies laid capacity in anticipation of future buyers, with customers seeking compute today while developers work through power, permitting, chip and financing constraints.

He said the biggest customers have substantial earnings and financing capacity. He expects some startups to fail and some developers to fall short of their commitments, but considers the broader market too pessimistic about demand.

2. Most data-center capital goes in after a lease is signed

Gray said data centers are Blackstone’s largest area of concentration. The firm commits most of its capital after securing 15- to 20-year leases with major technology companies. Its other investments include renewables, natural gas, pipelines, LNG, utilities, power plants and electrical equipment.

Blackstone also lends against infrastructure and chips. Gray said those deals can combine investment-grade counterparty credit with physical collateral and pay a premium over publicly traded debt. He expects fewer competitors to be able to execute the largest projects if financing gets harder.

3. Why lender concentration limits could require more equity

Gray said equity investors can accept large positions because they participate in the upside. Lenders receive interest and principal, giving them less reason to let one borrower or industry account for an outsized share of a portfolio.

He expects more AI infrastructure to be funded through hyperscaler cash flow and equity issuance as lenders reach concentration limits. He also expects borrowers to pay more for debt, particularly below investment grade. In his view, there is enough capital for the build-out, though financing costs and political constraints may slow its pace.

4. Where portfolio companies are seeing productivity gains

Gray cited a rental-housing company processing applications 90% faster, a European classifieds business handling customer-service questions 80% faster, and Ancestry processing family histories 30 times faster. He said companies are also producing a greater variety of ads at lower cost.

Adoption began in coding and spread into customer engagement. Legal and accounting workflows take longer to redesign because of privacy rules, regulatory requirements and the number of people involved in the process. Blackstone is working with Anthropic to help portfolio companies deploy the technology.

5. Chamberlain built a $50M revenue stream from garage access

Gray described an AI service from garage-door manufacturer Chamberlain that recognizes a delivery driver and opens the garage for a delivery. He said it went from zero to a $50M revenue run rate in less than two years. Chamberlain’s CEO sees the potential for $500M over the next several years.

Gray expects personal agents and agentic commerce to create other revenue opportunities. Inside Blackstone, investment teams are using AI to review earnings calls across a sector and investor letters across funds while the information is still timely.

6. Construction employment and agreements with local communities

Gray said U.S. employment across Blackstone’s portfolio companies was up 3%, with the strongest activity in physical industries including energy, data centers, defense and pharmaceuticals. He expects the number of people working at Blackstone’s U.S. data-center construction sites to reach roughly 70,000 by the end of 2027, up from approximately 10,000 a few years earlier.

He cited an Indiana project expected to return $1.75B to utility customers and an Iowa agreement freezing electricity prices for five years. Other projects have funded water infrastructure and skilled-trades training.

5. Atoms: Travis Kalanick on bringing Jensen Huang back to NVIDIA, automated kitchens and autonomous mining

Speaker: Travis Kalanick, Founder & CEO, Atoms, and Co-founder & former CEO, Uber.

Summary: Kalanick called for Jensen Huang’s reinstatement at NVIDIA and said he would join employees at company headquarters for a walkout in support. He also explained how Atoms grew out of CloudKitchens, why building autonomous food delivery brought him back to his former Uber autonomy team, and how that work expanded into mining.

1. Kalanick would join an employee walkout in support of Huang

Kalanick said he wants Huang back at NVIDIA and sees him as an advocate for privacy and individual control over AI. Drawing on his own departure from Uber, he argued that executives facing public pressure should have time to respond.

He said he sent Huang a message suggesting an employee walkout could have more impact than a petition. Kalanick said he would go to NVIDIA headquarters and join employees, founders and CEOs who wanted to participate.

2. A prepared, delivered meal for close to the cost of the groceries

Kalanick described food as a $10T global industry. His goal is to prepare a meal and deliver it to the customer at close to what that customer would spend buying the ingredients. To reach that price, he said, Atoms needs to automate both kitchen production and courier delivery and bring them together at the right properties.

He put the company’s footprint at roughly 500 properties globally. He estimated that 80–90% of meals are eaten at home and identified the time consumers spend cooking as a large part of the opportunity. At grocery-like prices, he believes delivery could reach occasions currently served by the household kitchen.

3. How autonomous couriers led to Pronto’s mining business

Working on autonomous couriers brought Kalanick back into contact with former members of Uber’s autonomy team. One had started an autonomous mining company. Atoms now operates its mining business under the Pronto brand.

Kalanick described visiting the Carajás iron ore mine in Brazil, where a fully loaded machine weighed roughly two million pounds and traveled around 35 mph. He said mine operators want to increase output and reduce operating costs, but the ability to remove workers from situations where an accident can be fatal is a particularly strong motivation. He sees mining and refining as foundational to an AI build-out that requires more equipment, materials and energy.

4. Why he wants manufacturing next to the product team

Kalanick said engineers designing a machine and engineers designing its production line need to work together daily while both designs are changing. He cited his manufacturing operations in San Francisco as an example of his preference for keeping that feedback loop short.

He also expects certain skilled trades to become more valuable with automation. If a small number of people have the expertise needed to bring a much larger operation online, he said, their work can determine the productivity of the entire site.

5. Housing costs influence where Atoms recruits

Kalanick identified rent as one of the largest financial pressures on engineers starting their careers. He credited Austin’s construction of new housing with bringing rents down and said affordability is one reason he is recruiting more young engineers into the company’s Austin office.

6. Valor: Managing fear and preserving better decisions under pressure

Speakers: Laura Harrison, PhD, Director of Neuroscience, Valor Equity Partners, and Antonio Gracias, Founder, CEO & CIO, Valor Equity Partners.

Summary: Harrison described how stress changes the range of outcomes a decision-maker can see. Gracias used Tesla’s decision to buy the Fremont factory when capital was scarce, and SpaceX’s later expansion into businesses outside its original plan, to illustrate the point.

1. Recognizing when stress starts to impair judgment

Harrison said performance can improve as stress rises, then decline once the threat response takes over. That response can begin before conscious reasoning and affect memory, planning and self-control.

She recommended learning to recognize the physical signs early, naming the feeling or taking a walk to create some distance, and returning to a calmer baseline before deciding.

2. Fear can narrow the decision tree

Harrison described a decision as a tree of possible outcomes. Under stress, people tend to concentrate on the branch containing the threat. They can miss a favorable surprise, an alternative course of action or an opportunity that only becomes apparent over a longer time horizon.

She said emotions carry information that belongs in the decision. Managing the response gives people enough room to weigh that information alongside the other possibilities.

3. Tesla’s Fremont purchase and SpaceX’s changing plans

Gracias recalled Tesla’s 2010 decision to buy the Fremont factory after the financial crisis, when the company had recently come close to running out of money. The purchase committed Tesla to substantial future capital spending. Without it, he said, Tesla might have remained a smaller assembler and technology provider to companies such as Daimler and Toyota.

He said SpaceX’s reusable rockets, Starlink and Starship likewise emerged as opportunities over time. What now looks like a linear progression required the company to recognize options that were outside its original plan.

7. Cerebras: Inference pricing, AMD integration and the next three chip generations

Speaker: Andrew Feldman, Co-founder & CEO, Cerebras.

Summary: Feldman said Cerebras is pricing new capacity at least 40–50% above its original OpenAI agreement. He described a production deployment that pairs AMD GPUs with Cerebras systems, a roadmap of three successive performance doublings, and the shortage of data-center space available to install the systems customers want.

1. Pricing for fast inference

Feldman said fast responses from frontier models command the highest gross margins because customers place the greatest value on them. He said Cerebras had signed AWS, Microsoft and Oracle over the preceding six months.

Since Cerebras signed its OpenAI agreement in December 2025, Feldman said models have grown larger and more capable, while power and space have become roughly 40% more expensive. He put pricing for new capacity at least 40–50% above the earlier agreement.

2. AMD GPUs process the prompt and Cerebras generates the answer

In Cerebras’s first production deployment of disaggregated inference, AMD GPUs handle prefill, the processing of the incoming prompt. Cerebras handles decode, the generation of the answer token by token. Feldman said the combination can produce approximately five times as many tokens from the same data center.

He said the approach also works with NVIDIA GPUs, AWS Trainium and other accelerators, including equipment in existing facilities. Asked how much of the installed accelerator base Cerebras could attach to if it had enough supply, he said most of the fleet used for inference.

3. Why Cerebras chose SRAM and wafer scale

Feldman traced the design to the decision to use SRAM instead of HBM. SRAM offers faster access but stores less data, which led Cerebras to build a much larger chip. He described its wafer-scale processor as 56 times larger than other chips and attributed its inference speed to the resulting memory bandwidth. Cerebras uses TSMC’s 5nm process and does not require HBM.

4. CS-4, CS-5 and CS-6

Feldman said CS-4 was shipping with twice the performance of CS-3. He expects CS-5 in summer 2027 with another doubling and CS-6 in summer 2028 with a further doubling.

5. The OpenAI contract set the manufacturing ramp in motion

Feldman said the OpenAI agreement was worth more than $20B. After signing it in late December 2025, Cerebras immediately began arranging manufacturing capacity in Taiwan. Chips came back in May and June. He said fourth-quarter revenue guidance implied roughly 40% sequential growth.

6. Most of Cerebras’s 2027 data centers will be outside the U.S.

Feldman identified space to deploy systems as the immediate constraint. He described 50–200MW sites as suitable for inference and expects approximately 75% of Cerebras’s 2027 data centers to be outside the U.S., including locations in Canada and Brazil.

He put construction time after permitting at 15–18 months. Given that lead time, he said a change in U.S. permitting conditions following the midterms would primarily affect projects scheduled for 2028.

8. Databricks: Genie’s role in running the company, enterprise AI adoption and the outlook for SaaS

Speaker: Ali Ghodsi, Co-founder & CEO, Databricks.

Summary: Ghodsi described using Genie to run a 12,000-person company, including an engineering-cost review that led Databricks to change how it routes model requests. He sees an opportunity for incumbent SaaS vendors to charge for agent access to proprietary data and expects five to ten years of work helping large enterprises redesign their processes.

1. How Ghodsi uses Genie to manage Databricks

Ghodsi said Genie has access to Databricks’ financials, organizational structure, employee responsibilities and permissions. It prepares his meeting materials, suggests questions and puts background agents to work investigating issues. He contrasted that with the earlier process of raising a question in a meeting, assigning follow-up work and reconvening a week later.

He is encouraging his executive team to work the same way. The prerequisite is a connected map of company knowledge, or ontology, that lets an agent understand who owns a decision, where the relevant data sits and who has permission to use it.

2. A question about engineering costs led to smart routing

Ghodsi asked Genie what Databricks could do about rising engineering costs. It traced token spending across teams and suggested applying the company’s smart router to selected workloads. He said the router can save up to 60% of token costs for some engineering groups using it.

He encouraged executives to give agents the business problem itself. In this example, asking where to cut costs allowed the system to identify an opportunity that a narrower request might have missed. Ghodsi then reviewed the options and helped direct implementation.

3. Digital-native customers are pulling ahead

Ghodsi estimated that digital natives account for about 20% of Databricks’ customers. They typically have fewer legacy systems, subsidiaries and accumulated data silos. In Databricks’ own usage data, he sees them processing more data, running more agents and consuming more tokens.

Across larger enterprises, he sees two uses already operating at scale: chatbots that retrieve and summarize information, and coding. He said engineering teams at those companies often report spending only 20–30% of their time writing code. Faster code generation addresses that portion of the job while spending on AI tools is rising. Extending adoption across the company requires work on permissions, data retention and access to business context.

4. Early uses in finance and healthcare

In financial services, Ghodsi cited regulatory reporting, faster CCAR calculations, fraud detection and wealth-management tools that match advisers with clients or suggest the next action. He sees younger financial companies moving particularly quickly.

In pharmaceuticals, he discussed agents supporting drug research and clinical-trial preparation, including work involving Novo Nordisk. He also described a Databricks model that predicts changes in gene regulatory networks. He expects these tools to shorten parts of the research process while clinical trials still have to run.

5. Different economics for routine work and frontier research

Ghodsi expects enterprises to use open models, fine-tuning and smart routing for repetitive, cost-sensitive tasks. At the other end, a researcher might willingly let the most capable model work for two or three days if it could produce a meaningful discovery.

In both cases, an enterprise agent needs proprietary data to do useful work. Once a company gives an agent access, IT has to control what the agent can see and do. Ghodsi expects demand for data repositories, governance and security to grow together.

6. Agent usage could accelerate revenue for some SaaS vendors

Ghodsi said large companies run core business processes inside incumbent SaaS products. Replacing an application can require redesigning the process itself, and the application often contains proprietary data that agents need.

He expects some vendors to charge on a consumption basis for access to that data. Agents can query information more frequently than human employees, giving vendors a potential source of accelerating revenue. He said vendors that raise prices without improving the product are more vulnerable to replacement.

7. Process redesign could sustain five to ten years of services work

Ghodsi expects people to use a small number of AI interfaces. Underneath them, agents still have to discover metadata, find the right information and coordinate work across systems. That is where he positions Databricks.

He cited Palantir, Cognition and Factory as companies already helping customers change how work gets done. He also sees an opportunity for Accenture, Deloitte and Capgemini if they adapt. Ghodsi expects five to ten years of implementation work because changing employee behavior and business processes takes considerably longer than demonstrating the technology.

8. His test for recursive self-improvement

Ghodsi said a model writing nearly all its own code does not establish that it can improve itself indefinitely. His test requires successive generations to become substantially smarter while taking less time and fewer resources to train, with that pattern repeating. He pointed out that frontier developers are currently building larger clusters.

He expects the broader productivity gains from agents to arrive later than current marketing suggests. Over time, he sees employees managing several agents while humans retain responsibility for decisions and outcomes.
