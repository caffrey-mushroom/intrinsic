Questions

00:00:20] Today I would like to learn about the latest developments in the wafer foundry industry. First of all, what progress have TSMC, Intel and Samsung made on their latest process roadmaps? Among the three, which nodes are in the same tier and what is the current progress of each?

Expert [00:00:35]: Overall, there is no doubt that TSMC is still the most advanced. TSMC’s current node is collectively referred to as the 2nm GAA platform, which later had a number of extended nodes, namely A16 and A14 and then A13 and A12. In essence, you can roughly view it as the same process, except that it produces continuous iterations when parameters are fine-tuned.

A16 is an optimised version of N2, while A14 is a version optimised for specific application scenarios. A16 leans towards high-performance computing, while A14 currently leans towards mobile communications and high-end consumer products. However, in essence, the overall feature sizes of these two process technologies are similar but they have different emphases. For example, A16 is more focused on computational capacity, including transistor switching speed. It may not pursue power consumption optimisation to the extreme but it has relatively high performance and reliability requirements.

00:02:44] A16 is a small node under the N2 large node. It introduces backside power delivery, while N2 itself does not have backside power delivery. In TSMC's roadmap, A14 should be positioned as a complete large node and A13 is its corresponding version with backside power delivery, right? But you mentioned that both A16 and A14 are counted as part of the N2 series. Was my previous understanding incorrect?

Expert [00:02:44]: In essence, the so-called full node does not mean that there is a significant structural difference between them. At present, in TSMC's GAA process, for example, TSMC's 7nm and 5nm are each a full node, with a 6nm and a 4nm implementation respectively. However, in essence, TSMC's N7 and N5 are relatively limited in their differences in terms of the technical parameters and process parameters of semiconductor manufacturing. The only difference is the feature size. You can think of it as a difference in process precision. In essence, they are the same type of process. Therefore, those who work on IP have a very intuitive sense of this. A 7nm process can be easily migrated to a 5nm process, without presenting significant challenges. However, from 5nm to 2nm or from 3nm to 2nm, the difference is relatively large, because the transistor configuration has completely changed. It is a GAA configuration.

Therefore, once the industry has moved to GAA, there is no particularly obvious difference at present. That is to say, 2nm and A16 are highly similar and A16 is a rebranded version of 2nm. A14 is definitely a full node, because any full node has a very typical feature. This node is used for mobile phone APs. Generally speaking, it is a main node.

00:05:23] Backside power delivery is not very useful for AP chips but it is more helpful for HPC chips with complex signal routing. Is that right?

Expert [00:05:23]: Yes, especially SRAM. HPC chips have very large SRAM and SRAM takes up a lot of space. If backside power delivery can be realised, it will be of great benefit to placement and routing. This is very important. However, it is true that mobile phones are not without benefit but the benefit is less obvious. That is, the voltage drop can be better controlled. However, for mobile phones, this is not the main purpose. The main purpose is more about overall low-power design and low-cost design. This is a consideration.

00:10:01] It should be a foregone conclusion that more 2nm production capacity will be built than 3nm, right? I mean the entire 2nm series, including A16. The vast majority of ASICs will basically adopt N2.

Expert [00:10:01]: From the perspective of HPC for AI computing, this is indeed the case. However, there is one very important point. If you look at the overall global semiconductor landscape, the overall shipment volume of 2nm-based chips, such as GPUs, CPUs or ASICs, remains relatively small. The products with truly large shipment volumes are in the mobile communications sector and the broader consumer sector, particularly mid-to-high-end consumer products. In fact, from the perspective of the consumer sector, including automotive and mobile communications, these three markets, which we call MAC, representing mobile, automotive and consumer, are where chips are mass-produced on a very large scale and are very cost-sensitive. In essence, HPC has a high unit value but its overall shipment volume is certainly far smaller than that of mobile phone chips. Therefore, 3nm will be a very long-lived major node in the future. In other words, HPC chips may gradually migrate away from 3nm but many consumer chips, namely MAC-class chips, will remain at 3nm for a long time.

00:11:43] On the mobile side, flagship chips from Apple, MediaTek and Qualcomm are all expected to adopt 2nm and Apple's 2nm products should already be shipping.

Expert [00:11:43]: Yes but here is the thing. Mobile phone chips are also divided into high-end, mid-range and low-end categories. From Apple's perspective, Apple is a special case, as it is the absolute leader in the global mobile phone market and it has always been a leading customer for TSMC's advanced process technology. MediaTek was boosted by it, while Qualcomm has its own pace and considerations. Even in the mobile phone market and the future mobile terminal market, 3nm will still be a very long-term target node.

At present, sales of high-end mobile phone models are gradually shrinking. The decline in mid-range or mid-to-high-end models is not as significant. Many people now also consider cost when replacing their phones. Of course, a few high-end mobile phone models will definitely move towards nodes below 2nm. That is certain. However, this does not represent the vast majority of market coverage, because in addition to mobile phone chips, there are countless consumer product categories and automotive-grade products to be developed and these are now migrating towards 4nm and 3nm nodes.

00:13:22] From the perspective of wafer demand, will the growth of AI-related demand during node migration fail to offset the decline in wafer volume in traditional fields such as mobile terminals?

Expert [00:13:22]: Yes. From the second half of this year to the first half of next year, although TSMC's AI chip production capacity looks relatively tight, everyone is now placing wafer capacity orders based on end-customer orders, on a one-to-one basis, so it is not actually that tight. The volatility of these AI chips is now relatively high. Of course, their wafer consumption is also relatively high because their yield is lower. Overall, however, wafer consumption for mobile phones and consumer electronics is still relatively large. There are also some CPU-type chips, in what we call personal computing applications and the consumption of these chips is also relatively large.

00:19:16] A14 itself is mainly positioned for Mac and other customer groups. In this family, A13 or A12 may be the ones truly targeting HPC. Currently, few people are using A14. Could it be because the mass production timelines for A13 and A12 are still some way off? If we push the timeline forward by another 1-2 years, will the production capacity of the entire A14 node still be greater than that of 2nm?

Expert [00:19:16]: I think this judgement of yours is linear. We believe that in the overall evolution of computing power, only absolute demand will give rise to this situation. However, there is now a problem. After AI chips are manufactured on 2nm and A16, both 2nm and A16 are relatively mainstream HPC processes. Of course, there will be others. However, 2nm is still the main process for now. The dividend of 2nm has not been fully realised yet and new 2nm products, whether GPUs or ASICs, have not shipped on a large scale. In this case, in the next-generation evolution, further down the line, A13, as you said, is even further off. It is indeed quite far away. However, the premise is that, by then, there must be genuine demand for these technologies, including algorithms, models and software application scenarios, before they can evolve relatively quickly. This is because there have been cases in history where stagnation in software and compute demand has made such iterative process upgrades unnecessary or has slowed them down.

00:28:16] At this point, how does the process technology of TSMC compare with that of Intel and that of Samsung?

Expert [00:28:16]: At present, in terms of 3D stacking, Intel is about 1 year ahead of TSMC overall and its process capability is stronger. Intel has long had to rely on the back-end process to compensate for the front-end process, that is, to first assemble more dies in 2D and then stack them in 3D. It has been on this path for 7 to 8 years, so its technological accumulation and practical experience indeed started early. Therefore, its current position is somewhat better than that of TSMC but the problem is that its front-end process bottlenecks offset this advantage. However, if it moves towards foundry services next year, it will monetise these technologies. This is a variable and a factor that can create incremental growth and this still needs further observation.

00:29:29] For 2.5D packaging forms such as CoWoS, TSMC can separate the front-end and back-end processes. For example, MediaTek can use TSMC for the front end and Intel's EMIB for the back end. However, at the 3D packaging stage, it seems that the front-end and back-end processes can no longer be separated. Is this a choice made by TSMC for commercial competition or is it that, from a technical perspective, the front-end and back-end of this process simply cannot be separated?

Expert [00:29:29]: Both but mainly the latter. This is because CoWoS-like packaging technologies, namely TSMC’s CoWoS and Intel’s EMIB-M or EMIB-T, are all in the direction of two-dimensional expansion. That is to say, as long as your die is made well, the external interface, that is, how dies are packaged together, can indeed be decoupled in essence. Of course, it is not completely decoupled. For example, if CoWoS made at TSMC is to be used for packaging with Intel's EMIB-T, its design specifications still need to be fine-tuned and several photomask layers need to be updated, especially in the top metal, where some changes may be required but these changes are relatively limited.

However, 3D stacking mainly relies on hybrid bonding. Of course, earlier 3D stacking mainly used TSV technology. With TSV technology, the vias are large in size and low in density, so comparatively speaking, the alignment requirements are not particularly high. However, compared with TSV, the vias in hybrid bonding are of a completely different scale: it is like comparing a manhole cover with a pen barrel. The TSV is as large as a manhole cover, while the I/O size of hybrid bonding may only be as thick as a pen barrel. The difference is enormous and the high alignment requirements basically lock the front-end and back-end processes together. That is to say, your alignment is highly correlated with the front-end design. Your I/O arrangement, metal rules and some bonding requirements related to reliability must fully take into account the needs and capabilities of hybrid bonding in the back-end process.

So this means that the I/O structure and power supply structure of your chip layout must be designed in the front-end process to match the back-end process capability of hybrid bonding. Therefore, the front-end and back-end are completely locked together at this point. Migration is extremely unlikely to occur. It is very difficult, especially for larger and more complex stacking, to avoid front-end and back-end integration.

00:33:15] In the field of silicon photonics, is TSMC's COUPE technology currently basically the only mature process option in the entire industry? Have both Intel and Samsung not yet launched corresponding process platforms?

Expert [00:33:15]: No. Intel's platform called OCI is also silicon photonics, where EIC and PIC are vertically bonded, similar to high-density co-packaging such as CPO. Intel does have this technology and it developed it earlier than TSMC did.

Client [00:33:53]: But it seems that hardly anyone uses it, right?

Expert [00:33:53]: Do you know why? This is because Intel's foundry business currently lacks clients. So in this regard, Intel has always had a leading capability but it simply has no clients. This is because these clients' front-end processes are all done at TSMC, not Intel. It was only after Lip-Bu Tan joined, starting from the first half of this year, that Intel's back-end design service capability was initially unlocked.

Even by 2025, after Lip-Bu Tan joined, Intel's back-end process had still not developed design service capability, so it held onto this capability without commercialising it but that does not mean its capability was backward. Intel's capabilities are far beyond this, especially in technologies such as on-chip silicon photonics. It achieved breakthroughs in the laboratory many years ago but it has never had good practical engineering application scenarios. So do not underestimate Intel in silicon photonics. Once it has clients, it can industrialise its production capacity very quickly. Foveros 3D and 3.5D technologies are relatively mature, much more mature than TSMC's. Their disadvantage lies in having no clients.

Client [00:35:28]: Do you think the binding between the front-end and back-end in COUPE silicon photonics is strong?

Expert [00:35:28]: This is quite powerful, because COUPE technology is essentially a partial realisation of hybrid bonding technology. EIC and PIC are vertically stacked. Theoretically, vertical bonding of PIC is feasible but essentially hybrid bonding will be required in subsequent steps. Especially in the medium and long term, vertical bonding is also required between the EIC and the main computing chip. That is to say, there are many ways to implement CPO. Even under the co-packaged structure, there are differences in implementation, with some leaning towards 2D and others towards 3D. Therefore, this technology is essentially closely related to front-end processes. Especially in terms of design specifications, because the electrical link between the silicon photonics module and the main computing chip also needs to be jointly designed, it is unlikely that you will completely abandon the back-end process or design in a completely neutral manner. That is impossible. Since these traces run on the substrate in between, their physical model and potential physical effects all need to be co-simulated with the structure between the main computing chip and the integrated optical module. So it is basically an integrated design, which cannot really be split apart.

00:46:01] Samsung seems to have signed a major deal with Broadcom. It is said that the deal involves not only memory but also some wafer foundry cooperation, right?

Expert [00:46:01]: The agreement they signed is a framework agreement for process and foundry cooperation, not a mass production agreement. In other words, Samsung has signed something with Broadcom under which Broadcom will help Samsung promote this process node. However, in reality, there are not yet that many customers using it. It just means that a cooperation mechanism has been established, that's all. For example, in China, there are also IP companies and design service companies that have entered into foundry cooperation agreements with Intel. It is just a matter of signing something and every agreement counts. After all, Broadcom is not a small company. However, Broadcom's focus is not on consumer-grade or automotive-grade segments at all. It is all on high-performance computing.

00:47:39] What is the specific progress of Intel's foundry business at present?

Expert [00:47:39]: Intel's overall process matrix actually has quite a long history, from Intel 7, Intel 5, Intel 4 and Intel 3 to 18A and 14A and now to 18A-P and 14A-E. Basically, so far, the truly advanced and dynamic process nodes are Intel 3, Intel 7, 18A-P and 14A-E. These four nodes are currently relatively competitive. Intel 7 is basically equivalent to TSMC’s 12nm-class process, which is relatively dated. Intel 3 is roughly equivalent to TSMC’s 5nm to 4nm process level. Its transistor density is not particularly high, so it is not very good for computing. However, it is still suitable for I/O, including I/O interfaces, high-speed SerDes and memory interfaces, because of its low cost. Because Intel uses a building-block approach, its computing chips use a good process, while its memory chips, namely the cache chips, use an older process, even 7nm. Of course, some are also made with Intel 4 but low-cost processes are mainly used.

After a major optimisation, the 18A process has become 18A-P, which is basically comparable to TSMC’s 3nm-class process. However, it features backside power delivery and RibbonFET transistors. In other words, apart from its transistor density, which does not reach TSMC's 2nm level, it is basically close to TSMC's 2nm level in all other peripheral capabilities. Therefore, it is a technology between the 3nm and 2nm levels. It is better than TSMC's 3nm node, so it can be used to make fairly good high-performance computing chips.

00:50:18] The Intel 18A node has reasonable performance but its density is somewhat lower. It may not be adopted for mobile chips, which are highly sensitive to density but it may be feasible for server chips, right?

Expert [00:50:18]: Yes. Intel's main original intention in developing this process was not to make consumer-grade products and Intel does not do much in this field. After this year's evolution, small and medium-sized silicon dies have now basically reached a relatively high level. First, small die sizes below 120 square millimetres are already relatively mature. Medium-sized silicon dies, i.e. those in the range of 200 to 400 square millimetres, are now usable too. At least the threshold for profitable production has been touched or has already been crossed. However, for large silicon wafers such as those in the 600 to 800 range, the reticle size is still somewhat constrained and gradually scaling up. From the foundry perspective, small and medium types can now be made. Some have basically completed risk production and many people are starting to use its front-end process to make things but they all lean towards high-performance computing. Therefore, it is still constrained by silicon wafer size and the pace of introduction is currently slower. However, overall, the 18A-P process has undergone significant evolution. The previous 18A relied on High-NA EUV but now 18A-P has changed that and does not require that type of EUV. It can be done using EUV with a standard numerical aperture. The advantage is that production capacity increases. Since there is only one High-NA machine, it would be a bottleneck.

Client [01:00:38]: TSMC recently announced that it would invest another USD 100bn and also said it would build new wafer fab clusters in Texas and elsewhere. Is this true?

Expert [01:00:38]: Yes, that is right. Obviously, it sees that Intel's yields are maturing and feels it needs to make a statement.

01:01:53] In the face of market share loss and competitive risks, what countermeasures is TSMC currently taking?

Expert [01:01:53]: TSMC is responding to all changes by staying unchanged. It believes that its process technology is still absolutely leading because it has plans for A14 and sub-2nm nodes. By 2028, nodes A14, A13 and A12 will have more customers and become mainstream nodes. It is not a full generation ahead of Intel but it is at least half a generation ahead. It hopes to maximise its technological lead and capacity advantages. That is to say, as long as its process maturity is relatively high and its capacity is sufficient, from a business perspective, all customers will still tape out with it. That's exactly right. However, it has overlooked the fact that its back-end process is lagging. It is possible that after 2027-28, the picture will be that its front-end process is still fully booked but the back-end process can only do some mid-range CoWoS, while all high-end CoWoS will go to Intel.

Client [01:03:06]: However, this also has little impact on its revenue, because the back-end itself accounts for a relatively small proportion, right?

Expert [01:03:06]: That being said, CoWoS is not cheap either and it has a certain degree of customer stickiness. If the front-end and back-end can be decoupled, in many cases others may no longer regard you as very important in the future or your voice and bargaining power will be weakened. You see, TSMC's front-end and back-end are integrated. That is why it holds firm on price with many major customers and they have to accept price increases every year. Then, if the front-end and back-end can be decoupled in the future and there is competition, can you still raise prices? This is a risk. However, Intel's yield still needs to be improved, which means the back-end process also needs to advance further.
