Four years have passed since ChatGPT launched and set the AI narrative in motion. Now, a week before that anniversary, the narrative is approaching its biggest moment yet: Anthropic, the fastest company in history to reach $50 billion in revenue, is preparing to go public.

Then Claude Code came to the rescue:

  • The standard for being AI-native shifted from AI writing 50% of the code to writing all of it.
  • Engineers went from running one agent to five, then dozens.
  • A typical engineer’s agents went from running for tens of minutes to several hours, even replicating features and software they found online.

The effects compounded, quickly pushing Anthropic’s monthly growth to 50%.

This changed how investors thought about AI ARPU. Monthly spending per person did not have to stop at the price of a ChatGPT subscription.

It also changed how they viewed data center constraints. Revenue generated per gigawatt of capacity could rise from $10 billion to $20 billion, or even $50 billion.

Instead of extrapolating AI annual recurring revenue (ARR) in a straight line, the market began estimating it from future compute capacity. Assuming revenue per gigawatt stayed constant, the split between training and inference capacity would be enough to derive an ARR trajectory.

We regard Anthropic as one of the great companies of this century. It has changed our understanding of AI, accelerated AI infrastructure development, and helped give rise to a cohort of data-labeling companies. It also takes AI’s consequences for humanity seriously, devoting substantial time to helping society understand AI safety even when doing so could hurt its own revenue. We believe this is the right approach and may ultimately help avert an AI crisis.

The picture has become more complicated as competition between OpenAI and Anthropic intensifies and their combined ARR growth slows. Anthropic’s S-1 also showed that inference’s share of total costs began falling sharply from 26Q2, with training reaching 75% of total costs that quarter. That makes it harder to infer ARR from data center capacity, or to assess the ROI of the AI narrative as a whole.

We have returned to this issue repeatedly: the closer we get to AGI, the larger training’s share of costs becomes, and the harder it is to measure ROI. It is like the final sprint of a 100-meter race.

Ahead of Anthropic’s IPO, we spent considerable time on field research to better understand the AI ROI question. We set out to answer the questions below. Awkwardly, given the timing, we still do not have complete answers; further company disclosures may be needed to resolve them.

  • If we divide Anthropic’s customers into startups, technology companies, and traditional enterprises, how is demand developing in each group, and where does it go from here?
  • How do KV caching and model routing affect ARR?
  • How does today’s token budgeting differ from the SaaS optimization cycle of 2022-2023?
  • How does competition from OpenAI and open-weight models affect ARR?
  • Which use cases create meaningful switching costs?
  • Beyond today’s applications, what new use cases could contribute to ARR in 2027?
  • How might recent developments at NVIDIA affect Anthropic, and which findings from our research might they challenge?

How Far Customer AI Spending Can Grow

We divide business customers into three groups: traditional enterprises in non-technology sectors such as telecom, finance, insurance, pharmaceuticals, and advertising; technology companies; and startups. Technology companies and startups have adopted AI fastest. Traditional enterprises have moved more slowly, but we believe they offer the largest future growth opportunity, so they receive the most attention in this section.

Traditional Enterprises

The 58 traditional enterprises we track generally expect AI spending growth to slow in 2027. A closer look at their spending mix and project pipelines suggests that growth is shifting from paid seats to more complex applications, rather than demand reaching a ceiling.

  1. On average, respondents expect AI spending to rise about 31% year over year in 2027. Roughly 60% expect slower

growth next year than this year, and a small number expect spending to be flat or decline.

  1. Respondents most often cited tighter budgets, engineering-led cost savings, and a higher comparison base as reasons

for the slowdown. Budget controls and engineering improvements explain why a given volume of usage costs less; a higher base affects the reported growth rate. We believe the more fundamental issue is that new applications capable of materially increasing usage are not coming online quickly enough.

  1. Seat Expansion Drove Early Spending Growth but Is Approaching Its Limit

Early in the adoption cycle, companies could quickly raise AI spending by rolling out paid seats to employees. Seats still account for about 55% of AI spending on average in our sample, but roughly 67% of employees are already covered, leaving limited room for further expansion. Growth must increasingly come from API usage in actual business workflows. About 60% of respondents expect API usage to be the main source of future spending growth. That requires new applications that consume more tokens.

Enterprise survey: main drivers of future AI spending growth

Source: FUNDA

  1. More Complex Applications Can Drive API Growth but Take Longer to Deploy

Basic API applications, including knowledge search and Q&A, document summaries, and coding assistance, are becoming widespread. Further growth increasingly depends on customer-facing applications and more complex, multistep agentic workflows. These take much longer to deploy.

The difficulties arise at both ends of the process: identifying the right problem and putting the solution into production. Companies must select valuable use cases from a long list of possibilities and define the requirements clearly. Many traditional enterprises lack the talent and experience to do this themselves and turn to external forward-deployed engineers (FDEs). Deployment then requires substantial organizational and process changes. Cross-functional workflows need redesigning, gaps in legacy data need filling, and permissions, compliance, and identity integration must be worked through. Sequential security and governance approvals are difficult to compress, while expansion across departments and regions requires extensive coordination.

Complex AI applications therefore typically take 6-12 months from kickoff to launch, with large- scale deployment taking longer at major enterprises. Our earlier research found that even products such as M365 Copilot take months to roll out: roughly 2-3 months to launch and integrate Copilot, and 3-6 months for ChatGPT or Claude enterprise editions. Custom API projects typically take 3-12 months from approval to launch, followed by a further ramp-up in usage.

  1. Project Pipelines Point to Launches in 27H1 and a Substantial Rise in Usage from 27H2

Most new applications discussed in our interviews are moving from pilot to production or are still in development and deployment. Those already live are still scaling up. Some are being built internally, while others involve external FDEs. Based on their current stages and respondents’ expected timelines, we expect a substantial number of applications to launch and begin rolling out in 27H1, with a meaningful increase in usage from 27H2.

  1. FDE Delivery Capacity Should Also Reach Scale Around Mid-2027

Many traditional enterprises lack the people needed to identify and deploy complex applications on their own. A larger FDE workforce could bring AI to these companies by helping them define requirements and deliver projects, supporting much broader adoption across industries. The pace at which FDE capacity grows is therefore an important determinant of both the breadth and timing of the usage ramp.

Model providers and consulting firms were the first to expand their FDE businesses this year. Examples include Anthropic’s $1.5 billion joint venture with Blackstone and other partners, OpenAI’s creation of a Deployment Company alongside its existing FDE team, and a joint FDE program from Accenture and ServiceNow. Cloud providers followed from June: AWS committed $1 billion and Microsoft $2.5 billion to establish FDE operations. Large-scale hiring and training began in September and October. Google Cloud and Accenture plan a 1,000- person FDE team, while Anthropic committed $100 million to train 10,000 FDEs by the end of 2027.

FDEs need to understand business problems, choose an implementation approach, and help customers coordinate internally. That limits how quickly they can be trained. At the current pace of expansion, we expect FDE delivery capacity to reach scale around mid-2027, followed by a substantial increase in deployments and usage from 27H2.

An FDE specialist at a global IT consultancy:

“Development alone generally takes 2-4 weeks for applications of low to moderate complexity, or roughly 8-12 weeks for highly complex ones. But the full project takes much longer. You first have to identify and define the requirements, then go through many reviews and meetings before production. Large enterprises still move slowly. Each industry needs a few leading companies to demonstrate the value before others follow. There is momentum, but it is still early. Realistically, large-scale launches are probably something we will see in the second half of next year.”

  1. Follow-Up Interviews Show Early Upward Budget Revisions as Complex Applications Move into Production

Budgets are beginning to move higher. As 2027 plans become clearer and agentic and other applications move from pilot to production, some companies have raised their AI spending forecasts. Of nine companies we recently revisited, five raised their forecasts, three left them unchanged, and one lowered its estimate because testing and change management were taking longer than expected. The upward revisions were mainly driven by pilots entering production and new agentic applications. The sample is too small to change the overall outlook, but both the direction of the revisions and their drivers are consistent with our analysis.

Startups Have Both the Appetite and the Means to Spend

For startups, deployment timelines are less of a constraint than they are for traditional enterprises. The central questions are whether startups want to spend on AI and whether they can afford to. Our research suggests neither is a significant constraint today.

  1. Startups Are Generally More Willing to Spend on AI

Startups are small, have simpler processes, and carry less legacy infrastructure and organizational inertia. They can identify and deploy new applications quickly, with more freedom to experiment. Coding, one of AI’s most mature applications, is also central to their work: AI directly affects how quickly they improve their products and how much each employee can produce. Ramp data show that median monthly AI spending per employee at VC- backed companies has risen to about $112, roughly 9-10 times the level at PE-backed companies (about $12) and other businesses (about $11). The gap has widened markedly since 2025.

Ramp: median monthly AI spending per employee by funding type

A corporate spend management platform:

“Rank the companies on our platform by token spending and you would be surprised how many near the top have fewer than 200 employees. Most are part of this AI wave and were founded relatively recently. On a per-employee basis, the top 1% are mostly fast-growing, VC-backed startups. They have substantial revenue coming in and venture funding behind them, so AI spending keeps accelerating. They do not have to cut other spending to fund it. They also adopted coding agents earlier and use them more extensively, and coding agents are the main driver of AI spending.”

AI-native businesses, a major focus of venture investment in recent years, are even more willing to spend because AI underpins their products and operations. According to PitchBook-NVCA, AI’s share of US venture deal value rose from about 21% in 2018 to roughly 67% in 2025 and 83% in the first nine months of 2026. Even excluding OpenAI and Anthropic financings, AI accounted for more than 55% in 2025.

An AI search startup:

“Our internal AI spending is definitely accelerating this year because AI can do more, and we are deliberately making our documents and code part of its context. I expect growth next year to be similar to this year; doubling is certainly achievable. Engineers’ allowances are effectively unlimited. We want people to use the best models and only block the ones that are excessively expensive. The startups around us are much the same.   1. Venture Funding Remains Plentiful and IPO Exits Could Replenish It Further

Whether startups can sustain this spending also depends on their finances. Many have yet to break even, so their ability to spend on AI remains closely tied to external funding, as our interviews confirmed.

A corporate spend management platform:

“Startups’ spending capacity is tied to VC funding. I would say that is the main factor. They are growing quickly, and many generate revenue themselves, but they also have a lot of venture capital behind them.”

An AI search startup:

“Our gross margin is fine, but we are not fully at break-even yet, so funding still matters in the near term.”

Venture capital remains plentiful, particularly when prospective exit proceeds from the SpaceX (SPCX) and Anthropic (ANT) listings are taken into account. Based on lockup schedules and current share prices, we estimate that more than $500 billion of venture capital could be realized and recycled into investment by the end of 2027. That is roughly equivalent to total US venture deal value in 2024 and 2025 combined, providing further support for startup funding and rapid AI spending growth.

The Impact of Token Budgeting

Led by technology companies, businesses began tightening controls on AI spending in 26Q2. This was one of the main reasons the two frontier labs’ combined ARR growth slowed in 26Q3. We first review the main ways companies manage token spending, then assess how far each customer group has progressed and how much room remains.

Two Approaches to Token Budgeting

  1. Administrative Controls Include Individual and Team Usage Caps, Stricter ROI Approval for New Applications, and

Restrictions on Expensive Models, High Reasoning Settings, and Fast Mode. These Are Relatively Easy to Implement Through Management Directives and Tend to Produce Results Quickly.

  1. Fast mode can increase Opus 5.5’s output speed by up to about 2.5x, but doubles the unit price. Disabling it can

therefore halve the corresponding cost.

  1. Engineering measures include prompt caching, model routing, context compression, and batching. They require

technical expertise and continuing optimization, making them harder to implement.

  1. Prompt caching: Anthropic’s own tools, such as Claude Code, enable it by default. But when developers call

Anthropic’s API through an in-house harness or another third-party tool, caching is not turned on automatically; they must enable it themselves. For Opus 5.5, cache reads cost just 5% of the standard input price. We estimate that enabling caching can reduce total costs by roughly 30-40%.

  1. Model routing assigns requests to models at different price and capability levels according to task difficulty.

Most companies currently route at the task level, selecting one model for an entire task. As discussed in our earlier research, step-level routing, which selects a model for each step within a task, could cut costs further, but third-party tools need to mature.

Traditional Enterprises Have Adopted Spending Controls Faster Than Engineering Improvements

  1. AI still accounts for a small share of traditional enterprises’ total expenses, so they pay less attention to these

costs than technology companies do. Their technical capabilities are also generally less developed. As a result, their token budgeting practices are less mature.

  1. The timing has varied widely. A few respondents began in 2025, others followed from 26Q2, and adoption became more

widespread in 26H2. Some have still done little or have introduced only basic measures.

  1. Budget caps, ROI approvals, and changes to billing arrangements have spread quickly and are now fairly common.

Engineering measures such as prompt caching and model routing are also in use, but their coverage and sophistication lag those of technology companies. At least 16 of our 58 respondents explicitly said they had not enabled cache optimization; others were unsure whether they were using it.

  1. We estimate that around 60% of respondents still have substantial room to optimize, potentially cutting the cost of a

given task by another 20-30%. However, respondents have already factored token budgeting into their 2027 budgets. These potential savings therefore do not imply a corresponding reduction in their forecast AI spending.

Enterprise survey: adoption of token budgeting measures and remaining savings potential

A North American sports streaming company:

“We are probably only halfway through cost optimization. We have implemented about 50-60% of the obvious, relatively easy measures. Next comes deeper engineering work. For example, an agent may have 15-20 tools, with every definition resent on each call. Retrieving tools only when needed would reduce that to two or three per step. Static prompt caching is live; semantic caching is still in staging. Non-real-time tasks can also use the batch API at another 50% discount.”

A North American medical device company:

“We introduced prompt caching three or four months ago and are still refining it. We are also trying to add a routing layer. Orchestration still needs Opus, but for execution we are testing whether a group of Haiku agents can replace some Opus and Sonnet usage. For the same workload, I think we could save another 35-60%.”

A large North American telecom operator:

“We only started using prompt caching in earnest over the past 6-12 months and will roll out over the next few quarters. Task-level routing entered production only 9-12 months ago, and its traffic coverage has gradually risen to 30-45%. Step-level routing is next. Taken together, these measures could still save 20-30% of our API spending.”

A large European insurer:

“Caching is enabled, but we are only just introducing context compression, output-length limits, and per- query token caps. Individual token budgets have also only just moved from testing to enforcement. We have not applied dynamic routing to actual use cases yet. Done well, it could save at least 20-25%.”

A midsize North American bank:

“We use these measures here and there, but not systematically. We mainly lack experience and mature practices. I would estimate that 30-40% of our costs could have been avoided. Model routing is still in the early design stage.”

A global advertising and media group:

“We only moved from unrestricted use to governed use around May this year. Prompt caching and routing are both still in pilot, and progress is uneven. I estimate another 20-30% of savings are possible.”

A global PR and communications company:

“Moving everything to our own gateway platform should save about 40%, and that migration is still under way. We have tested model routing in only a few applications. Making use of the AI features we already pay for in enterprise software could save another 10-15%.”

Technology Companies Have Tightened Budgets and Are Unlikely to Repeat the Earlier Spending Surge

  1. Technology companies were the fastest-growing AI spenders in the first half of this year. As coding agents spread

rapidly from the start of the year, companies worried about falling behind on AI and opened up internal use, and employees embraced the new tools. Meanwhile, many cost-saving measures were not yet in place. Two stood out. The first was caching. As noted above, prompt caching is on by default in Anthropic’s own tools such as Claude Code, so individuals and companies using them saw savings early. But companies calling the API through their own harnesses or internal platforms often went months without realizing they had to turn it on; our checks suggest some large technology companies only found out in June. The second was fast mode, which employees widely preferred despite its doubled unit price. With usage surging and unit costs not coming down, spending far exceeded expectations, and some companies even ran through their full-year budgets ahead of schedule.

  1. From 26Q2, technology companies recognized that spending had run too high and began tightening controls across the

board, moving faster than the other two customer groups. Public announcements and our interviews point to individual and team spending caps as the main company-level measures. Explicit bans on fast mode are rare, but these caps limit its use indirectly. On the engineering side, teams are now well versed in cost optimization, and most companies have already adopted the main techniques, such as model routing and caching. There is limited room to cut spending further through engineering work or tighter caps, although newer approaches such as step-level routing may deliver modest gains.

  1. Additional investment now requires evidence of returns. A repeat of the explosive spending growth seen in 26H1 looks

unlikely. Spending is more likely to grow steadily with business volumes and the deployment of new applications.

A North American customer service SaaS company:

“AI spending grew so quickly in the first quarter that it startled us. From the second quarter, controlling it became a priority rather than an afterthought. For coding, we restrict Opus and mainly use Sonnet. Outside coding, we built a gateway connecting frontier and open-weight models, and use evaluations to compare cost and quality. If quality is similar, we switch to the cheaper model. Tasks that can run offline go through batch processing, and we have added prompt caching and semantic caching. Spending growth is now reasonably controlled, and there is not much engineering optimization left. From here it should mostly follow business volumes: if the business doubles, spending will probably double too.”

A music streaming company:

“AI spending has increased severalfold this year. Usage per person is up, and many more people are using it. In the first and second quarters, the approach was ‘token maxing’: people could use as much as they wanted. By the third quarter, AI had become an everyday tool for all knowledge work, and we introduced controls. There is no hard cap, but we monitor unusual usage, send alerts, and ask managers to investigate. We restrict the available models. Most people default to Opus 5.5; Fable requires a separate request. Default reasoning and speed settings are also low. We have adopted essentially all the existing engineering techniques, although we are still tuning the settings. We might save another 10% on the same tasks. Growth will slow substantially next year, but spending will still rise. The main constraint is the budget: people want to see ROI before investing more.”

  1. Basic techniques such as caching and cheaper models are already common among startups. OpenRouter data show that

cache hits accounted for about 60% of input tokens in agentic calls at the start of the year; that has since risen to almost 90%. Vercel data also show active adoption of fast, low-cost decision models such as Jev, which accounted for about one-quarter of gateway requests in October.

  1. There is still room for more precise management. Some AI-native startups told us their controls remain fairly

Prompt caching is always on, and tasks that are not urgent use the batch API. We do some routing, but only for a few frequent, predefined tasks. Scheduled jobs, context compression, memory, and similar simple steps are assigned directly to cheaper models. We do not have dynamic routing yet. There are allowances, such as $10,000 per person per month, but I have not heard of anyone actually hitting a limit. They are effectively unlimited. Overall, we have not pushed optimization very far, and cost controls are not especially strict.”

How This Differs from SaaS Optimization in 2022 and 2023

Widespread tightening of AI spending naturally recalls the SaaS and cloud optimization cycle of 2022-2023. But looking back at what caused that cycle and what followed, the two involve very different stages of demand development.

  1. The Earlier Cycle Removed Waste from Existing Spending
  2. Companies expanded software and cloud spending aggressively during the pandemic. As the economy weakened and

technology companies began large-scale layoffs in 2022, businesses revisited those commitments.

  1. For seat-based SaaS, the main problem was buying more licenses than employees used. Data published by platforms

including Zylo and Productiv in 2023 suggested that roughly 40-50% of enterprise seats were unused or underused. Companies reclaimed idle licenses, consolidated overlapping tools, and negotiated lower renewal prices. Layoffs also directly reduced seat counts.

  1. For usage-based cloud and data platforms, optimization meant using services less frequently and adjusting resource

allocations. AWS revenue growth fell from 33% in 22Q2 to 12% in 23Q2; Snowflake’s net revenue retention fell from 158% at the end of January 2023 to 135% at the end of October. Both management teams identified customer optimization as a main driver, alongside a weaker economy, more competition, and tougher comparisons. But optimization did not keep pushing spending lower indefinitely. AWS management said at the time that customers were optimizing technology spending, not cutting it, and would invest the savings in new workloads. By 23Q3, AWS growth had stabilized at 12% and optimization had eased materially. The cycle was largely a one-time removal of waste, after which spending returned to growing with business activity and cloud migration.

  1. The Fundamental Difference Is the Stage of Demand Development
  2. The spending optimized in 2022-2023 served a relatively well-defined set of needs. SaaS functionality was largely

fixed, and cloud workloads mainly involved running existing businesses or migrating them from on-premises systems. The task was to eliminate waste and align spending with actual business volumes. Demand did not expand rapidly because the software itself became more capable.

  1. AI capabilities are still improving quickly, and the range of tasks models can perform keeps expanding. There has

been waste: in 26H1, companies including Meta and Amazon introduced internal leaderboards that ranked employees by token usage, prompting some to consume tokens on trivial tasks to climb the rankings. Those leaderboards were subsequently discontinued. Beyond such cases, however, most usage still reflects additional demand and value creation.

  1. Token budgeting therefore reduces the cost of performing a given task, rather than the underlying demand. Lower

unit costs may also make viable some applications that previously failed to meet ROI thresholds.

A North American gaming company:

“Demand itself is not slowing. Our token usage will still double next year, but spending will not grow as quickly because we have tightened cost controls through engineering and usage limits. Some agents that used to run on Opus 5 run fine on Sonnet, cutting their cost in half. When employees hit their limits, nine times out of ten they have chosen the wrong model: they defaulted to Fable when Haiku would have been enough.”

  1. Growth Is Shifting from Headcount to Usage

Seat-based SaaS spending is tied to employee numbers, which set a ceiling on growth. AI is closer to usage- based cloud services: agents can run continuously and in parallel, allowing consumption to grow independently of headcount. As we have argued repeatedly, the ultimate ceiling on AI spending is the labor cost it can replace.

The Impact of Competition

Within the overall enterprise AI spending opportunity, Anthropic’s share depends on direct competition with OpenAI, substitution by open-weight models, and the practical barriers to switching models.

Competition Among Frontier Labs

Frontier labs have accelerated model releases and stepped up price cuts this year. We have covered those developments in earlier reports. Here we focus on how the competition is showing up in the two companies’ ARR and market shares.

  1. New Model Releases Have Shifted ARR Growth Between the Two Leading Frontier Labs

When ARR growth accelerates at Anthropic or OpenAI, it often slows at the other. Anthropic led in 26H1: a rapid succession of releases from Opus 4.6 through 4.8, alongside continued growth in Claude Code, drove ARR to more than double quarter over quarter for two consecutive quarters. OpenAI grew much more slowly. The pattern reversed in 26Q3. OpenAI fully rolled out the GPT-5.6 Sol series in July, then released the GPT-6 series in September and halved Sol and Luna prices. Its ARR rose more than 70% quarter over quarter, while Anthropic’s growth slowed to 29%.

How Hard It Is for Companies to Switch Models

Model selection criteria are only part of the story. The extent to which capability and price changes translate into market share also depends on whether companies actually switch.

  1. Coding Users Switch Fastest but Are Becoming More Sensitive to Cost

Coding results are easy to verify, and coding tools are loosely coupled to business systems. Rules files and code repositories remain the same when the model changes, so technical switching costs are minimal. Developers also tend to prefer the most capable model and move quickly after releases, making market share more volatile.

A European payroll and HR services company:

“Developers are keen to try every new model. Take Fable: we had set spending allowances, and after it launched they used them up in about two days.”

A North American digital bank:

“Many developers now use open-source coding tools. The engineering framework stays the same and the codebase does not need to change, so moving between models is very easy. Once you decide which model you want, you can switch at any time.”

Even in coding, where capability carries substantial weight, cost is becoming more important and budgets constrain model choice.

A music streaming company:

“Switching costs in coding are almost zero. Rules files live in Git, and you can launch any harness from the same directory. You can use Claude today and Codex tomorrow. Engineers are also very willing to switch: if a new model benchmarks better and colleagues say it works well, they will move. But if the manager says they are spending too much, they will go back to a cheaper model. Our engineering usage leaned toward OpenAI midyear, then moved back to Anthropic after Opus 5.5 launched.”

  1. Production Workflows Rarely Switch Without Major Savings and Favor Incumbent Providers

Live workflows have already been tuned to their existing models, whose capabilities are sufficient for the job. Switching means retuning, regression testing, and the risk of changed outputs. Only a very large cost advantage is likely to justify a move. That favors incumbent providers: once a model is embedded in production, the associated spending is relatively secure.

Longer-Term Applications Offer More Potential but Need More Time to Prove Themselves

  1. Physical AI: robot deployments could begin scaling around 2030. Understanding and planning are increasingly being

expressed as coding tasks handled by large models, while dedicated controllers and smaller models manage low-level control. Speed and reliability remain the constraints. Simple tasks still take too long, and fine manipulation and performance in changing environments need further validation. Lower inference costs and smaller on-device models should help. We expect understanding and planning to be largely solved in 2027, giving robot intelligence its GPT-3.5 or even GPT-4 moment. A further two or three years of hardware testing, reliability validation, and manufacturing preparation would be needed before deployment at scale. In the near term, Anthropic mainly generates token revenue from the intelligence layer: planning, coding, and building simulations. It remains unclear who will supply the on-device models and robot hardware.

Near-term value is more likely to appear as API calls and token usage for model providers than as a separate revenue stream.

The Implications of Recent Developments at NVIDIA

We have analyzed the implications of the NVIDIA developments for the AI supply chain in earlier reports. For model providers, the effects would come through compute supply, influencing both inference costs and the pace of capability improvements. They could alter two trends observed in our research: intensifying price competition among frontier labs and a rising share for open-weight models.

Compute Uncertainty Has Increased for OpenAI and Could Benefit Anthropic

  1. NVIDIA May Become Less Able to Secure Supply and Sustain Its Technology Cadence
  2. To assess the impact, we need to revisit the value NVIDIA has provided GPU customers: dependable access to compute

and a consistently leading product roadmap.

On supply, NVIDIA has long secured more than half of TSMC’s CoWoS capacity. Its strong influence over upstream suppliers has itself been an important assurance that GPU customers can obtain enough compute.

On technology, NVIDIA accelerated its release cycle under Jensen Huang. Ampere (2020), Hopper (2022), and Blackwell (2024) followed a roughly two-year cadence. The company then moved to annual generations: Blackwell Ultra in 2025, Vera Rubin shipping in 26H2, followed on the roadmap by Rubin Ultra in 2027 and Feynman in 2028. That gave GPU customers a predictable trajectory of declining compute costs.

  1. Both advantages may weaken following the recent developments. If NVIDIA becomes more conservative about

financing, investment, and capacity commitments, its ability to assure supply could diminish. If a transition to professional management slows hardware upgrades and produces a more cautious technology roadmap, ASICs’ TCO advantage could widen and their performance gap with GPUs could narrow.

  1. OpenAI Depends More Heavily on NVIDIA

OpenAI’s current compute fleet is dominated by NVIDIA GPUs. It has signed agreements with AMD, Broadcom, and Cerebras, but most of those deployments are still ramping. Anthropic already uses substantial amounts of TPU, Trainium, and NVIDIA GPU capacity and plans to add AMD GPUs. That makes it less exposed to disruption at any single supplier.

  1. Less Aggressive OpenAI Pricing Would Ease Pressure on Anthropic

OpenAI could face pressure on both supply and cost. Available GPU capacity may become constrained, and inference costs may decline more slowly than at competitors that rely primarily on ASICs. OpenAI may therefore price less aggressively, easing competitive pressure on Anthropic.

A Widening Capability Gap Could Weaken Demand for Open-Weight Models

Open-weight models have been gaining share of enterprise spending and putting some pressure on lower-tier closed models. We expect the capability gap between open and closed models to widen again over the next 3-6 months, reducing the incentive to choose open-weight alternatives. Anti-distillation measures are the main driver, with NVIDIA’s new leasing restrictions accelerating the effect.

  1. Anti-Distillation Measures and Latent Reasoning Make Catching Up Harder

As discussed in our earlier research, frontier labs’ anti-distillation efforts and the use of latent reasoning in frontier models are making it harder to reproduce top closed models through distillation. Open-weight developers must rely more on reinforcement learning, synthetic data, and training environments, which increases their compute needs. We expect the leading open-weight models to fall roughly 6-12 months behind the best closed models again.

  1. NVIDIA’s Curbs on GPU Leasing to Chinese Companies and New Export Restrictions Make Compute Harder to Obtain

On November 16, the US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security also announced new remote- access rules, expected to take effect on November 19 upon publication in the Federal Register. Suppliers would need licenses to ship advanced chips to any data center worldwide that provides companies headquartered in China with large-scale access to advanced-chip compute. Existing services would need licenses within 90 days after the rules take effect. Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil have also been added to the destinations requiring advanced-chip export licenses. Shipping chips to data centers outside China and then providing compute to Chinese companies will become significantly harder. Our channel checks also indicate that server vendors have tightened know-your-customer checks for buyers in several Southeast Asian countries. Without additional compute, Chinese developers will face greater constraints on training their next generation of models.

  1. Less Scope for Open-Weight Substitution Would Favor Frontier Labs

Open-weight developers would face both harder distillation and less compute. Enterprises currently use their models mainly for repetitive, simple, low-risk work, replacing lower-tier or older closed models. A wider capability gap would reduce the scope for that substitution. Gains in open-weight models’ share of enterprise spending could slow, leaving frontier labs’ positions more secure.

Overall, Near-Term Growth Is Under Pressure, but Competition Is Tilting in Anthropic’s Favor

We return to the questions raised at the outset:

  • Demand across the three customer groups: traditional enterprises expect slower growth, but this largely reflects a shift from seats to complex applications. Usage from new applications should rise from 27H2. Startups have support from prospective VC exit proceeds. Technology companies are tightening controls, making another surge in spending unlikely.
  • KV caching and routing: these reduce unit costs rather than demand. Traditional enterprises still have room to optimize, but they have already included the expected savings in their 2027 budgets.
  • The comparison with SaaS optimization in 2022-2023: that cycle removed waste from existing spending that served relatively well-defined needs. AI demand is still expanding, and spending is shifting from a headcount basis to a usage basis.
  • Competition from OpenAI and open-weight models: capabilities still drive most share shifts, but price is becoming more important. Pressure from open-weight models is concentrated at the lower end, and their overall spending share remains small.
  • Switching barriers: coding moves fastest. Established production workflows rarely migrate, which favors incumbents, while new workflows are the main competitive battleground. CSP channels are lowering the barriers to switching providers.
  • New applications in 2027: the likely sequence in which revenue emerges is cybersecurity, enterprise applications supported by FDEs, then multimodal applications. Physical AI and AI-driven drug discovery will take longer.
  • The NVIDIA developments: OpenAI may become less aggressive on pricing, which would benefit Anthropic. The capability gap between open-weight and closed models could widen again. Frontier labs could gain more market share.
