SUMMARY

Overview

A gastroenterology expert reviewed emerging celiac disease therapies, focusing on a developer’s CD122-targeting antibody and comparisons with IL-15 and competing CD122 programs. Current care relies on strict gluten avoidance, which controls many patients but leaves unmet needs among those with accidental exposure, persistent inflammation, or refractory disease.

Key Insights

The CD122 program produced encouraging proof-of-concept evidence, with statistically significant protection of intestinal tissue during gluten exposure. Histologic improvement is promising, but the clinical importance of the observed effect sizes remains uncertain. The expert viewed the safety profile favorably because reported adverse events were lower with treatment than placebo, potentially reflecting better disease control. Future trials should combine biopsy findings with symptoms, anemia measures, and celiac antibody levels. There is no clear mechanistic winner between CD122 and IL-15 inhibition. Binding strength, target occupancy, dosing, and durability may differentiate drugs sharing a target. Initial use would likely focus on refractory or poorly controlled patients. Uptake among diet-controlled patients should be gradual, and well-controlled, highly adherent patients may not need medication. First-mover advantage should be modest. Long-term leadership will depend more on consistent efficacy, safety, access, and patient-support programs.

Clinical Data

The lead study used four weeks of treatment administered every two weeks, followed by a two-week gluten challenge of 6 grams daily. The expert considered this sufficient to detect tissue deterioration and demonstrate prevention of damage. A second cohort assessed treatment over four weeks with follow-up at 12 weeks, aiming to show healing of existing injury rather than prevention alone.

The villus-height-to-crypt-depth and intraepithelial lymphocyte findings were considered clinically relevant diagnostic measures. However, symptoms may change less consistently or more slowly than biopsy findings.
