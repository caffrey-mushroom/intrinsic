01 Board crisis and affair cover-up

Breaking news interrupted the recording: the Wall Street Journal reported NVIDIA’s board was preparing to meet as soon as Wednesday, Nov. 11, to determine Jensen Huang’s future following reports of two extramarital affairs, one with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, described in media reports as a suspected Chinese intelligence asset. Bishop noted a New York Times report that Huang had an ongoing affair with an NVIDIA VP, alleging his security detail offered a settlement and non-disclosure agreement to a bystander who filmed the two together — behavior the hosts say no board could tolerate from a public-company CEO regardless of the China angle. Both hosts said they had hoped Huang could survive the scandal but concluded the board has no real choice given its fiduciary duty, especially once corporate resources were allegedly used to help cover it up.

02 Commerce Department dysfunction

Axios reporting, per Bishop, described a Thursday principals’ meeting where Bessent, Hegseth, USTR’s Greer, and Rubio agreed to close Megaspeed-related loopholes even if it angered China, while Lutnick stalled by saying he needed presidential sign-off. When someone called his bluff and arranged a same-day Oval Office meeting, Trump phoned Musk, who reportedly backed the hawks; Trump then told Lutnick to get it done, though Lutnick said he would develop some options first. The hosts view this as evidence Lutnick may be shielding NVIDIA against the president’s own stated wishes, raising questions — which they expect Congress to probe — about whether China-trade caution, NVIDIA’s lobbying capture, or something else is driving his resistance.

03 Why the leak now and who Alice Huang really is

Turning to the pre-news conversation, the hosts examined why a memo on Huang’s relationship — reportedly commissioned by Rubio and first reported by the NYT on October 29 — surfaced now, roughly six months after it was drafted. Bishop speculated the leak’s timing, coming after the midterms and just before NDAA chip-control amendments and the Trump-Xi APEC meeting, suggests a senior official chose to weaponize it against NVIDIA’s lobbying position, though he repeatedly stressed the uncertainty of who was behind it. On Alice Huang’s actual role, Bishop cited FT and NYT reporting suggesting she may be less a trained MSS operative than an opportunistically co-opted “wheeler dealer” businesswoman the security services later found useful, noting one FT source said the MSS itself reached out this year asking about her, implying she wasn’t formally working for them. Both hosts stressed Huang’s extraordinary value as an intelligence target given his direct access to Trump.

04 Megaspeed allegations and NVIDIA’s China footprint

The hosts revisited the May Culper Research short report alleging Huang maintained an unusually close relationship with Alice Huang and Megaspeed, including partying together as early as June 2024, dining in Taiwan in 2025, and site visits always accompanied by Alibaba representatives, with Megaspeed allegedly funded largely by Alibaba and spending Sharp put at about $7.5 billion. Bloomberg reporting cited found that Megaspeed had imported 50,000 more chips than NVIDIA could account for in its data centers. Sharp noted this pattern of Southeast Asia-linked chip diversion stories has recurred for years without Commerce enforcement, calling it standard operating procedure NVIDIA has previously weathered.

05 NVIDIA’s collapsing DC standing

Citing Politico reporting comparing NVIDIA’s DC tactics to a mob boss, the hosts described a company that for years allegedly bullied critics — citing an earlier incident involving CSIS’s Greg Allen — and lacked the sophisticated political infrastructure of big tech peers, leaving it exposed now that its credibility has broken down. Both say the damage extends beyond Huang: executives’ statements on export-control compliance can no longer be trusted, and scrutiny is likely to intensify on how NVIDIA books revenue through Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand. Representative Brian Mast reportedly threatened to subpoena Huang to testify around November 18, which the hosts call a major bipartisan escalation given Congress previously held subpoena power but had declined to use it against NVIDIA.

06 Legislation and US-China stability risk

The hosts outlined three pending bills potentially folded into the NDAA: the Chip Security Act (chip-location tracking to detect smuggling), the MATCH Act (pushing allies to match US restrictions on chipmaking equipment exports), and the AI Overwatch Act (an 18-month ban on advanced AI chip exports to China with certification requirements). Bishop warned enforcement is uncertain even if the bills pass, drawing a parallel to Trump’s non-enforcement of the TikTok divestment law. On the broader relationship, Bishop said the US is reportedly receiving only about half to two-thirds of expected rare-earth concessions from China, with the two-month extension through January already in question, and suggested hawks like Rubio and possibly Bessent may be shifting back toward an escalate-to-de-escalate posture ahead of next week’s Trump-Xi APEC meeting in Shenzhen, raising the odds, in the hosts’ assessment, of a real break in the fragile US-China truce.

01 Compute as the central bottleneck

Buchanan, whose new book with Tantum Collins frames a ‘bitter struggle’ over AI, says his Biden-era policy team entered office convinced that compute, more than data, is what AI progress runs on — a belief that shaped the CHIPS Act, export controls, the AI Safety Institute, and the National Security Memorandum directing intelligence and defense agencies to adopt private-sector AI. He states that Chinese AI CEOs, including DeepSeek’s, have publicly acknowledged they have sufficient capital and talent but are constrained specifically by compute — a claim Buchanan treats as near-dispositive evidence that export controls are working. He asserts that if the US sold China unlimited chips, China would have closed or overtaken the capability gap.

02 Defending export controls against critics

Buchanan counters the argument that US controls merely provoked Chinese indigenization by noting Xi Jinping recognized as early as 2014 that China was spending over $300B annually importing chips — more than it spent on oil — and began investing upwards of $200B in public and private capital toward domestic chipmaking, well before the Biden administration acted. He credits Trump’s first term with meaningfully slowing China by cutting off Dutch EUV lithography access, and says the Biden team viewed its own controls as building on that foundation rather than initiating a new conflict. He criticizes Trump’s second term for repealing parts of the export control regime and declining to enforce others, arguing stricter enforcement would have widened the US lead further. He also addresses import controls on Chinese robotics and tech, saying he supports them only where a genuine national security threat exists, citing Chinese cars as an example, but disclaims deep expertise on humanoid or quadruped robots.

03 Enforcement failures and hindsight regrets

Asked what he’d change, Buchanan points to a specific technical miss: the team did not foresee the 2022-23 shift toward test-time compute, which elevated the importance of high-bandwidth memory chips (like those in the H20) that weren’t adequately restricted early on. He also faults US government execution broadly, saying chip and chip-die smuggling continued because Commerce was under-resourced, calling it a government failure to fully implement the policy, distinct from what he still considers a correct underlying strategy.

04 Grading the Trump administration and partisan dynamics

Buchanan contrasts the Biden team’s posture with successor David Sacks’s approach of letting the private sector proceed unimpeded, and JD Vance’s early framing of AI policy around opportunity rather than safety — a stance Buchanan says Vance has since partly walked back. He expresses cautious optimism that there are some signs the administration is taking AI risk more seriously, but stresses that genuine policy shifts will depend heavily on Trump’s personal conviction rather than ideology, since he doesn’t see AI acceleration as a fixed conservative or Republican commitment. He also argues regulation historically builds trust and economic growth, citing 19th-century railroad standardization as precedent.

05 Timelines, governance scenarios, and what comes next

Discussing the AI Futures Project’s forecast of superhuman AI researchers by 2027 and broadly dominant AI by 2028, Buchanan says his team held similar short-timeline beliefs as far back as 2021-22 and built institutions (chip controls, the AI Safety Institute, cyber/bio risk reviews) partly in anticipation. He distinguishes his approach from Leopold Aschenbrenner’s Situational Awareness scenario of lab nationalization, saying the Biden team never approached a Manhattan Project-style takeover and instead favored closer but non-nationalized government-lab relationships, including mandatory safety testing and embedded evaluators — an approach he believes a second Biden or Harris term would have extended further.

01 The scandal’s shifting center of gravity

The episode’s intro was rewritten minutes before publishing because Huang resigned as NVIDIA CEO amid reporting on his affairs, including an alleged relationship with Alice Huang, described in NYT reporting as a suspected Chinese intelligence asset. The interview with Chan, a Brookings Institution fellow, was recorded two days before the resignation, so his analysis addresses the scandal’s geopolitical logic rather than the resignation itself. Alice Huang is an executive at Megaspeed, an NVIDIA customer that Chan says builds data centers in Malaysia and could potentially serve as a conduit for Chinese firms to access cutting-edge Blackwell chips, though Chan repeatedly stresses how much remains unconfirmed and speculative.

02 What China could actually want

Patel pushes Chan on why Beijing would target Huang specifically, since he isn’t a chip engineer and China’s access to NVIDIA hardware already flowed partly through Megaspeed. Chan offers two theories: gleaning insight into U.S. policymaking, and, possibly the more significant concern, attempting to influence U.S. export-control decisions given Huang’s documented ability to reach Trump directly by phone. Chan also raises an alternative, less conspiratorial reading: Beijing may simply have wanted basic visibility into a chaotic, fast-moving relationship it didn’t fully control, rather than executing a deliberate strategy. Notably, Chan observes this scenario may have backfired on Beijing, since the controversy has now tainted the one prominent advocate for loosening export controls and galvanized Democrats pushing for tougher restrictions.

03 Governance failures and looming investigations

Patel details reporting that U.S. intelligence flagged concerns about Alice Huang and Megaspeed roughly a year before the scandal broke, via a memo overseen by Marco Rubio, yet no action followed. Separately, Patel cites reporting that NVIDIA general counsel Tim Teter invoked a relationship with a Commerce Department undersecretary to get a months-long inquiry into Megaspeed redirected to the DOJ, where it stalled; Trump has said the FBI is now investigating. Chan calls the lack of follow-through deeply troubling, and both speakers anticipate a congressional investigation and shareholder lawsuits, with Chan advising NVIDIA to cooperate proactively rather than backpedal after NVIDIA’s earlier public denials, which Patel attributes to its communications and legal chiefs, were contradicted by Huang’s own apology letter.

04 China’s diffusion-over-frontier AI strategy

Chan argues China doesn’t frame the AI race as a frontier contest: Beijing’s government reportedly sees the Western focus on AGI as wasteful, preferring to integrate good-enough AI broadly across its economy, especially manufacturing, often via open-source models. This contrasts with the U.S. AI-lab ethos, which Chan and Patel characterize as ideologically fixated on reaching transformative AGI. Chan says this divergence is also an intra-U.S. fault line, separating Silicon Valley labs from a broader public more focused on near-term costs and job impacts. Meanwhile, China is simultaneously building domestic chip alternatives (Huawei) for supply-chain resilience and weighing retaliatory export controls, such as on rare earths, if the U.S. tightens restrictions on chipmaking equipment.

05 NVIDIA without Jensen, and the broader espionage landscape

Both speakers question how much NVIDIA’s technical progress depends on Huang personally versus his less visible function as the industry’s risk-taking financier and market maker, whose public comments move NeoCloud and startup stock prices. Chan says losing that voice could leave a significant gap for the anti-AGI, pro-open-source, pro-diffusion camp in U.S. policy debates. The conversation also broadens to Chinese espionage across the AI sector, citing reported OpenAI employee warnings and TSMC/South Korea engineer poaching, and draws parallels to Apple and Tesla’s manufacturing moves to China, where technology transfer flowed both directions. Chan closes by dismissing the Silicon Valley trope of a monolithic, disciplined Chinese state, describing its system as rife with conflicting interests and corruption, with frequent bureaucratic turf wars and local-Beijing misalignment.

01 TeraWulf’s AI transition and Lake Mariner execution

TeraWulf, a former bitcoin miner, now derives 83% of revenue from AI services per its Q3 results, according to the hosts. CTO Nazar Khan details that Lake Mariner has already delivered 102 megawatts — 60MW for Core42 and 42MW for Fluidstack — with CB4 commissioning underway and CB5 (a replica of CB4) slated to complete the campus by end of Q1 2027. Khan says each building iteration is becoming faster and more efficient as the team’s experience scales from early 2-16MW halls to current 168-megawatt critical IT buildings. He attributes ongoing spec changes to the pace of hardware innovation — the original tenant agreement in August 2025 predated the actual hardware by four months — plus continuous industry-wide learning on deployment efficiency.

02 Rising costs and procurement strategy

Khan says Justified’s buildout cost guidance of $10-12M per megawatt (versus $8-10M at Lake Mariner) reflects most long-lead equipment and subcontracting already locked in, alongside a newly finalized Google backstop arrangement; TeraWulf plans to go to market for financing after the Anthropic IPO. He does not expect another ~20% cost jump for the Muskie site, though some increase is possible. On procurement, Khan describes a rolling 12-18 month forecast shared with key vendors (chillers, transformers, steel) to manage multi-year lead times, plus flexibility in electrical design, noting there is often more than one way to solve a given engineering constraint.

03 Power, Muskie, and regulatory risk

For the Muskie site’s second 500MW phase, Khan says utility AEP is comfortable supplying interim power ahead of the 760MW Big Sandy gas plant’s 2031 online date, and that AEP already has turbines on order even if the timeline slips. On the Chesapeake acquisition, closing hinges on environmental approvals and seller obligations to deliver the power plant in agreed condition, with no firm timeline from Khan. Asked about post-midterm political risk — moratorium proposals in Maryland, New York, and Kentucky — Khan argues TeraWulf’s near-term 250-500MW/year delivery pace (requiring billions of dollars of cash a year at the upper end) is unaffected, citing Kentucky’s Public Service Commission approving its energy supply agreement within three to four months and Governor Beshear’s executive order appearing to mirror TeraWulf’s existing approach.

04 CoreWeave’s guidance raise and debt load

The hosts report CoreWeave raised 2026 revenue guidance to $13-13.3B (from $12.4-13.2B) and exit ARR guidance to $20-21B (from $18.5-19.5B), driving adjusted operating income targets up and sending shares roughly 30% higher at the open, with IREN and Nebius also rallying. CoreWeave brought over 200MW online in Q3, reaching 1.7 gigawatts of active power, with Q3 CapEx of $14.5B exceeding original guidance due to ahead-of-schedule deployment. Management disclosed Ampere/Hopper GPU prices rose 30-50% quarter-over-quarter and some short-term contracts exceed $40M in annual revenue per megawatt. Despite the rally, CoreWeave carries $46.9B in debt plus ~$19.3B in lease liabilities, is spending $2.89 in CapEx for every $1 of revenue, and management says it will avoid unsecured debt at current rates, relying instead on investment-grade loans drawn in stages plus customer money paid up front, including about $9B expected over the next three months, per the hosts. The 2032 bond yield fell from an all-time high of 16.5% (Oct 14) to roughly 14.21%. Host Charlie draws and then complicates a shale-boom analogy, noting compute output (unlike rapidly declining shale wells) stays relatively consistent while GPU pricing is rising, undermining a simple bust comparison; Colin notes the key question is whether CoreWeave’s $132.5B contract backlog converts to cash flow sufficient to service debt, with the company not expected to be cash-flow positive until roughly 2028.

05 Anthropic-SpaceX compute deal in question

Citing Citrini Research, the hosts report Anthropic may decline to renew its SpaceX compute contract (worth up to $120.5B through 2029, ~$3.1B/month) past mid-2027, with Anthropic reportedly telling analysts AI is winner-take-all and that training the Claude 5.5 family cost over $10B. Neither company has confirmed. Host Charlie offers four theories: the deal was always meant as short-term “bridge compute” with a 90-day cancellation clause; the Colossus facility has had latency, aging-infrastructure, and mixed-NVIDIA-chip uptime issues; Anthropic and SpaceX/xAI are competitors (though both see this as a minor factor given Anthropic’s other multi-year deals); and — the theory Charlie favors — it’s a negotiating tactic to push down SpaceX’s Hopper/Blackwell pricing, which a Rittenhouse Research post he cites puts at about $30M per megawatt, ahead of cheaper Vera Rubin chips arriving next year. Colin notes SpaceX’s exposure is large relative to its ARR guidance if the deal falls through, but flags that the stock showed little reaction, suggesting uncertainty about how seriously to weight the report.

01 Anthropic’s IPO math

Anthropic set an initial IPO price range of $70-78 per share, implying a $1.3-1.4 trillion valuation, with final pricing expected November 18 and trading starting November 19. The hosts expect the range to be raised further, landing at $1.5-1.6 trillion, with a deliberate plan to “pop” from there; at the high end the raise would be roughly $60B in cash. The amended S-1 also revealed run-rate revenue hit $81 billion at the end of October, up $10B from the prior month — a stronger sequential month than prior periods — which the hosts attribute to momentum from the Opus 5.5 model cycle and the near-simultaneous release of Fable and Mythos 5.2 timed to the IPO.

Both hosts say hedge fund contacts are near-uniformly bullish on Anthropic given its scale economics and operating leverage, paraphrasing a hedge fund friend’s view that stock prices ultimately move based on whether more buyers than sellers want in, illustrating that narrative, not just fundamentals, drives pricing even at scale. They note structural volatility may be inherent to AI labs specifically because switching costs in the API business are low (as shown when OpenAI’s price cuts briefly took share from Anthropic over the summer), making labs trade more like volatile outcome distributions than stable SaaS businesses.

02 Jensen Huang and open source fallout

The hosts discuss the alleged Chinese spy controversy involving Jensen Huang (termed allegations throughout) and its implications for NVIDIA, whose stock is down roughly 30% from recent peak. Huang has long championed open source and chip exports to China under an economic logic the hosts call “commoditize your complement” — NVIDIA wants model/inference layers commoditized so GPU demand and integration value accrue to NVIDIA. They argue the controversy makes it politically untenable for Huang to keep advocating publicly for China access or open source, even though, per the hosts, serious analysts like Ben Thompson have argued there are rational reasons to sell chips into China to build CUDA dependency.

In their winners/losers framing, they expect closed-source Frontier Labs and non-NVIDIA chip companies to benefit, while American open source faces a mixed picture: trailing Chinese open-source efforts already, the controversy both threatens continued Chinese-model availability and simultaneously raises the strategic importance of domestic open source. One host argues broad intellectual support for open source should net out positively for American open-source efforts despite the setback.

03 The AI trade unwind

The hosts cite the SOX semiconductor index down over 20% in a month and CoreWeave down more than 40% from peak, attributing the pullback to a reset in data-center CapEx expectations from roughly 70 gigawatts to 40-50 gigawatts of new non-China capacity for next year. They argue the underlying problem is structural: combined AI lab ARR is under $150B while hyperscaler CapEx runs near $1 trillion annually, a gap they say makes some form of peak CapEx moment inevitable, referencing an earlier Sequoia essay warning revenue needs to catch up to CapEx.

The hosts frame the outlook around diffusion — AI value is real but unevenly penetrated across the economy, with usage inside individual accounts eventually rationalizing once a given model’s capability plateau is reached, while new frontier capability unlocks fresh spending. Both flag a possible bull catalyst: continued signs of recursive self-improvement (RSI), as recently suggested by Anthropic and Meter, which could trigger a major, unpredictable “unlock in intelligence” within 12-24 months, potentially extending into robotics breakthroughs that could produce outsized stock moves.

04 Midterms, data centers, and US-China sentiment

The hosts discuss the Democratic sweep of the House and Senate as decisive and note JD Vance publicly acknowledged the loss. They worry this could jeopardize Silicon Valley’s recent inroads with Washington given the highly polarized relationship, particularly for defense-tech-adjacent firms like SpaceX, Anduril, and Palantir. They cite a Gallup poll showing only 36% of Americans believe AI will mostly help the country versus 93% in China, calling it a severe narrative and geopolitical problem rather than a rational assessment, and link it to anti-data-center sentiment evident even among newly elected moderate Democrats like Texas governor Gina Hinojosa.

As a counterexample of community-aligned buildout, they cite Meta’s Hyperion data center project in Louisiana, which reportedly doubled local tax receipts and sharply increased teacher bonuses, in many cases to equal their salary. They contrast this with the 2017 Amazon HQ2 bidding war, arguing public sentiment has now flipped so that data center developers, not municipalities, must now prove community benefit before deals get done.

05 VC fundraising wave and firm identity

The hosts estimate $50-60 billion will be raised by major VC firms over the next 1-4 months, despite most having raised as recently as 12-18 months ago — a pace they say compresses historically typical fund deployment timelines. They note frontier labs (Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX) uniquely justified capital-intensive mega-funds, but with those labs approaching IPO or already funded, it’s unclear where comparable capital-absorbing opportunities exist, citing neolabs and robotics as unproven alternative categories. They argue mega-fund growth squeezes mid-size funds disproportionately and has made seed-stage valuations permanently elevated, since top firms no longer differentiate check sizes the way they once did.

On reflexivity, they describe how public-market selloffs propagate backward into private markets via crossover investors pulling back on pre-IPO deals, a traffic-wave effect that now moves faster than in past cycles due to hyperconnected information flow. They close by analyzing Sequoia’s restructuring — closing its London office, ending the Arc accelerator and scout program, merging early/growth teams, and cutting the Monday partner meeting — framing it not as a verdict on past strategy but as realignment of identity, strategy, and fund size, contrasted with Andreessen Horowitz’s maximalist expansion model.

  • At one site, community objections to emissions led Oracle to choose non-combustion natural gas power, so local acceptance now shapes power technology choices and may add cost or complexity (our read).

01 Demand and backlog

Thiagarajan frames OCI’s growth as driven by an exceptionally large AI/cloud infrastructure market that was anticipated roughly ten years ago when OCI began. He states that capacity for 2027 and 2028 is fully committed. Host Bob Evans puts the backlog at $664 billion, says it grew about 46% last quarter, and calls it almost the largest backlog any company has ever had. Thiagarajan frames this less as a sales success story and more as an execution challenge: the team’s task is to physically bring enough capacity online to fulfill commitments already made by customers.

02 Why OCI wins deals

Asked why customers choose OCI over other hyperscalers, Thiagarajan points to four pillars: Oracle’s roughly four-decade history as custodian of Fortune 1000 mission-critical databases, which makes OCI a natural extension for enterprises’ next workloads; best price-performance economics; infrastructure flexibility and choice; and security practices built into the infrastructure layer, which he attributes partly to Larry’s emphasis on security.

03 AI accelerating Oracle’s own operations

Thiagarajan says Oracle is using AI internally to compress deployment timelines across the business — in writing code, building tools, and improving network software reliability — rather than relying purely on headcount. He describes this as central to team culture, framing engineering goals around approaching a speed-of-light baseline for how fast new capabilities can be delivered. He links this AI-driven speed directly to the company’s ability to announce new capabilities that, by his account, would not have been possible without it.

04 From internal tools to enterprise products

Several recent Oracle product announcements originated as internal engineering fixes, according to Thiagarajan. Supply-chain optimization work built for Oracle’s own use became a capability inside Fusion applications. Developer pain points around maintaining, securing, and publishing in-house apps led to App Composer. Internal security hardening work for OCI’s own cloud became a security mesh product now offered to customers. He frames this as a deliberate strategy of using Oracle’s own technology first and understanding firsthand what enterprises need to accelerate AI, combined with partnerships spanning power, hardware/silicon, networking (citing continued investment in Acceleron), and data layers to deliver full-stack enterprise AI outcomes.

05 Power innovation and data center strategy

Access to power is described as the practical bottleneck beneath faster compute access. Thiagarajan details Oracle’s shift, starting in Abilene in 2024, to using turbines as backup generators instead of diesel generators, requiring new software to make UPS-based energy stores smooth out turbine ramp-up so AI workloads don’t stutter. Oracle has also added reciprocating engines (via a partnership with VoltaGrid), a non-combustion natural gas power source at one site where communities objected to emissions, plus solar and continued grid use, with Oracle presenting a uniform load profile to the grid so its demand resembles ordinary residential usage. Combined with the ability to build new regions in under 30 days using modular rack deployment, this power diversification underpins Oracle’s capacity rollout.

06 Community engagement

Thiagarajan frames data center buildout as requiring community responsibility, not just technology. Oracle commits to closed-loop liquid cooling systems and local workforce training — citing over 200 people trained in Abilene through a 12-week paid program for high-school and community-college graduates, aimed at building community trust and local economic buy-in around new facilities.
