  6. 专家提醒中国厂商的“开发完成”与龙头厂商约 90% 良率稳定量产的标准可能不同，长鑫起点单元更大，率先推出 4F² 或超越自身产品的 3D DRAM 并不代表领先三星或 SK 海力士，补贴只是延长其在低效率下维持生产的时间。
  7. 多数预测认为未来 3 到 4 年需求强劲、仅靠扩产难以满足，但专家对 2028–2029 年是否仍紧缺持保留态度，指出预测者本身是利益相关方，KV cache 压缩等效率提升对总需求的影响需看实际用量。
  8. 在 HBM 上客户愿为性能付溢价，使厂商更早导入新工艺，三星 1c 似乎先在 HBM 上稳定，美光表示 HBM4E 将用 1γ，三星 HBM4 采用 1c DRAM 和 4nm 逻辑基础 die，美光称同节点下 HBM3E 生产同样 bit 需约 DDR5 三倍的晶圆。
  9. 当前景气下厂商可能推迟节点转换以保住现有产线产出，公开报道称三星正调整下一节点转换节奏，这可能给持续研发的中国厂商缩小差距的窗口。
  10. EUV 与 High NA EUV 的价值在于减少重复图形化步骤、在洁净室空间紧缺时提高整厂良品产出，部分图形可用 1 次 High NA 曝光替代 3 到 4 次 Low NA 曝光，但若其他工序仍是瓶颈，产出不会同比例提升。

Where the discussion starts

New DRAM roadmaps keep drawing attention to 4F² cells, vertical channels and 3D DRAM. Much of the discussion focuses on which company will adopt them first. In this call, we asked what a new structure actually delivers in production. Does it produce more good chips at a lower cost? And how much does that calculation differ from one company to another?

Our guest has developed memory devices and processes and now studies how changes in DRAM technology shape future equipment requirements. We discussed the history of scaling, the payback on 4F² development, the manufacturing challenges of 3D DRAM, China’s progress and the meaning of volume production. The conversation also covered supply and demand, process choices for HBM, competing memory technologies, EUV productivity, and how changes in AI reach equipment roadmaps.

Key takeaways

  1. A structure’s name tells you little about the technology gap. Actual cell dimensions, yield, process cost and production scale matter. The same architecture can have different economics at companies starting from different positions.2. 3D DRAM needs an economical way to build the stack. Demonstrating a structure and making it at a speed and cost suitable for volume production are separate achievements. Equipment throughput and productivity become critical.3. The premium for HBM performance changes process decisions.Customers willing to pay for better performance can justify initial costs that would be harder to absorb in commodity DRAM.4. Market conditions change the value of equipment.Fewer process steps can reduce unit costs and increase output within limited fab space. During a shortage, the value of that additional output can carry more weight.

1. From memory development to equipment roadmaps

Damnang Could you start with a brief introduction? What have you worked on, and what do you focus on now?

Expert My background is in memory device development and process integration. I now work for a global semiconductor equipment company. In my earlier roles, I defined the structures, dimensions and process conditions for individual modules, then fabricated and tested wafers. We would find defects, work out what caused them and feed those findings back into the design. The goal was to make all the modules work together so the process could move into volume production.

Today, I look at where DRAM technology is heading and what equipment roadmaps will need to support it. My focus is roughly 5 years out and beyond. For nearer nodes, memory manufacturers and equipment suppliers are already working together on development. Further out, even the memory companies often cannot say for certain which structures they will use.

My job is to turn those expectations into equipment requirements. If this is the device we expect to make, what performance will each process tool need to deliver?

Damnang When you plan a DRAM roadmap that far ahead, how clear is the direction of the next architecture?

Expert There is no consensus yet on the most promising architecture for the next generation. Samsung, SK hynix and Micron are trying different approaches, as are manufacturers in China and Taiwan. These are people who fabricate and measure wafers and have access to extensive development data. The fact that they still make different choices tells me the direction is far from settled.

That uncertainty is the hardest part of preparing equipment. We cannot wait for memory manufacturers to finalize their choices. We have to judge which scenarios look most promising now and recommend a direction for equipment development. The tools have to be prepared before we know for certain which DRAM architecture will be adopted.

2. How DRAM structures converged and why scaling matters

Damnang I understand that a DRAM cell consists of one transistor and one capacitor. Within that basic design, how have the structures and manufacturing methods changed?

Expert The basic combination has stayed the same, but there have been many ways to arrange, connect and manufacture those two devices. Long ago, companies even differed on whether to build the capacitor in a trench in the substrate or stack it above the surface. In the middle and latter part of the 2000s, when Qimonda and Elpida were still around, several capacitor structures and cell layouts were competing.

As companies kept shrinking those structures and competing on cost, the alternatives fell away one by one. Today, despite differences in the details, the industry has largely converged on similar 6F² structures. Taking that structure through another node has become difficult, so manufacturers are looking for alternatives again. Their disagreement is about what should follow the architecture they currently share.

Damnang What has been the main reason for making DRAM cells smaller?

Expert The main reason has been to get more good bits from the same wafer and reduce manufacturing cost per bit. Historically, logic scaling also brought performance improvements, which were an important reason to shrink. DRAM can gain performance too, but the cell scaling we are discussing has primarily been about making memory more cheaply.

You can divide the cost of processing a wafer by the number of good bits it yields. Suppose processing cost rises 10% but good bits increase 20%. Cost per bit becomes 1.1 ÷ 1.2, or about 91.7% of the previous level. That is a reduction of roughly 8.3%. If the additional process cost rises faster, or initial yield is lower, a smaller cell may deliver no reduction in cost per bit.

The useful measure is the number of good bits you can actually sell, rather than the number the design could theoretically contain. Yield must be reflected in that figure, and packaging and test costs must be added when evaluating the finished product. Whether you shrink an existing cell or introduce a new structure, the reason to switch is the resulting reduction in cost per good bit. Area reduction alone does not establish the benefit.

Damnang How much room is left to shrink the current 6F² structure, and what comes after it?

Expert I think taking the existing structure through even one more node has become very difficult. Of course, the industry has kept finding a way forward whenever experts said it had reached the end. Warnings about a limit can sound like the boy who cried wolf. Even so, I increasingly feel that the existing structure has very little room left. That happens to coincide with node names approaching 10nm.

Moving beyond the current 6F² structure opens up several choices. There is 4F² using a vertical channel transistor, or VCT, as well as 3D DRAM and changes to the channel material. Some argue for moving to an entirely different type of memory. The comparison is between the additional process cost of each approach and the increase in bits it can deliver.

A new structure needs to offer more than a single reduction in size. It should support further improvements across several generations. We are looking for an economical path that might remain useful for another 10 years or so. Companies are pursuing different approaches because they disagree about which one can meet those conditions.

3. Why companies make different choices on 4F²

Damnang How are the major memory manufacturers approaching 4F²?

Expert CXMT is pursuing 4F², while Micron has outlined a path that skips it and prepares for 3D DRAM. That is one example of the different roadmaps companies are considering. But 4F² did not appear out of nowhere. Micron, Samsung and SK hynix have all spent a long time researching 4F² cells with vertical channels. People have been preparing the next structure with the eventual limits of the current one in mind for many years.

The industry has researched vertical channel 4F² cells and presented them at conferences since the 2000s. Micron also said in its recent webinar that it had studied the approach for more than 20 years. Ideas that were previously set aside because their cost and performance did not work are becoming candidates again as the existing structure approaches its limits. A long research history, however, does not by itself establish a reason to adopt a technology in volume production today.

Damnang Why do companies reach different decisions about the same 4F² approach?

Expert The cost of making the same structure differs from company to company. The calculation depends on the equipment they have already invested in, their labor costs, and whether they can use an existing fab or need to build a new one. Shrinking the cell may produce more chips per wafer, but the process cost of making those chips still has to be worked out.

The useful life of the structure also matters. If a company expects to use it across several generations, spending money to develop a new device may make sense. If it expects to move on again soon, there is less time to recover that investment. One company skipping 4F² does not mean the companies preparing it have made the wrong decision. The conditions that make it economical differ, even when the technology has the same name.

Damnang With that in mind, how do you interpret Micron’s decision to skip 4F² and prepare for 3D DRAM?

Expert My understanding is that Micron judged the actual density gain from 4F² to be insufficient relative to the added complexity and cost. Changing the name of the cell layout from 6 to 4 does not guarantee the shrink those numbers suggest. If the spacing of the capacitors limits density, the cell may not become small enough to justify the new transistor structure and process. Costs rise without a corresponding increase in good bits.

There is also the time available to recover development costs. If 4F² can serve several generations, there is a reason to spend money developing the device and improving yield. If its useful life looks short, extending 6F² while preparing the next architecture may be the better choice. That is why I interpret the decision partly as a question of recovering development costs.

Micron’s exclusion of 4F² from its current roadmap after more than 20 years of study does not amount to saying the technology cannot be built. It means the company has not chosen it as a next step that offers better cost and performance than its existing 6F² approach. A manufacturer with a different starting cell size, process capability and room for improvement could reach a different conclusion.

I read its 3D DRAM plans in the same way. Micron is preparing an economical next architecture while extending 6F², rather than committing to immediate volume production of 3D DRAM. Skipping the intermediate 4F² step gives it more reason to prepare 3D early. The actual transition should happen when the new structure can be manufactured more cheaply than the existing product.

Damnang Looking only at the numbers, moving from 6F² to 4F² seems to imply a reduction of one third in cell area. What should we check in a real comparison?

Expert Start by checking whether F is the same in both cases. F is the feature size used as a reference when expressing cell area. With the same F, moving from 6F² to 4F² reduces cell area by about 33%. The number of cells that fit in the same array area rises by 50%, based on 6 ÷ 4. The percentage reduction in area and the percentage increase in cell count are different figures.

Across different companies or processes, F and the actual cell dimensions need not be the same. If capacitor spacing cannot shrink enough, changing the transistor layout may deliver only a limited density gain. You need to check the actual capacitor pitch and cell area. The 6 and 4 in the architecture names cannot tell you how far ahead one company is.

Nor does 6F² always mean a literal 2F×3F rectangle. With capacitors arranged in a honeycomb pattern, some models use pitches of roughly 2.6F and 3F in the two directions, depending on the definition of F and the layout. The height of the triangular lattice gives 3F×√3/2≈2.6F, which produces an area of about 7.8F² under that definition. This does not mean every 6F² cell has that area. The actual coefficient for 4F² can also vary with the layout. It is another reason to compare physical dimensions rather than names.

Even a known reduction in cell area does not shrink the finished chip by the same percentage. Peripheral circuits must decode addresses, amplify small signals and send data out. You then have to account for yield and the additional process cost. To establish a cost benefit, you need to show how the smaller cell layout increases the number of good chips you obtain, and whether that gain outweighs the increase in manufacturing cost.

Damnang Another process specialist told me that the next DRAM architecture now matters more than the node’s nanometer label. What should we check when comparing CXMT’s move to 4F² with the leading manufacturers?

Expert Check the cell size each company is starting from. Public analyses suggest that CXMT is approaching the next architecture from a relatively larger cell than the leading manufacturers. Moving to 4F² from that point presents a different challenge from changing the structure of a cell that has already been shrunk extensively. The significance of a 4F² transition depends on the starting cell size and density.

Being first to introduce 4F² would therefore not, on its own, establish that a company had overtaken the leaders. You need to look beyond the node name to how far the existing cell has been shrunk and how much further the new structure can take it. The scope for extending 4F² is finite too. Some companies see an economic return within that limited window and others do not. They are also starting that calculation from different cell sizes.

4. 3D DRAM structures, silicon and equipment speed

Damnang How broadly are manufacturers preparing for 3D DRAM? How do you read their public announcements?

Expert Several manufacturers have researched it and presented results at conferences for years. Micron is not alone. Having seen NAND move from 2D to 3D, I think the industry broadly shares the view that DRAM will eventually need to stack cells to increase density. The major question is when it makes sense to switch.

Micron has shown roadmaps that include 3D DRAM in conference keynotes and its October technology webinar. A roadmap presentation is different from a technical session that reports specific device and process results. Samsung and SK hynix have published 3D DRAM research of the latter kind. Micron’s research into ferroelectric NVDRAM is separate from the DRAM we are discussing here.

Micron has said it will extend 6F² for several more generations while preparing 3D DRAM, without taking the intermediate 4F² step. Its roadmap calls for volume production of 1δ in the second half of 2027, wafer bonding at 1ε, and evaluation of High NA EUV and new materials at 1ζ and 1η. It places 3D DRAM between 1ζ and 1η, with development proceeding alongside planar DRAM.

Without 4F² as an intermediate step, there is more reason to prepare 3D in advance. But a position on a roadmap is not a firm production date. Preparing a structure and achieving the yield and cost needed for volume production are different milestones.

Damnang How does 4F² with a vertical channel differ from 3D DRAM? Where do you think the economics of 3D DRAM stand today?

Expert The 6F² and 4F² approaches we are discussing are both 2D DRAM, with cells arranged in a single plane. Orienting the channel vertically is different from stacking memory cells in several layers. 3D DRAM changes the orientation and arrangement of cells so they can be integrated across multiple layers to increase density.

Public industry roadmaps position 3D DRAM as a possible transition around 2030 or later. Micron has also said it will adopt the technology when it makes business sense. I think the view that process costs currently outweigh the density gains is widely shared in the industry. Building the structure appears possible. Making it cheaply enough to beat the existing approach is a separate challenge. If costs rise faster than the number of bits, there is no reason to switch now.

Damnang What becomes particularly difficult at the device level when you try to stack DRAM cells in multiple layers?

Expert It is difficult to achieve the channel quality needed for transistors with low leakage on every layer. DRAM stores information as charge in a capacitor. If that charge leaks away while the transistor is off, the cell cannot retain its state for long. The access transistor connected to the capacitor therefore needs very low leakage.

In the approach using silicon that we are discussing, meeting that requirement depends on a crystalline silicon channel of high quality. Planar DRAM is built on a silicon wafer. In a structure with multiple layers, the upper layers also need channels of the required quality. Adding material to increase the layer count and achieving DRAM’s charge retention requirements on every layer have to be solved together.

Damnang How does achieving the required channel quality on every layer affect process time and cost?

Expert In the approach using single crystal silicon that I am describing, the time needed to grow the crystal adds to the cost. Epitaxy grows new crystal in alignment with an existing crystal, but building up the layers while maintaining quality is slow. Being able to form the required structure and being able to do so at a speed suitable for volume production are separate challenges.

Suppose, for example, that you need to build hundreds of layers. The growth time for each one accumulates across the process. Demonstrating a structure in one or two layers has different economics from building many layers quickly enough. Stacking may increase the number of bits, but if the process costs too much, it will be difficult to manufacture them more cheaply than conventional DRAM.

This also raises a question about what we mean by having the technology. We might mean that the structure has been demonstrated, or that it can be manufactured in volume cheaply and quickly enough. Whatever definition we use, a commercial product has to satisfy both technical feasibility and manufacturing economics.

Damnang What needs to improve for 3D DRAM to become more economical?

Expert We need equipment that can make the same structure faster and at a lower cost. In the approach I described, the problem is how slowly we build up the layers while maintaining the required channel quality. If each layer takes a long time, the full stack takes longer and the equipment produces less in a given period. That leaves fewer units of output to absorb the equipment cost. Faster stacking or less expensive equipment would improve the economics of 3D DRAM.

The basic ideas for the structures are already there. I think the more important task is to improve the speed and cost of making them, rather than expect a completely different structure to appear and solve everything. A company that skips 4F² has even more reason to help drive equipment development for 3D DRAM. The processes and equipment for 3D DRAM differ substantially from those used for 2D DRAM, so memory makers will need to develop new processes with equipment suppliers, much as they did for 3D NAND.

I am optimistic that these problems can be solved, given what the industry has overcome before. When the economics work will depend on how quickly the equipment improves.

5. The benchmarks for assessing China’s progress

Damnang When a company announces that it has developed a new structure, what should we check first to understand how far it has actually progressed?

Expert Start with what the company means by developed. Researching a structure and producing it reliably at high yield are different stages. An announcement can tell you where development is heading without establishing the actual yield or maturity of the process.

Take a hypothetical example. One company might consider development complete only when it can sustain volume production at a yield of around 90%. Another might ship samples and continue production at around 20%. Both can say they have made the device, but they have achieved different things. Those figures illustrate different standards of completion. They are not the actual yields of any company.

An engineer who has spent years getting a process into production at high yield can easily assume that another company’s development announcement means it has met the same standard. The reaction is then to wonder how it got there so quickly. Before making that comparison, we need to establish whether both companies mean the same thing by development being complete.

Damnang What should we compare when looking at the economics of DDR5 production at Samsung and CXMT?

Expert Compare the cost of producing good chips with the same capacity and specifications. Even if wafer processing costs are identical, larger cells and larger chips mean fewer chips per wafer. Lower yield then reduces the number available for sale. Producing DDR5 therefore does not establish that Samsung and CXMT have achieved the same production economics.

Then consider each company’s objectives and the support it receives. If the policy objective is to acquire technology or establish domestic supply, immediate manufacturing cost may not be the only consideration. Subsidies that help cover capital investment or operating costs can allow development and production to continue while manufacturing efficiency is still low.

That support does not eliminate differences in cell area or yield. It changes how long a company can sustain production despite those differences. We need to consider both manufacturing efficiency and the financial burden the company can carry. That is why one company may continue producing at a level that a leading supplier would consider uneconomical.

Damnang I also hear that equipment restrictions pushed China to prepare for 3D DRAM early, and that it might even pull ahead in this area. What would we need to compare to assess that argument?

Expert Compare the density of the 2D DRAM each company produces today and the number of 3D layers it would need to exceed that density. Several companies have spent more than 10 years researching and designing these devices. From what I see, the broad structural concepts and manufacturing approaches have converged to some extent. We can therefore set cost aside for a moment and first compare the number of layers required.

CXMT moving from its current 2D DRAM to 3D starts from a different point than a leading supplier that has pushed 2D as far as it can go. The denser the existing 2D product, the more layers the 3D design needs to surpass it. Building the device becomes harder as the layer count rises.

CXMT could therefore be the first to introduce a 3D DRAM product that outperforms its own existing product. That would not, by itself, establish that its 3D DRAM technology was ahead of Samsung or SK hynix. Improvement from a company’s own starting point and an advantage over the industry leaders are different comparisons.

Damnang Some Chinese analysts I have met doubt that China can catch up quickly because of the technology gap and its reliance on policy support. Yet new structures and technical terms keep appearing in the news. How should we read those development announcements?

Expert We need to distinguish between making a device, putting it into volume production, and catching up. Demonstrating a structure, producing large quantities reliably, and competing at a comparable cost are different achievements.

A succession of headlines about new structures can make a company seem much further ahead than it is, especially when they use striking technical terms. But the terminology tells us nothing about the actual dimensions, yield, or production volume.

It is already difficult to know exactly what another company is doing. Assuming that the name of a structure means it has met the high standard you have in mind adds to the confusion. We need a comparable standard before judging relative technology or competitiveness.

6. EUV restrictions, equipment performance and cost

Damnang My understanding is that China faces restrictions on EUV access, while DUV multipatterning makes the process more complex. How do those equipment differences affect DRAM development and competitiveness?

Expert The effects show up in the number of process steps, precision control, and cost per good device. Some fine patterns that EUV can print in one exposure must be split into several steps with DUV. That can require additional etching to transfer the patterns or additional film deposition, as well as more exposures. Repetition adds process time and more points where alignment and defects need to be controlled. Two processes can produce the same shape at different costs in volume production.

Chinese manufacturers do use ASML equipment. ASML supplies DUV as well as EUV systems. EUV is not supplied to China, and advanced DUV immersion systems are also subject to export licensing restrictions. Access to equipment with lower specifications depends on the applicable rules and the customer and end use. To assess a Chinese manufacturer’s competitiveness, we first need to know which equipment it can actually use for which processes.

A lack of EUV does not immediately make all DRAM development impossible. Companies can continue developing devices while accepting more complex processes and lower productivity. The extra burden can grow as dimensions shrink, though. Demonstrating a prototype and producing it at a cost that competes with the leaders are different achievements. Equipment restrictions are often reflected in the time and cost required to make the same product, rather than a simple division between what is possible and what is not.

Damnang When a company announces that it has developed domestic equipment, how should we assess its technical performance and economics?

Expert First establish whether it meets the process requirements, then consider the equipment price relative to the actual output of good devices. A low purchase price can still lead to a high cost per good device if the equipment is slow, stops frequently, or introduces process variation that reduces yield. Equipment price and the manufacturing cost of its output measure different things.

Performance also takes more than a single headline specification to assess. Can the equipment print patterns as fine as the best systems from global suppliers? Can it consistently meet the requirements of a particular node? How quickly can it repeat the result? Those are separate questions. A good result on one research wafer must be repeated across continuous production before the equipment is suitable for volume manufacturing.

Equipment can still be economical for a particular application without matching the highest performance available. It may meet the needs of the current product, achieve adequate utilization and yield, and cost less. Labor costs and subsidies can further change the burden on the manufacturer. The useful question is what domestic equipment now allows the company to make, and how reliably and cheaply it can make it.

Damnang How could a move to 3D DRAM change the requirements for EUV?

Expert The stacking approach I described could depend less on EUV than continued 2D scaling because it changes how density increases. In 2D, the emphasis is on fitting cells more closely together in the same plane. In 3D, adding cells vertically becomes another option. The same density gain no longer has to come entirely from smaller planar patterns.

That does not mean EUV exposure counts rise or fall in proportion to the number of layers. We need to know which layers require separate patterning, the smallest patterns in the cells and peripheral circuits, and how precisely the layers must align. Semiconductor manufacturing already combines EUV with different types of DUV equipment according to the resolution and cost required for each layer.

If the bottleneck is the speed of silicon channel growth, it belongs to deposition and epitaxy equipment. Better EUV resolution will not directly solve it. Rather than infer EUV demand simply from a transition to 3D DRAM, we need to understand where the burden on lithography falls and where it shifts to other processes. That is also why a new structure that changes lithography requirements is a different problem from using DUV multipatterning to make an existing structure.

7. Capacity expansion, demand and the pace of process transitions

Damnang Some expect oversupply in 2028 or 2029 as companies add substantial capacity and China expands production. Their argument is that China could meet more of its own demand and reinvest current profits, as it has in batteries and displays. How do you see memory supply and demand over the next few years?

Expert Many of the public reports and industry forecasts I see expect demand to remain strong for the next 3 to 4 years, making it difficult for capacity additions alone to produce enough supply. But I also question whether demand will grow enough to keep the market short despite so many new fabs. I would not treat today’s optimistic forecasts as a settled conclusion about 2028 or 2029.

For supply, the important measure is when and by how much output of good bits increases, rather than the number of fabs. Completing the building, installing equipment and starting wafers, and reaching a yield that produces saleable volume happen at different times. Existing fabs are also affected by two changes at once. New nodes increase bits per wafer, while a growing share of products such as HBM consumes more production resources for the same number of bits. Bit supply does not necessarily grow in proportion to capital spending or wafer input.

On the demand side, separate the number of systems sold from the memory capacity installed in each system. If China replaces some imports with domestic production, that changes the demand left for existing suppliers. But substitution requires products that meet the specifications and can actually be supplied in sufficient volume.

The comparison is between how quickly added capacity becomes good bits and how quickly demand grows. Faster yield improvement and capacity expansion could produce oversupply if demand growth slows. If the shift to HBM and more complex processes holds back growth in bit output, supply could remain short even as more fabs are built. We need to see which conditions materialize. Capacity announcements alone do not establish either an eventual surplus or a shortage.

Damnang Do those forecasts follow from existing demand, or do they depend heavily on new applications such as physical AI?

Expert The forecasts I see assume that demand from existing applications stays high even without new demand from physical AI. That seems to be why they expect supply to struggle to keep up despite new fab construction.

Damnang What concerns you most when assessing those optimistic demand forecasts?

Expert The people making the forecasts also participate in the market. NVIDIA, memory manufacturers, and equipment suppliers all have a stake in its growth. Hearing the same argument repeatedly should not replace examining the assumptions behind the numbers. A different forecast from outside the industry deserves consideration if it has evidence behind it.

Technology that reduces the KV cache is a useful example. If we need less memory to store intermediate calculations during a conversation, the memory required for the same task can fall. Lower costs could also encourage more requests to be processed at once or longer contexts, consuming that saving again. The reduction in memory per task and the increase in the total workload are separate variables.

An efficiency improvement alone does not establish that total demand will fall. Invoking Jevons’ paradox does not prove that it will rise, either. We need to see what happens to actual usage and the memory installed per system. If every change is eventually explained as another reason for demand to grow, I think we should ask whether the forecast is really being tested. When everyone uses the same assumption, a change in that assumption can shift expectations all at once.

Damnang How could the current boom affect the pace of node transitions and China’s ability to catch up?

Expert When selling more of the current product is attractive, a manufacturer can delay moving volume production to a new node. Output temporarily falls during the transition. That loss matters more when everything a company makes can be sold, as it can today, which creates a reason to delay. Public reports suggest Samsung is adjusting the pace of its next node transition to protect the profitability of its existing lines. Companies make different choices, though. Micron has outlined plans for volume production on its next node in the second half of 2027.

Development can continue while the production transition waits. If the leaders slow that transition and Chinese manufacturers keep developing their next nodes, the latter may narrow the gap in the meantime.

Chinese manufacturers could also respond to the same boom by prioritizing sales of current products. That would affect how quickly they catch up. The boom therefore does not automatically narrow the technology gap. The outcome depends on how each company balances development with the timing of its production transitions.

8. How HBM changes process choices and memory economics

Damnang How do memory makers choose a DRAM process for HBM?

Expert For HBM, a major consideration is how much customers will pay for the performance a new process delivers. Commodity DRAM has largely been about producing the same bits at a lower cost. With HBM, better performance can command a higher price. That gives manufacturers a reason to adopt a new process earlier if the return is enough to cover the burden of initial yields and manufacturing costs.

The usual sequence was to introduce a process through DDR products, improve yields as those products shipped, then apply it to HBM dies and stabilize production again. That interval is getting shorter. From what has been disclosed, Samsung’s approach to 1c looks closer to reversing the sequence and stabilizing HBM first.

Existing processes can support commodity DDR supply while the new process focuses on the performance and yield HBM needs. Even within one company, different product groups can follow different sequences for process adoption and production ramp. That is why I look at DDR and HBM separately, even from a wafer fabrication perspective.

Damnang How do those process choices affect competition among HBM suppliers?

Expert Suppliers compete to meet the speed and power requirements of the next HBM generation, and process choice affects who can get there first. Micron has said it will use 1γ for HBM4E, while Samsung introduced 1c DRAM and a 4nm logic base die with HBM4. Samsung said it chose advanced processes over reusing an established design to leave room for customers to request higher performance. Suppliers can choose different amounts of process and design headroom within the same product generation. I also saw Samsung’s choice as an attempt to offer performance suited to the next generation earlier.

A DRAM node name alone does not establish an advantage. The core dies containing the memory cells, the base die handling signals, the vertical connections, and the package all have to meet the performance target. A better core die does not translate into a better product if power, heat, or signal quality at the connections prevents it from reaching the required speed.

This competition can shorten the interval between stabilizing a process on DDR and applying it to HBM. If customers pay enough for higher performance to offset the initial yield and cost burden, adopting the process sooner can make sense. A fair comparison goes beyond the announced peak speed. Can the product sustain that speed within the customer’s power and temperature limits, and when can the supplier deliver the required volume?

Damnang How does process development begin for the next HBM generation?

Expert It starts before the final product specifications are settled, using test structures built around expected operating conditions. For a DRAM cell, developers can check how long it retains charge, how much leaks through the access transistor, and whether reads and writes work under the target conditions. Those results guide changes to device dimensions and process conditions.

HBM adds connections between dies and the requirements of stacking them. Developers assume targets for data rate, power, stack height, and connection pitch, then build wiring and bonding structures to check resistance, connection failures, and behavior as temperature changes. Testing expands from an individual cell or connection to whether the assembled stack maintains performance and reliability.

Developing a process for the next generation does not mean the final product specifications and yields are already settled. The device and interconnect technologies still have to go into the final design, and the complete product must be validated under the conditions customers will use. Process development, product sampling, customer qualification, and volume production each establish something different.

Damnang How should we think about HBM in relation to the price competition and cycles of commodity DRAM?

Expert Commodity DRAM competition is heavily driven by cost. Manufacturers try to supply it more cheaply, but they cannot adjust output immediately when demand changes. That is part of what creates the memory cycle.

HBM has given suppliers a way to charge more for performance. It introduces a consideration beyond moving to a new process simply to lower the cost per bit. How long customers continue paying that performance premium matters.

If the premium for additional performance weakens, suppliers may have less reason to rush into the next process even while demand for HBM remains. Customers can need HBM without being willing to pay much more for HBM made on a newer process.

9. DRAM on logic and new memory tiers

Damnang What advantages could come from stacking DRAM directly on logic? How would that fit alongside conventional HBM?

Expert The aim is to make the connections between logic and DRAM shorter and denser. Conventional HBM generally sits beside the processor. Putting DRAM above the logic allows many connections to run vertically, with the goal of increasing bandwidth while reducing the energy used to move data. Samsung presented zHBM as a concept that places HBM directly above an AI accelerator. I also see Micron’s proposed vHBM as part of this move toward vertical connections between logic and memory.

These designs can serve different purposes. One changes the location and connections of existing HBM. Another creates a new memory tier between SRAM and conventional HBM to keep some data closer to the processor. Placing DRAM on logic does not by itself create a new tier or eliminate existing HBM. It is also different from the 3D DRAM cell architecture we discussed earlier, which builds the cell arrays themselves across multiple layers.

As a new memory tier, it could keep frequently used data nearby and reduce traffic to memory farther away. Shorter connections do not make a DRAM cell behave like SRAM, though. Some data needs very low access latency, while other data needs more storage capacity. Different memories still have roles to fill.

Heat is another challenge. With memory above active logic, the path for heat to escape and the memory’s operating temperature have to be designed together. Bonding precision and yield after stacking also have to be sufficient. The bandwidth gain has to justify the cooling and manufacturing costs for the design to make sense in a product.

Damnang How could a new memory tier affect DRAM production and equipment demand?

Expert If separately manufactured DRAM dies take over some of SRAM’s role while the existing HBM capacity stays in place, the system adds DRAM bits. If conventional HBM is replaced by the same capacity of DRAM on logic, the location changes without automatically increasing total DRAM capacity. The production impact depends on whether the design adds capacity or provides existing capacity in a different way.

Then there is the number of wafers needed to make those bits. You can think of the DRAM wafer requirement as the target bit shipments divided by the good bits ultimately obtained from each wafer. That calculation includes cell scaling, the area occupied by peripheral circuits and connections, wafer fabrication yield, and losses during stacking. Micron’s comparison is a useful example. It said HBM3E uses about three times as many wafers as DDR5 to produce the same number of bits on the same node. Equal DRAM capacity can place very different demands on production resources. That factor of three cannot be applied to every other form of stacked DRAM.

For equipment, wafer fabrication and stacking need to be considered separately. If a design bonds dies made on an existing DRAM process onto logic, the first additional demands may be precise bonding, surface planarization, thinning, and inspection. Where individual dies are selected for bonding, the ability to choose good dies and limit connection failures after bonding becomes important. Increasing DRAM wafer input or moving to a smaller node also adds to the demands on lithography, etch, deposition, and other fabrication processes.

Connecting this to demand for ASML lithography systems requires another step. How much does DRAM wafer input increase? How many DUV and EUV exposures does the process require, and can the installed tools handle that workload? Stacking several DRAM dies does not mean the EUV exposures per wafer rise in proportion to the number of dies in the stack. If existing fabs have no spare capacity, wafers assigned to the new product reduce the capacity available for DDR or conventional HBM. Equipment requirements become more concrete when manufacturers address that shortfall through node transitions or capacity expansion.

Damnang’s perspective · Wafer allocation and product mix

This allocation of production resources is also what I have been trying to understand about HBM. Even if it takes more wafers to produce the same capacity, expanding HBM can make sense when its price more than compensates for that burden. If commodity DRAM prices rise, producing more of those existing products can become more attractive too.

DRAM stacked directly on logic adds another choice. We need to consider how limited manufacturing resources are allocated across products, as well as which products are seeing stronger demand. Increasing HBM’s share of production and maximizing profit across the memory business are not necessarily the same decision.

10. Competing with a memory technology that keeps improving

Damnang I have also been looking at approaches that use extensive SRAM across a wafer, as Cerebras does, and designs that use LPDDR. How much of HBM’s role could these take over?

Expert They address different demands currently placed on HBM. Putting a large amount of SRAM near the compute units, as Cerebras does, can reduce bottlenecks in moving data to and from external memory. But models and data that exceed the SRAM capacity still have to be placed and supplied somewhere in the system. Cerebras describes external memory and connections among multiple systems as well as the SRAM on its chip.

LPDDR offers a way to add memory capacity at relatively low power. Instead of keeping everything in HBM, a system can keep the working data that needs high bandwidth there and place the rest in LPDDR or DDR. The attraction diminishes if the performance lost while moving data between tiers outweighs the cost and power savings. Reducing HBM use for a particular workload is a much narrower claim than replacing it across all applications.

HBM also keeps improving while alternatives are being developed. Growing demand attracts investment, and greater production scale and process improvements create room to lower costs. A new technology may look better than today’s HBM, only to reach the market after HBM has improved its bandwidth, power efficiency, and manufacturing cost.

We have seen a similar problem when considering MRAM or FeRAM as replacements for DRAM. Existing DRAM keeps advancing while the new memory is being developed. A technology can have useful applications without being able to replace a large existing memory market economically.

11. Evaluating EUV across the whole fab

Damnang What do memory makers consider when deciding whether to adopt EUV?

Expert The starting point is whether EUV lowers the total cost of producing good bits. An expensive lithography system can make economic sense if it eliminates repeated patterning and related steps, saving time and reducing the equipment needed. Better resolution brings less benefit if it removes too few steps or costs too much to stabilize yield.

The value of output also matters. When supply is ample, the priority is making the same bits more cheaply. When cleanroom space is scarce and new fabs take a long time to build, producing more good output within existing space becomes more valuable. Micron has also described cleanroom shortages and longer construction times as supply constraints. Having the money to order a tool does not mean there is space to install and run it.

Under those conditions, a choice that looks unattractive when judged on a few process costs may be worthwhile if it increases output across the fab. EUV does not automatically raise production, however. The steps it removes must be examined to see whether they were limiting output and whether the other processes have capacity available. Tool price, process cost, and good output belong in the same calculation.

Damnang How does reducing process steps affect fab space and output?

Expert Think of a bakery with five steps from mixing dough to finished bread, each using machinery inside a limited building. If a few intermediate steps can be eliminated, they no longer need that time or space. Putting other necessary production equipment in the freed space could allow the same bakery to make more bread.

In semiconductor manufacturing, reducing repeated steps used to form a particular pattern can shorten both the route a wafer takes and the total processing time. It changes the equipment required and how space can be used. That is why the wafers per hour of a single lithography tool are not enough to judge the result. The steps before and after exposure that complete the pattern count too.

But if the oven remains the bakery’s bottleneck, removing dough mixers will not immediately produce more finished bread. The same applies to a fab. Eliminating or shortening a bottleneck can have a large effect on output. If another etch, deposition, or inspection step remains constrained, the production gain is limited. A percentage reduction in process steps does not imply the same percentage increase in output.

Saving money, shortening the time a wafer spends in the fab, and shipping more good products are related but different results. When supply is tight, that last result becomes more valuable. When price competition intensifies, cost savings carry more weight.

Damnang What about High NA EUV? What determines whether it is worth adopting?

Expert High NA makes sense when its ability to print finer features simplifies the manufacturing process enough to outweigh the cost of buying and operating the equipment. If today’s EUV process divides a pattern across multiple exposures, a useful starting question is whether High NA can print it in one.

Examples have been presented for selected logic and DRAM patterns where 1 High NA EUV exposure replaces 3 or 4 Low NA EUV exposures. Reducing those separate exposures can also remove some of the related processing between them. That shortens process time and removes opportunities for errors and defects introduced by the repeated steps.

Four exposures becoming one for a particular pattern does not make a fab produce four times as much. The calculation depends on how many such patterns the actual product uses, the throughput and availability of the High NA tools, and whether other processes remain bottlenecks. Resists, masks, metrology, and inspection must also meet production requirements before a simpler process translates into more good output.

The timing of adoption changes the value of those gains. When supply is scarce, producing more within the same space matters more. When supply is sufficient, the reduction in cost per bit takes priority. Improved resolution needs to come with a concrete account of the process steps that can be removed and the costs that can be recovered on that node.

12. Changing demand and the equipment roadmap

Damnang What makes it difficult to respond to customers’ equipment needs?

Expert Customers can change their requirements quickly, while equipment takes a long time to prepare. Demand assessments can change from one quarter to the next.

Advanced equipment such as EUV systems can take more than a year from order to delivery. Once a production plan is underway, a sudden change in the quantity or type of equipment needed is hard to accommodate immediately.

The signals of strong demand are clear. But when assessments change this quickly, I also have to ask whether the next assessment will hold. Changes in the markets we serve can alter the assumptions behind plans made years ahead. We have to respond to today’s strong demand while allowing for the possibility that those assumptions change again.

Damnang How do changes in AI models feed through to demand for memory and manufacturing equipment?

Expert The first question is whether the change requires more capacity, more bandwidth, or both. A system that lacks space to hold a model needs a different response from one that cannot read the data quickly enough. That affects the choice of memory and the system around it.

For example, inference with a long context needs space for the model weights and a growing KV cache that retains computation information from earlier tokens. With the same model and storage format, longer contexts or more concurrent requests require more cache space. Compression and better cache management can reduce memory per request. A service operator can use that saving to reduce the hardware required, or to serve more requests and longer contexts on the same hardware.

The storage location can also change. Data needed for immediate computation can stay close to the GPU, while some KV cache waiting to be reused moves to CPU memory or SSDs. That reduces pressure on GPU memory without making all the stored data disappear. It changes the roles of HBM, commodity DRAM, and NAND, and the mix of demand across them.

For an equipment roadmap, these changes have to be translated into the bits required for each product, then into the good bits per wafer on the relevant process. A model’s percentage reduction in memory use cannot simply be treated as the same percentage decline in DRAM demand. Usage, concurrency, and memory placement all affect which products need to be manufactured and in what quantities.

Damnang What is difficult about turning device and process requirements into an equipment development plan?

Expert Device requirements have to become specific targets that an equipment team can develop against. Saying that the next DRAM needs to be smaller is not enough to design a tool. We need to specify which patterns must shrink and by how much, the acceptable alignment error between layers, and how consistently the process must reproduce the result across a wafer.

As connections inside a cell get smaller, for example, alignment between layers becomes important alongside linewidth. Printing a good pattern is only part of the job. Its actual position and shape have to be measured, and those measurements fed back into tool control. Hardware, sensors, and control and correction software have to improve together.

Throughput and cost add further requirements. Demonstrating a very precise result on one wafer differs from repeating it on many wafers every hour. Device developers find process conditions that meet the performance target. Equipment developers have to maintain those conditions at the speed and availability needed for volume production.

The difficult part is setting those targets before the future cell structure and product volumes are settled. Precision and productivity requirements shared by different structures can be prepared in advance. Features needed only for a particular structure have to be developed according to the scenarios in which it might be adopted. Much of the job is turning what devices and processes could require into equipment performance targets and dates by which the tools need to be ready.

Damnang What should we be careful about when judging the longer term direction amid that uncertainty?

Expert A widely repeated forecast is still a forecast. Even people who have researched device structures and fabricated wafers choose different paths. Rapid changes in systems and AI models add more uncertainty.

People inside the industry also have a stake in its growth. Hearing similar forecasts repeatedly is not a reason to feel assured. If an opposing explanation has evidence behind it, I think we should consider it too.

Closing thoughts

The calculation differs from company to company. We kept returning to that point. Companies approaching 4F² start with different cell sizes and cost structures. Those developing 3D DRAM also face different density targets set by their existing products. Treating a research result, a product launch and volume production at high yield as equivalent achievements distorts the comparison.

HBM adds the value of performance to that calculation. If customers pay for better performance, a manufacturer has a reason to adopt an advanced process earlier. Any technology seeking to replace HBM must also compete with the investment and improvements that keep HBM moving forward. Stacking memory above logic could change both the roles of different memories and the allocation of wafers.

EUV raises questions about process time, fab space and output alongside resolution and equipment price. Strong demand changes the relative value of producing more and producing at a lower cost. Yet customers can revise their plans faster than equipment suppliers can respond. Assessing a technology’s future means understanding the conditions that would make it worth adopting, and how those conditions could change.
