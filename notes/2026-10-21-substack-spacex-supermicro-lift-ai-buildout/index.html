原标题：SpaceX, Supermicro and this morning’s AI buildout

#hardware #ai-demand

Substack · 2026-10-21

$738m.

That’s roughly how much extra quarterly gross profit Supermicro’s preliminary update last night implies compared with the old guide.

At the previous $15bn revenue midpoint and 10.6% gross-margin midpoint, the guide implied about $1.59B of gross profit. At the new $19bn revenue midpoint and 12.25% margin, it implies about $2.33B.

Revenue up 27%. Gross profit up 46%.

With SpaceX also reporting a near-tripling of AI revenue QoQ and a move into operating profit, these two gave the AI trade some needed momentum. We have equipment being delivered at better margins and deployed compute generating much more revenue.

I think that is a lot more exciting than another announcement about how much somebody intends to spend.

Supermicro and SpaceX

I’ve never spoken about SpaceX before so let me start with a quick overview that is relevant to this discussion. Since acquiring xAI in February, SpaceX has a much bigger business than rockets and Starlink. It owns AI clusters that support its own products, including Grok, and rents capacity to other AI developers. Anthropic uses that capacity for Claude. Google is another customer. SpaceX supplies the GPUs and the power, cooling and networking needed to run them.

So when somebody uses Claude, some of that demand can generate revenue for SpaceX even though Claude competes with Grok. That gives SpaceX another way to earn from AI adoption alongside its own models.

At SpaceX, Super Intelligence (SI, what some people are calling ‘AI” now) revenue reached $7.56bn, up around 195% QoQ, with $431m of operating profit versus a $1.26bn loss in Q2. Cursor contributed $608m after its August acquisition. That leaves most of the roughly $5bn sequential increase coming from elsewhere, with management pointing primarily to new cloud agreements.

On the call, they said short-term compute rentals are renewing at higher prices per watt and described current deployment paybacks below a year. More capacity is coming online into a market where customers are paying more for it.

With Q1 2027 total-company revenue guided to $33bn–$37bn, the next question is how quickly SpaceX can build enough capacity to support the growth.

A huge part of the funding came from June’s IPO, which raised about $85.7bn net. By September 30, SpaceX had roughly $89.9bn in cash and marketable securities. That helps explain how it can plan $35bn–$40bn of Q4 capex after spending $19.59bn in Q3. Investors have already put up a substantial amount of the money.

Beyond that next phase, management is discussing debt and vendor or customer partnerships alongside revenue coming in from the existing businesses. At this pace of construction, the timing of those arrangements becomes very important. The current reserve gives SpaceX room to build, while getting customers onto the new capacity brings in the cash for subsequent expansion.

For Q4, the CFO expects group gross margins nearer Q2 levels after favorable Q3 one-offs and customer mix. The revenue ramp and the spending ramp both get bigger from here, with a different margin mix than the quarter just reported.

Will be interesting to see how this all plays out. We know Elon has a history of big ambitions that sometimes don’t follow through. Have to keep that with a grain of salt with the $500B-$1T revenue discussion. But for now, it’s nice that it ignited momentum in the trade again and demonstrated that compute is scare, when there are some discussions stating the opposite view.

Compute rentals and NBIS

When Elon said older H100s were renting for more than when they were new, I think that’s worth talking about. One of the big worries with this buildout is that you spend a fortune on GPUs and a newer generation arrives before you’ve earned the money back. If the older equipment can still command strong rents, that changes how we should think about its earning life.

He also said SpaceX could rent its capacity several times over if it accepted all the demand coming in. And when SpaceX tried moving some inference to outside providers to free its own machines for training, it struggled to find capacity there too. So it is seeing the shortage from both sides.

Let’s talk about the software point for a sec. If a GPU-hour produces more useful work, the customer can pay more for that hour and still pay less for each task. Cheaper AI does not automatically mean cheaper GPU rentals. It depends on how much additional work customers want to run as the products improve.

That helps explain why Elon wants shorter rental contracts. He expects to charge more when they renew. Locking in a long agreement today would give up some of that opportunity.

For NBIS, we already have a very similar decision. Nebius reported older-generation GPU pricing more than 30% higher in Q2 than Q1. Management also said it was deliberately keeping some 2027 capacity available for immediate customer needs, rather than selling everything ahead of time.

Nebius put annual contract value for its large Q2 deals at $20m–$25m per MW, then described a $40m–$50m opportunity in short-term capacity. Those are different contract types, but the premium helps explain why it would leave some capacity available. Long contracts help finance construction and shorter contracts leave room to earn more from customers who need working compute now.

At this morning’s GTC Berlin pregame, Volozh described demand and pricing as strong and discussed using auctions to match available capacity with demand, according to published transcriptions of the clips. Nebius also launched public live spot prices today, alongside short-term reserve auctions and a pilot that lets customers return idle GPUs for a rebate. That makes its pricing strategy much more visible.

For me, the attraction is the capacity Nebius has left available to capture those prices as it comes online.

Anthropic and that $750bn

$750bn of potential computing spending over the coming years. Some of those deals are still being negotiated, according to WSJ’s draft-prospectus report. Even spread over several years, though, that is an enormous amount of money.

My first question was how Anthropic pays for it.

With $95bn raised this year and a reported IPO target of up to $100bn, it is pursuing nearly $200bn of equity funding across those raises. The IPO alone could almost match everything raised earlier this year. That gives us a much clearer sense of why the listing is so important to the expansion plan.

Through Broadcom, Anthropic could also access up to $42bn of conditional financing toward a $125.2bn five-year TPU lease. It did not expect to draw that financing before the IPO. The supplier would be helping finance the customer’s capacity purchases. Anthropic would still owe the money, but it would have another way to fund the equipment access while the business grows.

And the business it expects to have is much bigger. Reported Q3 revenue reached $16.8bn, more than the entire first half’s $16.3bn. Reuters also reported a management projection of $190bn–$200bn of annual revenue in 2028.

The logic is to raise capital to secure the capacity, put that capacity to work, then use growing customer receipts to carry more of the later bills. The difficult part is getting the timing to line up. Some contracts require payment regardless of usage, so a slower revenue ramp would leave more of the spending dependent on another raise.

I’d also look at the IPO through the suppliers’ eyes. A successful $100bn raise would strengthen the customer behind a lot of their expansion plans. That could support the infrastructure trade well beyond Anthropic’s own listing.

Getting more compute into service

After Musk’s comments about factory-integrated inference clusters, NVIDIA announced DSX POD this morning. Factory-built containers that arrive with the systems integrated and tested. NVIDIA says deployment at a prepared site can fall from roughly six months to as little as 14 days, with initial partner deliveries expected in early 2027.

Fourteen days. The site still needs to be ready, but I can see why operators would want as much work as possible done before the equipment arrives. If customers are waiting to rent the compute, months spent assembling and testing it are months without that revenue.

I wrote about this in my September infrastructure article, including Lektra, Penguin and on-site power.

When we this about this general market for Penguin I’m thinking about the operator who wants several smaller sites but doesn’t want to build a separate technical team and operating process for each one.

Penguin’s September Lektra announcement covers distributed micro data centers. Its October 14 Axe Compute agreement includes deployment, ClusterWare AI software and 24/7 operating support at specified sites. Axe owns the infrastructure and Penguin supplies the capability to get it running and keep it running.

Once that setup works, more of it can be reused at the next location. I like the possibility of one customer opening several sites and needing Penguin across them.

For CAT, the separate problem is electricity. A finished container is still useless if it cannot be powered. AIP’s January order for 2 GW of continuous-duty natural-gas generators is one example of operators arranging their own supply, with deliveries scheduled from September 2026 through August 2027 and batteries handling the AI load swings. When we think about servicing, equipment running continuously gets used very differently from a backup generator waiting for an outage. More operating hours should mean more parts and maintenance demand after the original sale.

With optics, I’d want to know how the work is split between the sites. A smaller cluster keeping its models and data nearby has different networking needs from a system constantly moving work between locations. The ideal setup for optical would be to connect a bunch of these different pods up together, creating demand for more interconnect across sites.

Further inside the machines, Amkor’s interposers help connect the chips in advanced packages. This morning’s NVIDIA supply-chain announcement included Amkor’s target to expand that South Korean wafer capacity by more than 300% by the end of 2028. SPIL also targets fifteen times its co-packaged-optics engine capacity by mid-2027.

If packaging capacity is limiting how many systems NVIDIA can ship, expanding it gives Amkor a route to more production work as those systems ramp.

Google and OpenAI

With OpenAI’s frontier models and Codex coming to Google Cloud, enterprise customers will be able to buy them through its Marketplace using existing GCP spending commitments. Availability is planned in the coming weeks.

This changes the purchasing decision. If I’m a business that has already committed a budget to Google Cloud, I can choose OpenAI’s models and still use that commitment. I don’t have to take the purchase elsewhere just because I prefer that model for a particular job.

For Google, that keeps the customer relationship and purchasing activity inside its cloud. Gemini doesn’t have to win every comparison for Google to participate in the spending. That flexibility is one part of what interests me about the hyperscalers.
