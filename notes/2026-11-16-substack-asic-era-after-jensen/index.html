1. Why NVIDIA’s customers are building their own chips

The reason large NVIDIA customers are investing in custom chips is straightforward. They want to lower the compute cost of their AI services.

NVIDIA reported a companywide gross margin of 75% for the quarter ended July 26, 2026. Its offering combines CUDA, system integration and access to supply, allowing it to maintain high profitability.

That is not necessarily welcome news for customers that already run their own models, software and data centers. Wherever possible, they want to bring work in house and retain some of the margin that currently goes to NVIDIA.

Custom chips can also reduce operating expenses, not just purchase costs. After buying GPUs, customers continue paying for power, cooling and maintenance. When service usage exceeds the capacity of their existing equipment, they need more servers. As services grow, the savings from handling the same work with fewer systems and less power become larger. Designing the chip and software together can therefore reduce both equipment spending and operating costs.

Large companies running their own models and AI services have another reason to design custom chips. They understand their most common workloads better than anyone else. That allows them to optimize compute units, memory configurations and data movement around those workloads, reducing power consumption in the process. Comparing NVIDIA’s GPU architecture with Google’s TPU makes this easier to understand.

NVIDIA GPUs combine general compute units with Tensor Cores for matrix operations, allowing them to handle a wide range of tasks. Google TPUs arrange compute units in arrays optimized for the repeated matrix calculations used in AI. Data moves between neighboring units and is reused within the array, instead of being fetched repeatedly from memory. This reduces the energy spent moving data. By matching the architecture to their own models, customers can perform more work within the same power budget.

This is why we call these chips ASICs, or application specific integrated circuits.

Conceptual comparison of GPU and TPU design priorities. GPUs also contain dedicated matrix units, while TPUs also contain vector and scalar units. Actual performance and cost depend on the model, software optimization and system configuration.

The tradeoff is that a design optimized for particular calculations supports a narrower range of operations and execution patterns. Workloads that do not fit the architecture may fail to deliver the expected efficiency. The most economical starting point is therefore often to optimize established models with large, recurring usage for the custom chip, rather than moving every new model onto it immediately.

For cloud providers, a custom chip is both a way to reduce internal costs and a product they can sell to external customers. Google earns usage revenue from installing and operating TPUs, but it is also expanding into supplying TPU systems directly to customers’ data centers. Customers can operate those systems in their own facilities or in contracted data centers.

This lets Google expand the TPU business without building a corresponding amount of data center space and power infrastructure itself. Put simply, Google is moving into business territory that NVIDIA already occupies.

I have been discussing these ASIC business models and their implications for NVIDIA’s influence with industry professionals for some time.

Most expected this trend to accelerate as AI compute spread across more inference workloads and agent services such as workflow automation attracted sufficient demand. The market is moving in that direction, and many industry specialists expect XPUs to keep gaining share relative to GPUs. In this article, XPU refers collectively to AI accelerators other than GPUs.

2. Why custom chip developers still need ASIC suppliers

If customers are designing custom chips to save money, why do they pay ASIC design companies to build them? Why not handle all of the design work themselves?

The most obvious answer, unfortunately, is that their own chip design capabilities still fall well short of what is required. Google, AWS and Meta began as software companies. They have far less experience in hardware design than established fabless semiconductor companies, and that gap cannot be closed overnight.

What exactly does chip design capability mean here?

Suppose Google defines the compute architecture of its next TPU. Turning that architecture into working silicon requires more than designing the compute blocks.

The team must implement and validate the circuits connecting the TPU to memory, the links between chips, physical placement and routing, and the package itself. Google continues working with ASIC suppliers such as Broadcom despite having its own TPU design team because Broadcom brings decades of accumulated PHY and SerDes IP, manufacturing and packaging work with foundries such as TSMC, and experience in volume production.

Those assets are difficult to replace. Working with an ASIC supplier can be cheaper in several respects than recreating everything internally, which is why these relationships persist.

The main development roles of Google and Broadcom mapped onto Google’s publicly disclosed TPU 8i architecture. Blue represents Google’s compute and system design. Orange represents Broadcom’s high speed IP, physical implementation, packaging and production capabilities. Broadcom’s physical implementation work extends across the chip, including the compute blocks.

That naturally raises another question.

If customers build custom chips to replace NVIDIA GPUs and save money, does paying an ASIC supplier simply leave them facing the same problem of additional external costs?

Building a custom chip does not eliminate payments to outside companies. But it can still make economic sense if total costs, including the ASIC supplier’s fees, are lower than buying NVIDIA GPUs to operate the same service.

When customers own the compute architecture and software while purchasing the necessary IP, implementation and production services, they replace part of the product and system that NVIDIA would otherwise supply.

They can also optimize chip and memory configurations for their services, reducing the equipment and power required. At sufficient volume, development costs are spread across more chips. The ASIC supplier still earns a profit, but the customer aims to save more on equipment and operations than it pays for those services.

At the same time, hyperscalers are diversifying their partners to reduce the fees involved in developing custom chips. Google, for example, maintains future TPU development arrangements with Broadcom and MediaTek. I am also hearing about plans to use Marvell and AMD for programs beyond TPU 10 around 2030. Adding suppliers is a way to spread supply risk while improving the customer’s ability to negotiate ASIC costs.

3. Could Jensen’s absence accelerate the shift to ASICs?

There is a common thread in the customer strategies discussed so far. They are building custom chips to reduce what they spend on NVIDIA GPUs, then expanding the design and procurement work they handle themselves within those programs. The next question is how they will divide the budget for their next expansion between NVIDIA GPUs and custom chips.

Reports that Jensen Huang is considering a return are emerging as I write, so the outcome remains uncertain. Nevertheless, following his resignation, I believe this event could give customers an additional reason to accelerate the validation and procurement of alternatives they have already been developing.

Customers making long term infrastructure commitments to NVIDIA may reassess whether product development and supply expansion will continue at the same pace without Jensen’s leadership. If it takes time for the next management team’s investment priorities and contracting approach to become clear, customers may see accelerating existing ASIC programs and deployment validation as a way to reduce risk. Reducing dependence on one supplier could become an additional motivation for ASIC projects that were originally driven by cost savings.

Validating alternatives and negotiating supply take time. Starting earlier can give customers more options when comparing GPUs and ASICs for their next expansion. This is a conceptual illustration, not a forecast of actual timing or volume.

Those alternatives can also affect negotiations. If customers can operate services reliably on other chips and obtain the volume they need, they can compare NVIDIA’s prices and supply terms against credible options. They may keep buying GPUs where NVIDIA offers the better terms, while allocating additional demand to ASICs for workloads with a more attractive total cost and delivery schedule. If Jensen’s absence continues, I will be watching whether customers give validation and supply commitments greater priority, and whether that eventually changes actual new orders.

As discussed in Section 4, industry professionals expect OpenAI and Anthropic to increase their ASIC demand. That includes developing their own chips, but they also expect TPU orders to rise substantially. As the TPU customer base expands, external requirements are increasingly influencing product design. I am hearing that customer requests also drove the memory expansion in the recently announced TPU 8i Max. The product combines two TPU 8i chips in one package, doubling both SRAM capacity and HBM bandwidth. It targets workloads with demanding memory requirements, such as agents processing long contexts. I expect external customers’ models and services to have a growing influence on TPU configurations alongside Gemini.

Distribution is also expanding beyond Google Cloud. Supplying TPU systems to customers’ data centers, or selling through providers such as Oracle, which plans to deploy TPUs from 2027, allows Google to meet external demand without building all of that data center capacity itself. Existing TPU generations can continue selling to external customers even after Google moves its internal workloads to a newer generation. As system sales and repeat orders grow, I expect the TPU business to expand beyond the limits of Google’s own infrastructure investment.

Higher external sales volume also increases the incentive to lower supply costs. I view Google’s efforts to expand its internal design role and divide orders between Broadcom and MediaTek in that context. Ming Chi Kuo expects MediaTek to be the sole design partner for v9 and v10, but the industry accounts I hear suggest that the division of responsibilities is still being discussed. Given Broadcom’s implementation and production experience across multiple generations, maintaining existing supply while introducing competition on future products looks more realistic. Google could use the resulting cost savings to lower TPU prices and attract more customers, or retain more profit at the same selling price.

Meta uses GPUs, TPUs and MTIA to keep suppliers competing

Meta has said it is using Google TPUs for production inference, alongside NVIDIA and AMD GPUs and its own MTIA. According to industry accounts, Meta outsources much of the implementation beyond its core compute design to design houses, third parties and Broadcom.

My view is that Meta is focused on using external capabilities to bring MTIA supply forward and reduce the cost of recommendations, advertising and Muse based AI services as quickly as possible, rather than owning every design asset itself. For a company that already has extensive data for AI and an established social media business, that may be one of the smartest choices available in the AI era.

AWS turns Trainium’s cost advantage into more cloud customers

Google is exactly the company AWS is aiming to emulate. AWS wants to use Trainium to lower the cost of AI compute, then use that price advantage to attract more customer training and inference workloads and grow cloud revenue and profit. But from what I hear in the industry, AWS still appears to have substantial ground to make up relative to Google’s TPU development capabilities.

There is also a difference in model development strategy. The assessment I hear is that Amazon has not achieved the results it hoped for in the frontier model race. Its internal AI organization is reportedly putting more emphasis on general purpose AI for Amazon services than on AI for research and coding. I also hear about development of models tailored to agents that carry out everyday tasks, similar to Meta’s Muse.

Another interesting account is that Amazon is preparing to launch its own physical AI product within the next three years. I expect AWS to develop its ASIC capabilities alongside those efforts, supporting the training and inference needed for these services and products. Meeting service launch schedules may also require using outside technology to reduce development cost and time, rather than building every interconnect and memory technology independently.

I view the collaboration with NVIDIA as one such technical and economic compromise. AWS is integrating NVLink Fusion into Trainium4 and pursuing NVHBM collaboration with NVIDIA, while retaining Nitro for cloud operations and EFA for connectivity between systems. I see this as a way to reduce interconnect and memory development time and bring Trainium systems to customers faster. It also leaves AWS dependent on parts of NVIDIA’s technology and roadmap. Trainium’s expansion therefore creates an opportunity to replace NVIDIA GPUs while also providing a route for NVIDIA to retain business in connectivity and memory.

OpenAI and Anthropic

OpenAI is securing NVIDIA and AMD GPUs while developing Jalapeño with Broadcom. I am hearing rumors that silicon bringup could be completed by the end of this year. Its goal is to lower the cost of recurring workloads with its own ASIC while expanding compute capacity through GPUs and external ASICs, mainly TPUs, in the meantime.

Anthropic is also expanding its compute capacity through TPUs and Trainium, while recent industry accounts suggest it is hiring silicon engineers aggressively. Several experienced engineers I know have moved to that organization. There are also rumors that it is already designing a chip with major ASIC suppliers such as Broadcom.

6. Broadcom expands connectivity while MediaTek grows TPU volume

Broadcom’s strengths are sometimes misunderstood. They extend beyond ASIC design to its own optical technologies and the connections between accelerators. Its portfolio spans SerDes for high speed chip I/O, Ethernet switches linking accelerators, DSPs that process optical signals, lasers and CPO. When multiple chips work together on one model, time spent waiting for data and power consumed by communication also affect overall system efficiency. Broadcom can help define that connectivity when implementing a customer ASIC, then supply switches and optical components around it.

I view NVIDIA’s investments of $2 billion each in Lumentum, Coherent and Marvell this March through the same lens. NVIDIA is adding Lumentum’s and Coherent’s light source technology and production capacity to its existing NVLink, Spectrum X and CPO offerings, while using Marvell to bring customer ASICs into its connectivity platform. In my view, NVIDIA is using investment and partnerships to complement its capabilities in areas where Broadcom already has a broad optical component and custom silicon portfolio. Competition is expanding from who makes the compute chip to who supplies the technologies and components connecting those chips.

Broadcom’s customer ASIC implementation, Ethernet links between accelerators, optical links between racks and optical component supply. Pluggable optical modules and CPO are separate implementation approaches.

That is why Broadcom should not be viewed solely through TPU development. When customers beyond Google expand custom ASIC programs, Broadcom has opportunities to supply both design and implementation services and connectivity silicon. Industry accounts suggest that APD, the division responsible for its ASIC development, has increased hiring by tens of percent this year as customer programs and development work expand rapidly. I also hear about major customers and scopes of work that have not yet been disclosed, along with additional contracts under discussion. I see these accounts as signs that other customer programs are expanding alongside existing TPU volume.

The company’s growth outlook is also aggressive. On its recent earnings call, Broadcom projected AI semiconductor revenue of $58 billion in FY2026, approximately $115 billion in FY2027 and $230 billion in FY2028. That implies roughly doubling in 2027, then doubling again in 2028. What stands out to me is that the company also expects AI networking revenue to grow at a rate similar to XPUs over the next few years. Its business combines expanding ASIC volume with growth in switches and optical connectivity.

As accelerators exchange more data across longer distances, customers will need to improve network bandwidth and energy efficiency while adding compute chips. Broadcom can sell Ethernet and optical components not only around ASICs it designs, but also into GPU and ASIC systems built by other companies. Even if Google assigns some TPU designs to another supplier, Broadcom can retain separate revenue if those systems continue using its networking products. To assess its growth prospects, I would look beyond design share in each TPU generation to how much chip, switch and optical component content Broadcom wins within each new ASIC customer program.

MediaTek grows as Google brings more work in house

MediaTek allows Google to take more direct control of compute design and procurement while still buying the I/O IP and implementation capabilities it needs. I do not see this simply as a business built on designing chips more cheaply than Broadcom. Even as customers take on more work internally, they still need MediaTek’s validated SerDes and implementation technology. Higher volume lets MediaTek recover those development costs across more units.

MediaTek expects approximately $16 billion of revenue from its first AI accelerator program in 2027 and has targeted the first quarter of 2028 for volume production of its second project. If external sales of existing TPUs continue, as Ming Chi Kuo’s outlook suggests, MediaTek can earn profit from those products while preparing the next generation for production. The key variables are yields on new packaging technologies and the scope of work Google assigns to it. Even if shipment share increases, growth in reported revenue and profit will depend on how much HBM and manufacturing spending passes through MediaTek.

7. Marvell targets both Google and NVIDIA

But what I have heard since then suggests that it was actually closer to the outcome Google wanted. I see the acquisition as a signal that Google wants to develop a supplier outside Broadcom that can handle both ASICs and optical connectivity.

One of the main reasons is Google’s use of optical circuit switching, or OCS. OCS changes the path of an optical signal without converting it into an electrical signal in between, and Google has used it in TPU systems for years. Installing more TPUs and allocating them to workloads of different sizes requires securing the optical technology that connects them, not just the compute chips. If Marvell’s optical DSP and silicon photonics capabilities are combined with Lumentum’s OCS and laser capabilities from 2029 onward, as I expect, Google could consider Marvell across a broader range of ASIC supply decisions. Google and Marvell have already worked together on inference accelerators, NICs and memory interfaces. I believe the design and validation experience gained there could extend into future ASIC development, potentially including TPU v9 or v10.

Potential Marvell roles in Google’s diversification of ASIC and optical suppliers and NVIDIA’s expansion of NVLink Fusion. The Lumentum combination illustrates a business direction following completion of the acquisition.

I also hear that Marvell has recently been expanding its optical interconnect organization significantly, including optical DSP teams. I view this as an effort to add people who can design chips and optical components together for customer systems, rather than sell them only as separate products.

NVIDIA is another reason I continue to watch Marvell. Having exposure to that side of the market gives it a somewhat different technology hedge from Broadcom or MediaTek. NVIDIA wants NVLink Fusion to expand into the same territory Broadcom is pursuing, and sees Marvell as an important bridge. The idea is to bring customer XPUs designed by Marvell into NVIDIA’s interconnect ecosystem and use optical collaboration to build larger systems. This has been a major reason for my positive view of Marvell since early this year. I continue to follow it because both sources of demand could grow at the same time.

As I discussed in my recent AMD article, the biggest question for me is how quickly AMD can supply enough systems to serve as an alternative to NVIDIA. As I explained there, AI technology is, ironically, eroding much of the strength of NVIDIA’s CUDA moat. AMD is also expanding ROCm and support for PyTorch, Triton and vLLM, giving customers more options. I expect this direction to accelerate. Meta’s experience shows that alternatives can work in production. From 2028 onward, if customers validate cost and response time in their own services and begin placing repeat orders for the same systems, AMD could have an opportunity to capture a meaningful amount of volume from NVIDIA.

Instinct and Helios GPU supply shown separately from EPYC host CPUs used in systems such as Google TPU 8tx. The packages and racks are conceptual illustrations of their roles.

EPYC is a separate opportunity that can already generate revenue. One of AMD’s additional strengths relative to NVIDIA is the CPU expertise it has developed over many years. As illustrated by EPYC’s inclusion in Google TPU 8tx, even customers using their own accelerators need host CPUs to prepare data and control workloads. I therefore look at three opportunities together: GPU replacement volume for Instinct, EPYC sales into customer ASIC systems, and an expanding custom ASIC design business. One organizational change I hear about is AMD’s effort to strengthen its ASIC capabilities within AECG, the group that came from its acquisition of Xilinx. The group is reportedly broadening its scope beyond its FPGA foundations into ASIC development for external customers, including discussions involving custom chips for Google and Microsoft. I interpret this as AMD applying its CPU, GPU, chiplet and packaging experience to customers’ own chips. Even when a customer chooses an ASIC instead of a general purpose GPU, AMD wants the opportunity to participate in the design and implementation.

I see the nearer growth opportunity in actual Instinct and EPYC deliveries and repeat orders, with additional opportunities further out in AECG’s ASIC development for customers.

Repeat orders will validate growth in 2028

Ultimately, I believe the key to turning these companies’ plans into 2028 revenue is how many followup orders they win after the first product launches. Once customers confirm cost savings in actual services and secure enough supply for the next expansion, they have a stronger reason to invest again in the same design and operating environment. I expect that this could also mark a period when NVIDIA’s influence begins to weaken. It will be important to keep tracking supply chain developments and how they change the outlook presented here.

Customers will continue diversifying suppliers to reduce their dependence on NVIDIA and Broadcom. But the number of companies that can complete all the required validation and take responsibility for large production volumes remains limited. I expect the ASIC era to produce a substantial expansion in the businesses of a small group of companies with both the necessary technology and supply chain access, rather than equal growth for every design company.
