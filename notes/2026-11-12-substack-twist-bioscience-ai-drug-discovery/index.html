Twist makes the DNA that researchers use in their experiments, and it has been moving into more of the work that happens after that DNA is made. AI can send a huge amount of work through these labs.

How much Twist earns from doing it is where this gets interesting.

I thought there was plenty to like in the results.

Revenue grew 28%, AI drug-discovery revenue grew 175% for the year and Twist reached its first quarter of positive adjusted EBITDA.

Those are encouraging numbers for a company I’ve been following from the research side.

Below, we’ll go through how customers are spending, why Twist is putting so much more money into capacity, and how much of the growth is reaching the rest of the business and what level I am watching for a potential entry.

How this works

A researcher can send Twist a design, have the DNA made, then use its protein production and testing services to find out how that design behaves.

Those results feed the next round of research.

Let’s look at an actual example.

In a project by Amazon researchers generated 288,000 designs for small antibody fragments. These fragments can attach to a specific target, such as a protein associated with cancer. Researchers were testing how well the designs could do that.

Twist made the DNA library containing those designs. The research team screened 100,000 candidates and eventually obtained reliable binding measurements for 46.

You can have thousands of promising designs on a computer and still have a lot of lab work ahead of you. Making the DNA gives researchers something they can actually test. They learn which designs attach to the target, how strongly they attach, and which are worth taking further.

Twist earns revenue from supplying material for those experiments.

A research program can place orders through several rounds of testing, with each round helping the team decide what to pursue next.

The AI business brought in $59.1 million in fiscal 2026, up from $21.5 million. On the earnings call, mgmt described customers starting with a small pilot, then placing a multi-million-dollar order to generate data for their model.

After receiving that data, a customer can spend a quarter or more working through it. It may then return with more specific experiments as it develops promising candidates. Those discovery orders can run from $250,000 to $1 million.

So you could get a big order, a quiet quarter while the customer works through the results, then more orders as the research moves along.

Management says that ongoing revenue generally exceeds the original model-building revenue. Getting another order after the customer has had time to use the first set of results gives us more to work with when thinking about how lasting this demand could be.

More research / same budget

They said that customers are asking for more data points at a lower price per point. Each result helps them learn something about a design. Buying more results with the same budget lets them test more ideas.

Researchers can test many sequences together in a pooled experiment. Better models can also help them choose which designs to send to the lab in the first place. Both affect how much work a customer buys and what it is willing to pay for it.

Twist’s silicon platform makes many different DNA sequences at once in a small amount of space. They say this lowers production costs and gives it room to scale.

If the cost of making that DNA falls faster than the price charged to customers, Twist can earn more from the work while customers get more for their money.

Its new cell-free antibody service produces proteins outside living cells, allowing it to skip growing those cells. Management says this is faster for customers and higher-margin for Twist. A researcher can get from a design to a protein ready for testing sooner, and Twist gets paid for another step in that experiment.

Twist has competition here too so researchers can order these services from other companies, and some of them show up right next to Twist in the software labs already use.

I was also curious who actually owns the work that comes out of all these experiments. Under Twist’s standard terms, the customer owns what they paid for. Twist keeps the technology behind its manufacturing and testing.

So Twist gets paid to do the work and gains experience making and testing all of this material. How much of an advantage that gives them over time is something I’m still thinking through.

How much of Twist is growing this fast?

Twist’s initial fiscal 2027 outlook includes $100 million of AI revenue, up about 69% from the $59.1 million just reported.

Management also said it remained confident in triple-digit growth in both AI orders and revenue. It described $100 million as the amount it could forecast with high confidence today.

There’s a fairly wide gap between that starting forecast and the growth management believes it can achieve.

For the whole company, the midpoint of revenue guidance is $555 million, up about 21%. The identified AI business would account for roughly 18% of sales and about 43% of the increase.

What it costs to handle the larger programs

Twist plans about $75 million of capital spending next year and $150 million over two years.

It is building the capacity to handle larger customer programs, with the money going out before those programs fill the equipment with work.

Twist ended September with about $582 million of cash and short-term investments after raising approximately $425 million net through an offering and additional share sales. Much of the money available for this build-out came from selling more shares.

Coming back to the price

This is where these companies are so tough to value. Because by all metrics, they are still expensive to me. Sentiment definitely plays in here, just as it did for the AI infra trade. Right now it’s valued at roughly 13 times the midpoint of next year’s revenue guidance. That’s after a 50% drawdown in a month.

That’s still a pretty high price for a company working toward consistent profitability. The opportunity in AI drug discovery is exciting and all, and Twist has a real business supplying the experiments behind it. But obviously a lot of future growth is already reflected in the stock, though, and they are about to spend heavily on new capacity.

Personally, I’d be more interested in the $60s.

That’s a long way from today’s price, and it may never get there.

At those levels, the valuation would come down to roughly 6–7 times next year’s expected revenue after cash.

That would give me more room for the uneven growth and investment that could come with building this business.

If the stock did reach that range, I’d revisit the earnings, outlook and reason for the decline before making a decision.

For now, I’m happy to keep following it.
