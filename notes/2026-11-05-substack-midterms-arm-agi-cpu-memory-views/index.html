01 2027 locked in, 2028 the swing year

Recorded on Election Day morning, before any results (per the publisher), the episode opens with analyst Maya Barkin restating the article’s central argument: roughly three-quarters of the 2027 capacity due in the top 20 states is already under construction, so the midterm results won’t stop it, which is why SemiAnalysis stays comfortable with its 50+ GW global 2027 estimate. The 2028 pipeline (62GW in the US model) is different — most of it still needs permits, power agreements, and construction schedules finalized in states electing governors today. In September, the team had estimated only about 1.5GW of roughly 20GW sitting inside local restrictions was actually delayed; that broad conclusion — that moratoriums are not stopping buildouts — still holds, but two things changed since then: Texas’s permit freeze escalated, and candidate positions hardened, with most 2028-critical governors now favoring either pauses or stricter conditions.

02 Texas as the pivotal market

Texas’s TCEQ stopped issuing datacenter permits at the end of September, layered on an ERCOT interconnection queue audit since August — together pulling even on-site power generation into limbo. Barkin modeled two scenarios before the vote: if Governor Abbott won reelection (she saw him as favored, though the race had tightened), his directives would simply expire and permitting could reopen with stricter terms once ERCOT’s audit report lands, targeted for early December — a delay most 2027-targeting projects can absorb. If Democratic challenger Gina Hinojosa won, she would keep the moratorium until the legislature (in session January–May 2027) passes new protections covering household power and water bills, an end to subsidies, an NDA ban and union labor: about eight months if approvals come in that session, close to a year if the new rules only start in late summer, or five to nine months past a December restart, which is tight for 2027-delivery campuses. Since recording, per the publisher, the AP called Texas for Hinojosa and Georgia for Keisha Lance Bottoms, and Democrats won a 3-2 majority on the Georgia commission that sets power rates. Jeremie Eliahou Ontiveros flags the main downside risk to SemiAnalysis’s 2027 forecast, which he says sits on the high side of market estimates: some funded shells already under construction don’t yet have their final air-pollution permits, and under an extended Texas pause those approvals could slip from December toward mid-2027 — the data centers can keep building, but their power plants can’t.

03 Why the backlash is bipartisan

Wayne Ma says his research found the party divide doesn’t track pause-vs-conditions preference as expected; instead, ordinary residents near proposed sites oppose datacenters regardless of party, driven by AI job-loss anxiety and water/electricity misinformation. Ontiveros frames this as a structural shift: datacenter investment-per-footprint is unusually high (a 1GW site can cost $50 billion), and as annual US buildout scaled roughly 10-20x in five years (from a few GW to 40GW in 2027), the old tax-incentive playbook designed for smaller scale no longer fits — he expects market forces to push developers toward paying communities more directly rather than relying on incentives. Wayne Ma points to Ohio, where incentives expected to cost about $136 million/year reached $1.6 billion last year, as a reason candidates of both parties are running to roll incentives back. Ontiveros also flags grid risk from the load side, which he frames as the reverse of Spain’s blackout on the generation side: in Virginia, he says, roughly 1.5GW of datacenter load dropped off at once when UPS systems tripped during a voltage dip, which he says nearly caused a blackout, since a loss that large, that fast can trigger one.

04 Mechanics of permits, conditions, and federal limits

Barkin distinguishes a “pause” (moratorium halting permit issuance — building, air, or zoning) from “conditions” (grid-cost funding, cooling redesign, community agreements) that let projects proceed slower and costlier rather than stall entirely. Governors can move fast via agency directives (Abbott in Texas, an Oregon environmental-permit hold), while legislatures move slower and haven’t adopted any moratoriums yet. Across the tracked policy table — grid cost responsibility, tax exemptions, water limits, bring-your-own-power mandates, community benefits, NDA bans, local control — Democrats lean toward union-labor requirements and NDA bans while Republicans lean toward local control, but ending tax incentives and requiring grid-cost payment are popular across the spectrum. At the federal level, even a Democratic House/Senate sweep is seen as unlikely to change outcomes much, since Trump retains veto power through his term and a narrow Senate would still need Republican votes; the one cross-party consensus is making large electricity users pay their own grid costs, which Trump also supports.

05 Community relations and PR

The panel discusses how developers might win over neighbors: Wayne Ma points to Amazon and Google ending NDAs as reducing the perception of secret dealmaking, alongside highlighting hyperscalers’ renewable-energy purchasing and local infrastructure contributions (fire stations, playgrounds) in underdeveloped areas like West Texas. Ma says direct cash payments to residents are seen as ineffective per on-the-ground interviews, while Barkin argues that transparency, community education on water/environmental impact, and funding schools or roads are better received. One local representative told Barkin that if developers were “hanging up Christmas lights, then we wouldn’t be opposed to them.” The jobs debate surfaces tension: while construction jobs benefit some local workers, an Iowa county-board candidate noted many datacenter construction jobs are filled by out-of-state or international labor, limiting local job-market benefit. The team closes by flagging Texas and Georgia (a toss-up, per Wayne Ma, between Keisha Lance Bottoms, who wants a temporary pause, and an opponent who favors conditions) as the races they’re watching most closely, alongside SemiAnalysis’s ongoing work on the anti-datacenter backlash, which Barkin calls a 2026 phenomenon.

04 The Panhandle water dispute

02 Data center overtaking smartphones

Haas says the ramp of the new Arm AGI CPU, which carries a $2 billion target for what he called the next fiscal year plus, will make data center a bigger business for Arm than smartphones. He puts Arm’s share in data center at 15% in 2024 and 50% in 2025, heading for 60% as this year ends, without saying on air what that share is measured against; the AGI CPU then comes on top of Arm’s fast-growing data-center IP business. He says the combination means the business mix will have shifted substantially twelve months from now, a shift he suggests people haven’t fully recognized yet.

03 Meta, Muse, and agentic AI customers

Haas identifies Meta as the chief customer for the Arm AGI CPU, pairing it with Meta’s Muse AI agent, which he personally uses to manage email. He explains the technical rationale: agentic AI workloads involving scheduling, monitoring, and tokenization checks are CPU-intensive tasks, and the Arm AGI CPU’s power efficiency suits this application well. Beyond Meta, he names OpenAI, SK Telecom, and Cloudflare as additional customers for the product, framing agentic AI broadly as a strong growth use case.

David Faber presses Haas on how the recent shift in Congress might affect future data-center buildouts. Haas responds in two capacities — as Arm CEO and in a role he holds inside SoftBank — noting SoftBank has active US data-center projects, including one in Portsmouth, Ohio. He acknowledges political shifts could create short-term friction in some states while easing buildout in others, but argues that if the US hesitates, the rest of the world is positioned and willing to absorb the demand, citing China’s continued pace as competitive pressure that will keep Western investment intact regardless of near-term US political dynamics.

05 Supply chain confidence

Cramer references past conversations in which supply constraints were Haas’s biggest worry, asking if supply has eased. Haas says it hasn’t become easy but Arm is more confident about its supply situation across wafers, substrates, testers, and memory, built on long-standing industry relationships and chip-industry experience within the company. He notes Arm has been conservative in prior confidence statements on fulfilling demand, and that the $2 billion Arm AGI CPU target reflects increased — not decreased — confidence.

01 Power as the new bottleneck

Arcuri’s framework starts with a gap analysis: back out the gigawatts implied by semiconductor revenue expectations for NVIDIA, Micron, AMD, Broadcom and MediaTek, and compare that to what the grid can actually accommodate plus available off-grid capacity — the gap is large enough that achieving 2028-2030 semiconductor expectations requires significant conversion of currently unpermitted power. This isn’t an immediate problem (not even really a 2028 issue) but becomes material by 2030. He notes a wrinkle: slower frontier labs could push the power problem out a little, as NVIDIA suggested on a call with him, but open-source models are competitive and also power-hungry, and he agrees with the hosts that power could then become AI’s natural pacer. Arcuri frames the recent May-June semiconductor sector correction as the market reassessing where true bottlenecks lie — funds have only regrossed back to roughly half their prior exposure, leaving a “high watermark” that stocks must grow back into.

02 Chip volumes and the ASIC-merchant GPU balance

UBS estimates NVIDIA will ship 6-7 million GPU units this year, with Broadcom’s ASIC units reaching 3.5-4 million this year and roughly 6 million next year — meaning ASICs are closing the unit gap with merchant GPUs even as NVIDIA retains higher dollar share due to higher ASPs. Arcuri views NVIDIA’s edge as resting on CUDA, software moats, and superior rack engineering that let it operate roughly “two steps ahead” of competitors with an annual product cadence. On AMD, he sees ROCm as no longer a meaningful obstacle for hyperscalers (who have their own software resources), making AMD’s hardware “very competitive” for many workloads, though he still sees no compelling reason customers choose AMD over NVIDIA outright. Intel, he’s far more bullish on its foundry/manufacturing business (citing Lip-Bu Tan’s turnaround) than its product roadmap.

03 The ASIC supply chain and startup ecosystem

Arcuri describes a hyperscaler “journey”: companies start with full-service ASIC providers like Broadcom (charging roughly 60% margin) before eventually building internal expertise to control HBM procurement and chip design directly — Google took seven generations to reach this point, Amazon even longer (having acquired Annapurna Labs over a decade ago). Google is now diversifying toward MediaTek for customer-owned tooling, buying its own HBM and doing more of the chip engineering itself; Broadcom’s implied Google-related growth flattens in 2028 as volume shifts to MediaTek and others. Separately, NVIDIA has struck a MediaTek deal to extend NVLink Fusion to non-Broadcom ASIC players, illustrating what one of the hosts calls the industry’s coopetition. On startups, Arcuri highlights disaggregated inference innovation (prefill is compute-bound, decode is memory-bound) addressing GPU fleet utilization often cited at just 30-50%, naming Cerebras, SambaNova, and Etched as active players.

04 Cerebras’s evolving model

Cerebras’s fortunes shifted significantly after signing its OpenAI framework deal — three tranches of 750 megawatts each with embedded optionality — which Arcuri characterizes as currently a cloud deal since OpenAI lacks its own infrastructure. He expects the company to evolve toward roughly a 50/50 split between cloud and hardware sales over time, noting Cerebras occupies a unique position as both a hardware seller and a cloud provider, “more by necessity” than strategic choice. He notes skepticism from some market participants that wafer-scale advantages diminish once large models are taken off the wafer, though he’s only moderately sympathetic to that critique, also referencing failed wafer-scale attempts, including one by Elon Musk.

05 Memory’s structural transformation

Arcuri’s core call: memory, historically supply-driven and highly cyclical, has been fundamentally altered by HBM, though he says that after 30 years in the business he still expects a cycle on the other side. HBM’s annual cadence (new ASIC/XPU generations require new HBM versions, rendering old versions largely unusable) ties memory tightly to the compute roadmap, while each HBM wafer consumes roughly four times the capacity of a non-HBM wafer, constraining supply even as demand rises. Crucially, hyperscalers have for the first time signed long-term agreements with prepayments (e.g., $10B commitments) locking in volume and floor pricing — something Arcuri says “never” happened in prior memory cycles — meaning even a 60-70% price collapse would likely keep Micron profitable and still adding to book value. He credits Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra with transforming the company technologically into what he calls a “Silicon Valley company,” closing the gap with Korean peers (SK Hynix, Samsung, which Arcuri doesn’t cover), while also noting that Micron fits the administration’s push to support American businesses, which already includes an investment in Intel.

06 Equipment spend and innovation

Arcuri estimates roughly $4 billion of front-end equipment per gigawatt of compute capacity, implying wafer fab equipment spend could exceed $400 billion annually in later years versus $158 billion this year — a figure he flags as beyond his formal estimates but illustrative of the scale. He sees equipment (KLA, Lam Research) as earlier in its capacity-building cycle than compute, since historically there’s been “nowhere to put new tools.” Innovation is shifting toward back-end packaging, new materials (citing a memory startup that came out of stealth last week with very different materials), and fab architecture rethinking, while reticle/field-size limits on ASML’s lithography tools — not wafer size — have become the binding constraint, with no larger wafer size adopted since 300mm in 2002.

07 Startups, robotics, and political risk

Discussing venture investing, Arcuri questions whether it’s “too late” to invest in core compute/ASIC startups given entrenched incumbents, prompting the hosts to describe Primary’s broader “compute curious” thesis spanning packaging, materials, and photonics across its industrials, fintech, and enterprise AI teams. On physical AI, he notes Teradyne’s cobot business, slower to grow than Teradyne had hoped, is finally taking off, while humanoid robots are discussed by public companies as semiconductor-content opportunities comparable to EVs. On political risk, Arcuri says his biggest worry isn’t demand but a “French revolution”-style backlash against AI’s economic and job impacts, plus growing concern about power concentration among a small number of companies (NVIDIA’s customer concentration implicating Applied Materials, Micron, and others). He suggests better PR and an administration-Silicon Valley “meet halfway” approach as partial remedies, while noting limits unless China is also brought on board.

01 Ownership as Robinhood’s organizing thesis

Tenev frames Robinhood’s sprawling product set — zero-commission stock trading, Trump accounts, Robinhood Ventures, tokenization, prediction markets — as unified by a single idea: expanding ownership. He argues ownership offers both individual benefit (identity, wealth-building) and a collective one: societies with broad asset ownership are more stable because owners become invested defenders of the system. His own formative experience — leaving Bulgaria in 1991, when inflation ran around 170%, then seeing it hit roughly 2,000% annualized by 1997, while his grandfather turned each salary into copper cookware — shapes this worldview; he says it gave him an early understanding of money.

02 Tokenization and Robinhood Chain

Tenev describes tokenization as analogous to stablecoins: real assets (stocks, private company shares, art, real estate) are custodied traditionally while tokens representing them trade freely on-chain, fractionally, and composably in DeFi pools. Robinhood first launched tokenized EU stock tokens last year without full DeFi functionality; this year’s relaunch on Robinhood Chain enables full on-chain composability, which Tenev says sparked an explosion of developer activity — including stock-token-linked NFTs and meme-coin pairings he says he couldn’t have predicted. He acknowledges critics calling Robinhood a “corpo chain” compared to decentralized consortium-run blockchains, but argues centralized, trusted leadership accelerates adoption. He frames the long-term vision as extending US equity and private-company access to the long tail of countries lacking functioning capital markets.

03 Prediction markets and KPI contracts

Tenev describes prediction markets as an evolution of news, letting users monetize specific knowledge (e.g., forecasting AI model rankings or a California billionaire tax vote) more directly than indirect proxies like stock options. He cites the firm’s planned KPI contracts, which let traders bet directly on a company’s EPS or revenue print rather than on stock price moves confounded by unrelated factors.

04 Agentic trading and infrastructure risk

Robinhood’s Agentic Trading and newer Robinhood Agents product (unveiled at the Hood Summit) let users run daily strategy loops, skills such as a portfolio rebalance, and agent apps from a new app store, such as satellite data for strategies like counting cars in retailers’ parking lots. Tenev positions the north star as putting hedge-fund-level capabilities into retail investors’ pockets, predicting trading activity will first concentrate among sophisticated users before diffusing broadly, similar to coding-agent adoption patterns. On risk, he says current safeguards include segregated agentic accounts and optional trade-approval settings, and argues no major regulatory or infrastructure overhaul is needed yet since markets have been algorithmic for two decades — though he concedes agentic trading could eventually require new regulatory clarity as volumes grow, noting that layers like Citadel, Jane Street, and Jump Trading already handle extreme low-latency volume.

05 Expanding private-market access and Trump accounts

Tenev argues AI’s biggest companies stay private far longer and at far higher valuations than past tech giants like Microsoft or Amazon, shutting ordinary investors out of outsized early returns. Robinhood’s response includes Robinhood Ventures Fund I (holding OpenAI, Eleven Labs, Crusoe) and Fund II, built with Y Combinator and run by Rich Aberman, targeting seed/Series A exposure — both structured as publicly traded, IPO-raised vehicles. He also discusses Trump accounts, where Robinhood serves as sole initial trustee/broker alongside BNY, Treasury, and the IRS. As Tenev describes the program, Treasury seeds $1,000 for each child born from January 2025 “for the next three years,” invested in a low-cost diversified ETF such as one on the S&P 500, supplemented by billions from the Dell family foundation and donations from Gwynne Shotwell and Brad Gerstner (whom Tenev credits as the program’s architect). On accreditation rules, he argues the asset-based accredited investor standard predates the internet era and should give way to knowledge-based alternatives, pointing to the new SEC-directed FINRA exam as a step in that direction.

06 Harmonic and AI-math centralization

Harmonic, Tenev’s math-superintelligence startup, built Aristotle, a model that won gold-medal-equivalent recognition at the International Math Olympiad and has been used by outside mathematicians on Erdős problems. Tenev voices concern — his own stated opinion — that AI-driven mathematical discovery risks becoming centralized within large AI labs’ proprietary data centers, and argues discoveries will be better received by the public if made by ordinary users of accessible tools rather than lab employees alone. Asked to close, Tenev credits his resilience to a childhood shaped by family hardship and hyperinflation, saying leaders should focus on building thick skin since that is the one thing within their control.

01 AlphaFold and the science case for AI

Hassabis frames his entire career goal as using AI to advance science and medicine, citing AlphaFold — finished in 2020 — as the clearest proof point. He says the protein-folding problem was a 50-year “grand challenge,” compared informally to Fermat’s Last Theorem for biology, and that nearly every biologist and medical researcher worldwide has used AlphaFold’s structures, as he expects almost every future drug will. He adds that his spinout, Isomorphic Labs, and future AlphaFold versions aim to extend into biochemistry, toxicity, and efficacy prediction — areas still needed before AI can meaningfully shorten full drug development, not just the structural-biology step.

02 Recursive self-improvement: promise and peril

Hassabis explains RSI works best in “verifiable domains” like math, coding, and games, where answers can be checked and synthetic data generated at scale — citing AlphaGo and its successor AlphaZero, which learned chess from random play to superhuman level within roughly twelve hours using only self-play. He says today’s RSI is in an early, human-supervised phase, boosting engineer productivity 3-5x, but warns the next phase — closed-loop RSI without human review — carries the most risk because it could be fast, uncontrollable, and produce intelligence unlike human cognition. He frames this as an open “intelligence explosion” concern debated for two decades, driven by intense competitive pressure among labs that he suggests all recognize as risky yet continue pursuing regardless.

03 Safety engineering and industry governance

Hassabis states many recent cyber incidents tied to AI agents resulted from insufficiently robust engineering — including agents running unnoticed for weeks — and calls for hardened sandboxes, better monitoring, and prioritizing cyber defenders’ access to fine-tuned models over attackers. He references his own summer essay proposing an industry self-regulatory organization (SRO) with government oversight, ideally U.S.-led and eventually international, which he says influenced a subsequent multi-lab safety accord that he calls insufficient on specifics but still worthwhile since it provides “air cover” for labs to collaborate. He proposes bringing in mathematicians skilled in complexity and game theory to study multi-agent “swarm” dynamics, and floats third-party auditors/evaluators as an emerging ecosystem, noting Accenture’s acquisition of a safety-focused startup as an early example.

04 Consciousness, corrigibility, and two “Rubicons”

On whether AI systems could be conscious, Hassabis says no agreed definition of consciousness exists and that current systems, in his view, are not conscious, distinguishing intelligence from consciousness as “double dissociable” — citing his dog as conscious but not especially intelligent. He opposes building AI with self-volition or reduced corrigibility (the ability for humans to override or shut down a system), warning this combined with RSI would be “doubly dangerous,” and invokes Asimov’s robot stories as a historical warning that simple rule-based constitutions fail at edge cases. He proposes crossing only the “first Rubicon” — building intelligent tools — then using them to advance neuroscience on consciousness for roughly five to ten years before society decides whether to cross a “second Rubicon” toward conscious machines.

05 AI sovereignty and national strategy

Hassabis advises most countries against building their own frontier models, instead recommending they build applications atop existing frontier models (citing healthcare as a prime downstream opportunity) or rely on Western open-source models, which he estimates lag frontier capability by six months to a year. He notes enterprises have more flexibility to arbitrage between providers given different models’ relative strengths (e.g., multimodal vs. coding), while countries face a harder choice, suggesting collaboration arrangements with multinational AI companies as a practical path forward.
