Texas policy risk rises as permitting freezes may last longer, threatening 2028 projects and possible delivery cuts

Nov 4

READ IN APP

What Happened

In the November 3 midterm elections, Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. Texas was the biggest surprise: Talarico won the U.S. Senate seat, becoming the first Democrat to win the seat since 1988. Hinojosa defeated Abbott, who was seeking a fourth term, while several statewide offices, including lieutenant governor, attorney general and comptroller, also changed hands. Republicans are still expected to control the Texas Senate. The final composition of the Texas House and the comptroller race should be updated before publication based on the certified vote count.

Data centers were one of the central issues in the election. Hinojosa pledged to impose a moratorium on new data centers until the legislature establishes safeguards; require projects to demonstrate that they will not raise residential electricity or water bills; prohibit developers from signing NDAs with local governments; and eliminate the sales tax exemption for data centers (6.25%, for up to 20 years, with an estimated two-year fiscal cost of approximately $3.2bn). The freeze was already underway. On August 3, Abbott halted the ERCOT large-load queue (approximately 474GW of applications, around 90% of which were data centers, or more than five times Texas’ historical peak load) and ordered a project-by-project review. ERCOT subsequently delayed Batch Zero in its new interconnection process. On September 21, Abbott also directed TCEQ not to issue any data-center-related permits until the ERCOT and TWDB audits are complete, with all state agencies suspending related approvals. The ERCOT audit is expected in early December, but its completion will not automatically restart permitting.

Our View

Before the election, the market broadly expected Abbott to win re-election and was focused on when the freeze would be lifted after the audits were completed. Hinojosa’s victory could mean a longer freeze and tighter conditions for reopening the permitting process. However, she said during the campaign that she was not opposed to data centers. Her proposals focused mainly on protecting household utility bills and giving communities a say in site selection. The main impact of the election, therefore, is likely to be on how long the freeze remains in place and the conditions under which permitting resumes. Under our base case, we are temporarily maintaining our 2027 delivery forecast. Supporting that view will require a closer review of project permitting status: how much capacity has obtained key permits, how much Texas capacity remains subject to approval, and how much delay is already reflected in the original model. If the freeze lasts longer than assumed in the base case, or the probability of the downside scenario increases, expected deliveries could also be revised lower. New projects in Texas face greater exposure in 2028: most are likely to be delayed, while some demand may shift to other states.

Congress Has Limited Influence Over AIDC Policy

Subscribed Legislation to ban data centers would need 60 votes in the Senate to overcome a filibuster and could also face a presidential veto. Democrats do not currently have the votes, making a broad moratorium difficult to pass in the near term. Cost allocation and disclosure requirements are more likely to advance. There is no unified position within the party: the AI Data Center Moratorium Act introduced by Sanders and AOC in March has not yet made it onto the party’s agenda, while a more prominent proposal is to require data centers to bear their own grid costs. On September 30, the Ratepayer Protection Act received a 57–43 vote in the Senate, falling short of the 60 votes required. The bill had previously passed the House by 417–3. Opponents argued that it merely required state regulators to “consider” the relevant factors and imposed no binding requirements. Similar rules could increase project costs and are directionally consistent with the large-load tariffs being advanced by state PUCs; we have included this impact in our cost assumptions. Federal policy tools that directly affect whether projects can be energized—including EPA air-permitting rules, FERC rules on large loads and co-location, and DOE decisions on federal land siting—remain primarily under the executive branch. Congress can exert influence through hearings, appropriations riders and personnel confirmations, but its impact on project execution is more indirect.

Texas’ Freeze Affects Permitting and Grid Access, but Equipment, Transmission and Skilled Labor Remain the Main Constraints on 2027 Deliveries

As discussed in our previous report, “Deep | AIDC: 2027 Demand Is Around 2x Deliverable Supply, with Power as the Key Constraint,” demand in 2027 is expected to be roughly twice deliverable supply, with power, permitting and labor as the main bottlenecks. State authorities oversee some permitting and grid-access matters: the PUCT governs the ERCOT queue and large-load access (its five commissioners are appointed by the governor); TCEQ handles air permits (its three commissioners are also appointed by the governor); TWDB oversees water-related matters; and the state legislature determines sales tax exemptions and county-level zoning authority. Abbott took action in both August and September to suspend the ERCOT queue and related TCEQ permitting, affecting project approval timelines. Turbine and transformer deliveries, transmission-line construction and skilled-labor shortages are not directly controlled by the state. However, permitting delays could still cause projects with equipment and workers in place to miss their planned energization dates. Texas has relatively favorable gas supply conditions, but whether on-site power plants can enter service in 2027 will also depend on air permits and other approvals. Permitting restrictions began before the election; the election result is more likely to affect the duration of the freeze and the conditions for resuming approvals.

Texas previously had certain advantages in grid interconnection, gas access and air-permit processing. TCEQ’s expedited track can shorten PSD approval to approximately six months, compared with around 1–1.5 years in other states. Texas accounts for about 26% of the announced project pipeline nationwide. The original report estimated 4–5GW of incremental 2027 capacity could bypass the grid queue through BTM projects. However, the amount located in Texas, the portion still awaiting air permits and the portion that has already secured key permits need to be verified project by project. The state’s share of the national project pipeline cannot be used directly to estimate deliverable capacity in Texas in 2027.

If the freeze remains in place until the new legislation takes effect, permitting for new projects in Texas could be constrained for much of 2027. This is a downside scenario, not our base case. Texas bills can take effect immediately if approved by a two-thirds vote in both chambers; otherwise, they generally take effect on September 1. September 1 should not be treated as the fixed effective date for every bill.

Our base case assumes that relevant approvals gradually resume after the audits are completed and that the state legislature may reach a compromise on new rules during the 2027 session. The governor’s September 21 directive tied the suspension of permit issuance to the ERCOT and TWDB audits. Under this assumption, applications already submitted could resume review in the first half of 2027; if lawmakers reach a compromise during the session, new applications could be accepted under the revised rules in the second half. However, continued Republican control of the state Senate does not guarantee a legislative compromise and could also result in a longer administrative freeze. The timeline above is therefore a model assumption. Permitting could resume through an administrative decision after the audits, a legislative compromise or a court ruling; the timing remains uncertain.

Implications for Our Supply and Demand Estimates

For now, we are maintaining the original supply-and-demand framework and 2027 delivery forecast, but this view still needs to be supported by project-level data. We need to verify how much capacity in the original forecast has obtained key permits, how much Texas capacity remains subject to approval, and how much delay is already reflected in the model. The fact that constraints on power equipment, transmission and skilled labor have not changed does not mean projects will necessarily be delivered on time. If additional permitting requirements cause projects to miss their energization windows, deliverable supply in 2027 will still be affected.

  • Texas projects should be assessed by permitting status. The first group has obtained key permits and is expected to be energized on schedule. The second group is already under construction but still needs air, water, interconnection or expansion permits. The third group has not started construction and is still awaiting key permits. Having started construction does not mean all permits required for later phases are in place. Third-party data show that around three-quarters of the 33.8GW scheduled for 2027 across the top 20 states is already under construction. However, this nationwide figure does not demonstrate that Texas projects face limited risk. Likewise, the 1.5GW of delays among approximately 20GW of projects subject to local restrictions as of September does not indicate the impact of an extended freeze. Permitting for Stargate in Abilene needs to be checked by phase: already approved phases should be assessed separately from later phases that still require new permits.- If the freeze continues through most of 2027, the low end of the deliverable-supply range could be revised further down in the downside scenario. Our preliminary estimate is 2–3GW IT, but we first need to determine how much reflects pre-election permitting restrictions not yet included in the original model and how much comes from a longer freeze or narrower exemptions after the election. The calculation should start with the GW IT of Texas projects in the original 2027 forecast that are still awaiting key permits, estimate the share that would slip into the following year because of the extended freeze, and then deduct delays already reflected in the model and any capacity that could be substituted in 2027. We also need to clarify whether the 2–3GW refers to a further reduction from the low end of the original range or the total loss relative to the original base forecast. The original forecast cut-off date and the policy impacts already included in the model still need to be confirmed.- In this downside scenario, assuming demand is unchanged, the ratio of demand to deliverable supply could rise from 2.1x in the original report to approximately 2.5x. This is a static sensitivity analysis, not a “shortfall multiple.” Based on this change, deliverable supply would decline by around 16%. If that corresponds to 2–3GW IT, the implied original supply base would be approximately 12.5–18.75GW. We still need to confirm that this calculation uses the same scenario and definitions as the original model. The change in the supply-demand ratio also does not by itself indicate when rents might decline; we need to distinguish between the incremental compute capacity Meta/xAI use internally and the portion available for third-party leasing.- At the company level, the 3.6GW Sweetwater expansion is still awaiting the Batch Zero interconnection arrangement; the developer and project status remain to be confirmed. CRWV needs to be assessed across three categories. Long-term contracts for projects already delivered help stabilize existing revenue; new and renewed contracts may secure better pricing; projects that have not yet been energized face delayed revenue and carrying costs. Around 79% of the company’s projects have not experienced delays, but it remains unclear whether this figure is based on project count or capacity. Without the share of capacity that can be repriced and the company’s exposure to delayed Texas projects, we cannot yet determine whether the net impact would mainly come from higher rental rates.

SPCX: Limited Direct Impact on xAI’s Identified 2027 Deliveries, but Other Project Risks Remain

  • Based on our xAI site tracker, the expansion projects we can currently identify are concentrated in Memphis and DeSoto County (Colossus 1, Macrohard, MACROHARDRR, MiniHard, Olive Branch, the Southaven auxiliary facility and the fifth site approved in September). In Texas, there are currently only two leads that have not been tied to specific sites: first, a permanent power plant in Pampa suggested by third-party research (with no corresponding TCEQ record); and second, The Information’s July report that xAI had evaluated several sites, deployed personnel and begun hiring. There is still no clear information on land acquisition or permitting.- Based on the projects currently identified, the Texas freeze does not yet provide a reason to make an additional direct cut to xAI’s 2027 delivery plan. This does not mean xAI’s overall deliveries are free of risk. Permits for the permanent units in Southaven, conversion of temporary units and power supply arrangements with TVA/MLGW still need to be monitored separately. The 41 permanent units reported as not yet installed as of July 30 are based on dated information and should be updated before publication; if no update is available, the data cut-off date should be stated. PSD permitting for new greenfield projects typically takes 15–24 months, compared with approximately six months under TCEQ’s expedited track. The two timelines apply to different project conditions and permitting starting points and should not be compared directly.- xAI’s identified expansion projects remain concentrated in Memphis, Tennessee, and DeSoto County, Mississippi. If Texas permitting remains constrained, alternative sites in other states may become more important. However, whether local power supply, permitting and construction schedules can accommodate additional projects still needs to be assessed. Louisiana’s regulatory environment is worth watching, but there is not enough evidence to treat it as a confirmed expansion destination for xAI. A concentration of projects in a small number of states could also trigger local opposition. Southaven has already seen resident noise complaints, questions from a state senator about confidentiality provisions in the agreement, and litigation under the Clean Air Act. Mississippi and Louisiana will hold gubernatorial and legislative elections in November 2027, before xAI makes decisions on its 2028 expansion plans. These elections are worth monitoring, but they do not mean the local policy environment will necessarily remain stable.- Terafab’s permitting and incentives require monitoring. Available information does not currently indicate that Gigasat is directly affected by the freeze. Terafab is located in Grimes County, with Phase I representing an investment of $16.8bn and a target start of operations in 2028. Its JETI filing indicates plans for a self-supplied GW-scale gas-fired power plant and energy storage, as well as a space compute test facility. The associated gas turbines will still require TCEQ air permits. The county property-tax abatement, $30m in support from the Texas Enterprise Fund and other state incentives should be checked separately for approval status, disbursement progress and conditions. It remains unclear whether the test facility would bring Terafab within the scope of data center restrictions. Gigasat is located in Bastrop and manufactures satellites for orbital data centers; based on current information, it is outside the scope of the current data center freeze.

Proposals Requiring Data Centers to Bear Grid Costs Could Spread to More States

Data centers have become a bipartisan political issue. Around two-thirds of respondents nationwide oppose building new data centers in their communities (76% of Democratic voters and 52% of Republican voters); at least 50 local ballot measures concerning data centers have been proposed across 13 states. Abbott is a Republican governor, yet he suspended related approvals twice in the three months before the election, indicating that project costs and household utility bills are concerns among voters across party lines. The November 3 results also reflect the political pressure surrounding this issue. However, the available evidence is not sufficient to show that data centers were the direct cause of Texas turning blue, nor does it establish that other states will adopt the same policies. Most state legislatures will be in session in 2027. Other states may follow aspects of the Texas approach by reviewing projects, temporarily suspending approvals, or requiring developers to bear related costs and provide more disclosure. The project cancellation rate is currently around 11%, of which 78% is attributed to permitting; whether this rate rises will become clearer after the state legislative sessions conclude. In the near term, other states are more likely to discuss large-load tariffs, developer-funded grid interconnection costs and disclosure requirements. Having developers bear incremental grid costs could address some local concerns, but project approvals will still depend on local politics, power supply and demand, and specific regulatory requirements.

What Could Change Our View

Signals That Would Support Maintaining Our Forecast

  • If the ERCOT audit is completed and TCEQ resumes accepting relevant air-permit applications, and the projects in our original forecast can still be energized in 2027, the 2–3GW downside adjustment could be smaller. However, resuming application intake does not mean permits have been granted or that projects will necessarily be energized in 2027.- If, after Hinojosa takes office on January 19, 2027, the moratorium is limited to new applications and does not apply retroactively to applications already submitted or projects that have obtained key permits, this would support the base-case forecast. The policy impact could then be concentrated more in 2028 and beyond.

Signals That Would Require a Forecast Downgrade

  • If the ERCOT audit leads to an expansion of the suspension to projects already under construction but not yet energized, or if key projects consequently miss construction, equipment installation or energization windows, the 2027 delivery forecast should be lowered.- If the 2027 legislative session passes an unconditional moratorium, or cancels tax incentives retroactively for projects that have already been approved, the forecast for incremental Texas supply in 2028 should be lowered.- If Georgia, Michigan or other states impose a broad permitting freeze similar to Texas in the first half of 2027, the forecast for nationwide incremental supply in 2028 should be lowered. The trigger should be a formal policy action, not merely campaign statements by candidates.
