SUMMARY

Overview

The expert, an AI and data engineering leader at a large contract research organization (CRO), discussed how artificial intelligence is being applied across clinical development. The most established uses are in clinical data management, trial operations, and medical and regulatory writing. AI tools combine information across trial systems, support monitoring and document preparation, and automate repetitive tasks. Adoption remains uneven, and many study-level benefits are not yet firmly demonstrated.

Key Insights

CRA support is a major near-term opportunity. AI assistants can prepare site-visit materials, flag issues, prioritize tasks, and help with follow-up documentation. The expert estimated substantial time savings in visit preparation, while cautioning that task-level savings do not equal an equivalent increase in overall productivity. Data management, clinical operations, and medical writing are the top value areas. Data anomaly detection and query prioritization are especially promising because these activities recur throughout a trial. Study timelines may improve, but evidence is emerging. The expert gave a tentative estimate for trials using AI across multiple workflows, not an established industry-wide result. Enrollment and site selection could affect timelines more directly than speeding up tasks outside a study’s critical path. Human oversight remains essential. Protocol design, patient eligibility and consent, safety decisions, and regulatory accountability require qualified professionals. CRO economics will evolve. Providers may initially retain savings on fixed-price contracts, while sponsors are increasingly seeking lower prices as efficiency gains become visible. The expert expects greater sharing of benefits over time.

Competitive Landscape

Player or factor

Position and implications

Large CROs

Can draw on historical trial, site, enrollment, and monitoring data, and established clinical networks. Their challenge is turning prototypes into compliant, scalable production tools.

Smaller and midsized CROs

Can access capable foundation models and public or licensed datasets. Replicating the breadth, quality, and standardization of long-term operational data is harder.

Sponsors

Increasingly assess CRO AI capabilities and may bring selected document-heavy or data tasks in-house. Expertise, quality, delivery records, and site networks remain important selection criteria.

CRO–sponsor contracting

AI may support hybrid pricing with a base fee and incentives tied to outcomes such as enrollment or milestones.

Potential impact of 20%–25%; central monitoring assistant roles could be fully automated in the expert’s view.

Overall trial timeline

A tentative 5%–10% reduction where AI spans multiple workflows; evidence remains limited.

CRO revenue per trial

Possible gradual declines in labor-intensive services, alongside potential growth in trial volume and demand for higher-value support.

Clinical Data

AI can flag anomalies, prioritize data queries, identify protocol deviations, and support monitoring. It may also assist recruitment and site selection, but patient engagement, eligibility confirmation, safety escalation, and regulatory decisions still require human judgment.
