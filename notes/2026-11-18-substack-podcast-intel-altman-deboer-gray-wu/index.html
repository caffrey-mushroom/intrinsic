  • Altman’s double-counting warning, reportedly made privately to Samsung executives: many new chip vendors each tell the silicon supply chain the labs will buy most of their output, so the same demand gets counted repeatedly (our read).
  • Altman puts the DRAM price rise at roughly 5-7x and says memory makers held back capex because the business historically meant investing $20B to earn $19B; he says production is now ramping.
  • Altman calls iPhone photos and iMessages tech’s biggest lock-in and says Dots’ learned behavioral preferences are harder to export, and says OpenAI is still working out what export would look like, implying strong consumer switching costs (our read).
  • After Albergotti says voters turned against data centers in all but one referendum, Altman says people now put data centers far out in the desert, sympathizes with not wanting one nearby, and says the country has room.

01 Huang’s ouster and NVIDIA

Altman calls Jensen Huang a longtime hero of his, adding that heroes have flaws, and expresses personal empathy given his own 2023 board ouster; Albergotti notes a letter circulating about Huang appeared copied from one Altman’s supporters drafted in 2023. He declines to say whether Huang should return, citing insufficient information, but says he can’t picture the AI revolution without Huang and the company he built. Asked whether NVIDIA’s guarantee for OpenAI’s Ohio data center, which Albergotti puts at $105 billion, still holds the same weight post-ouster, Altman says NVIDIA remains a behemoth whose guarantees people still trust, though he concedes Huang’s ambition and scale have mattered to the industry and there could be some ripple effect — while still expecting the industry overall to be fine given strong demand.

02 Going public and chip supply tensions

Altman confirms OpenAI will eventually IPO, citing real downsides to being public — volatility, high-stakes safety decisions — but argues the world as a whole, not just private investors, deserves exposure to AI’s value creation. He dismisses the idea that OpenAI risks becoming an also-ran once public behind Anthropic, arguing investor demand has exceeded supply over the past year. On chip supply, he explains that with many more chipmakers now in the market versus the historical single-provider era, several vendors are each telling the silicon supply chain that OpenAI, Anthropic and others will buy most of their chips — purchases that can’t all materialize, even as he still sees the underlying data-center buildout as solid. He notes supply constraints have shifted over time between memory, rack assembly, data-center capacity, and components like PCB laminate.

03 Data-center backlash and AI politics

Altman argues the industry’s sales pitch has been self-defeating — offering a cure for cancer and cheap goods in exchange for people losing agency — and says data centers serve as a visible proxy for deeper anxiety about power concentration and loss of human control. He says the industry needs to take a firm position against any gradual or sudden handover of decision-making to AI. On politics, he reaffirms his earlier view that he is politically homeless and hopes both parties move toward a more innovation-friendly posture, saying he last spoke with Trump at a China state dinner and would try to reach officials like Stephen Miller if needed.

04 Security, safety, and RSI

Albergotti raises what he calls OpenAI’s own spy drama: a hidden microphone in an office and warnings to employees about possible foreign agents. Altman answers that people look for an edge on AI in all sorts of ways, citing a camera put in a conference room and attempts to get engineers to give up pretraining secrets over drinks; he says OpenAI has had more practice with this than peers now suddenly confronting it, while admitting its security hasn’t been perfect. He frames OpenAI’s safety approach as centrist and pragmatic, rejecting both total secrecy and treating AI like any other technology. Asked about recursive self-improvement, he says OpenAI isn’t letting models self-improve without human oversight, though development is already faster using AI tools, and says OpenAI deliberately holds back on RSI based on the principle that people must stay in control of the future. Asked about a METR report that Anthropic doubled its capability-development speed on specific tasks, he says he doesn’t know of a comparable measurement and that blended numbers are hard to generalize, and notes OpenAI now works with embedded evaluators including METR.

05 Products, memory, and the buildout

On Dots’ memory feature, Altman agrees people should be able to see, modify and export their data, though says Dots’ deep behavioral understanding makes this harder than standard ChatGPT memory export. He defends his controversial RAM comments, clarifying he wants people to buy lots of RAM, but argues shared cloud memory is a more optimal use of scarce supply than idle local RAM given the shortage he flagged years earlier. On debt-funded buildout, he says he hasn’t seen revenue and margin growth like this in any past tech cycle he’s witnessed, expects some excess that needs to come off but not current recklessness, and points to AI-driven science — citing math, physics, chemistry and materials breakthroughs, plus newly expanded free academic compute — as evidence the buildout’s value remains underpriced.

THE SIGNAL

  • DeBoer says memory has been tight for several years and calls that the reason for the global build-out; the 250B+ US plan plus expansions in Asia suggests a large wave of supply arriving later (our read).
  • Micron’s technology priorities have changed: for about 20 years, bits per wafer and cost drove it, but recently performance, customization and customer co-design matter more than raw bit growth, which supports a less commodity-like margin argument (our read).
  • Even with AI targeted at sharply raising designs per engineer, Micron is still adding staff because the work is labor-heavy, so productivity gains likely become more custom designs, not lower opex (our read).

01 US fab expansion rationale

DeBoer details more than $250 billion in planned investment, including a major new facility underway in Boise, Idaho (Micron’s headquarters city), a larger facility in Clay, New York, and an expansion in Virginia — among the largest industrial projects in US history, in his telling. Pressed on why Micron is building in the US rather than cheaper labor markets, he says the company is balancing its output across the globe, with additional expansion projects ongoing in Asia, but that the US push reflects the concentration of Micron’s largest customers domestically and the strategic value of matching US supply to US demand.

02 Community strategy for new sites

DeBoer says the company’s approach to building large fabs centers on upfront partnership with local communities, community colleges, and schools, citing recent Micron donations in Boise toward roads and housing. He frames the jobs created as durable, given ongoing manufacturing needs, and notes the broader multiplier effect — partner companies supporting fab infrastructure plus retail and restaurant growth in surrounding areas — particularly in New York, a region without prior heavy industry presence.

03 Capacity constraints and customer co-design

Asked when Micron has to refuse customer requests, DeBoer says increased R&D spending has let Micron say yes more often by better understanding customer roadmaps three to four years out and co-designing features jointly. Still, he concedes Micron cannot support every request and must align expectations when capacity or technical roadmap timing doesn’t match customer needs — describing deep, forward-looking roadmap discussions with large customers that didn’t exist when memory was treated as a commodity.

04 Memory’s shifting role in AI

DeBoer describes an internal cultural shift at Micron toward recognizing memory as central to AI tokenomics and the broader AI computing ecosystem, a change he says has elevated the company’s sense of purpose; the host raised past boom-bust cycles like crypto-driven demand spikes as a contrast. He positions current AI-driven demand as a different, more durable architecture-level opportunity rather than a speculative bubble, though this view is presented as his own assessment rather than established fact.

05 AI inside Micron’s own engineering

DeBoer says AI adoption is most impactful in engineering, where Micron is targeting designs done in half the prior time and twice as many designs with the same number of people, alongside standard coding use. Applications include design verification and physical AI for simulating process, thermal, and chip physics. On whether large general models will handle this out of the box versus requiring fine-tuning, he says it’s a mixture: proprietary process-technology work requires bringing models in-house and optimizing them internally, while more industry-common tasks like design verification benefit from off-the-shelf or partner AI models.

THE SIGNAL

  • Gray says spending on AI models across Blackstone portfolio companies rose 20-fold in the past year because of productivity gains, a firsthand data point on enterprise token demand from outside the AI labs.
  • Gray says Blackstone’s data centers would be worth much more if it ripped up the contracts and went to the spot market (our read: contracted landlords give up upside when rates rise).
  • The largest model companies keep asking Blackstone for compute available now, and Gray describes the tenants as hyperscalers averaging $2.5T market caps with little net debt, supporting his low counterparty-risk claim.
  • Gray says the phase 3 trials Blackstone helps fund cost a billion dollars or multiple billions, a capital bottleneck it can fill (our read: if AI widens the drug-candidate pipeline, demand for this financing should grow).

01 Scale and the railroad analogy

Gray says the firm looks to the late-19th-century railroad era, not dot-com, as the closer historical parallel, because that period combined huge capital intensity with civilization-level transformation—moving society from horses and candles to cities with electricity. He argues internet infrastructure required one build-out and then stopped; AI, by contrast, needs continuous trillions in capital with no clear end point, amid what he calls a profound compute and energy shortage in the US. He frames Austin/Texas as symbolically central because it’s where Silicon Valley’s technology meets the physical-world buildout — spanning AI infrastructure, defense, pharmaceuticals, manufacturing, and robotics.

02 Why this cycle is different

Unlike the 1990s fiber buildout or railroads, where companies built speculative supply and hoped demand followed (often with heavy leverage and failures), Gray says today’s data centers are built against long-term contracts with hyperscalers that carry low net debt and high cash flow. He likens poorly-timed historical speculation to overbuilt condo markets during a real-estate boom and contrasts it with today’s constrained supply — chips, power, entitlements, and capital are all hard to aggregate, which he suggests limits reckless overbuilding. He floats Elon Musk’s approach — using balance-sheet strength to build speculative capacity and serve the spot market — as a rare exception, and predicts that any shakeout will consolidate capacity toward better-capitalized hyperscalers and major model companies, with the compute shortage worsening as the industry moves toward personalized agents and physical AI.

03 Responsible buildout and worker impact

Gray says the economic gains from AI are large enough that benefits shouldn’t accrue only to a small group of technology and financial firms — he says Blackstone is partnering with unions and local communities on job creation, lowering consumer electricity costs, and managing property-tax impacts tied to data center development.

04 Internal AI adoption and productivity

Within Blackstone and its portfolio, Gray says the firm is still encouraging more AI usage rather than restricting it, given early returns: rental applications process 90% faster, and Ancestry.com processes family-history searches 30x faster. In the hedge fund business, the firm is using AI to synthesize earnings reports and calls in real time to improve investment decisions. The hosts compare current AI token spending to marketing budgets — some spend is wasted, but the point is to learn and reallocate, not to stop. He expects cost-sensitivity to matter more later, but says that isn’t the primary focus now.

05 Biotech promise versus physical bottlenecks

Gray describes AI’s potential in biology as among the most exciting developments he’s seen, enabling synthetic chemistry and biology approaches that could transform cancer, neurological, and pulmonary disease treatment. He says the real constraint isn’t the technology front-end but the physical-world bottleneck of clinical trials — phase 3 trials that he says cost a billion or multiple billions of dollars and require FDA processes and human participants. Blackstone has a business investing in phase 3 trials and recently announced deals with J&J and Pfizer. He also mentions Blackstone’s deployment joint venture with Anthropic, called Ode, whose chief challenge he says is simply hiring enough people to meet business demand for deployment services.

06 Broader societal upside

Gray closes by citing wider gains beyond infrastructure and biotech: democratized education (access to top-tier instruction without needing to live near Cambridge, UK or Massachusetts), a boom in company formation and entrepreneurship, and a boom in blue-collar jobs, while acknowledging society must manage job transitions and skills training as disruption occurs.

THE SIGNAL

  • Devin Bot routes work across models whose cost spans roughly 10-30x from largest to smallest, and routing has been a focus for about six months; that mix is a lever on inference gross margin (our read).
  • Early Devin Bot beta users Fireworks, Reducto and Decagon use it for reactive maintenance: watching customer channels for performance regressions, spinning up sub-agents to find the bad commit and fix it, and handling version upgrades.
  • Cognition ships its own coding models; Wu says capability is now table stakes, so the contest shifts to style, speed and cost, which favors post-training on proprietary usage data.
  • Wu rejected the idea that companies will build everything in-house: he guessed Salesforce took about 10M engineering hours, and making software 10x easier to build doesn’t make non-core systems worth replicating.

01 Devin Bot and persistent agency

Cognition released Devin Bot the day before the interview, which Wu describes as persistent agents with memory that take on entire outcomes rather than individual tasks, extending Devin’s original vision of a virtual AI teammate. Rather than scoping narrow coding tasks, these bots run ongoing projects—his example is Cognition’s Android app, built entirely by one Devin Bot instructed to mirror the existing iOS app’s behavior, with no human engineer involved. Another bot continuously optimizes inference kernels, a task Wu says gives a taste of recursive self-improvement since GPU throughput and token output are directly measurable. He notes early personal-assistant-style agents from other teams (Instinct, Muse, GrokBot, and Poke, from the Interaction team; he says he’s known Marvin for years) influenced product thinking, but Devin Bot is built from a “teammate at work” framing—each instance addressable in Slack, working alongside humans, proactively scanning channels.

02 Cybersecurity as a forcing function

Wu says Cognition brought on Alex Stamos, former Facebook CSO, as its own CSO, and shipped a major security release about a week and a half before this conversation. He argues security is the clearest case for agent-based defense: periodic code scans can’t counter continuous automated attacks, so enterprises need an always-on agent defense force. He says the security push is generating both net-new customers and cross-sell, especially as recent high-profile incidents push enterprises to scan code and surface overwhelming backlogs—one example he gives is a company finding thousands of warnings and vulnerabilities with no clear remediation plan. The hosts separately reference reports of a bank responding to similar vulnerability discoveries by hiring roughly a thousand contractors, framed as an illustration of the scale problem Cognition aims to address via automation instead.

03 Training philosophy: skip pretraining

Asked why Cognition, despite ample capital, doesn’t build its own foundation models, Wu says it could but chooses not to—pretraining is a very different discipline from Cognition’s core competency. Instead, Cognition does mid-training and post-training on top of existing models (recently one he refers to as InkLink), leveraging proprietary data from real Devin usage to push coding-task scores higher. He frames the differentiator as not raw problem-solving, now achievable across many models, but tuning behavior, style, and tool-use patterns—e.g., whether an agent reuses existing utilities instead of writing redundant code—which he says is inherently a mid/post-training problem requiring deep evaluation work on what counts as a good job for a given task.

04 Scale, hiring, and executive philosophy

Cognition’s engineering/product/design org numbers about 75 people, within a roughly 500-person total company, which Wu jokes is partly achieved by running large numbers of Devin Bots rather than human hires. On executive hiring, he says Cognition recruits leaders from companies specifically known for the skill being hired for—citing CRO Chris Degnan (credited with taking Snowflake’s revenue run rate from zero to over $3 billion), legal chief Paul Grewal, a marketing lead who scaled Rippling’s revenue run rate past $1 billion, and a partnerships lead who came from Brex as Chief Business Officer. Wu’s heuristic: look for people who identified the underlying first-principles success factors rather than those who merely executed a playbook suited to one company’s circumstances.

05 Jagged intelligence and uneven diffusion

Responding to a host’s observation that models solved Olympiad-level math before people were booking haircuts or restaurant reservations with agents, Wu describes AI’s capability frontier as non-linear, invoking the term jagged intelligence. He speculates that AI models may eventually design novel, creative math competition problems better than humans can, floating the idea of AI-generated Math Olympiad-style questions. He sees coding agents in 2025-2026 as a precursor pattern: well-scoped, verifiable tasks got automated first, and consistency gains are now enabling broader, open-ended personal-agent use cases. He expects a current diffusion bottleneck—the difficulty of teaching people to use new AI tools—to erode as agents shift from delivering tools to delivering outcomes directly, such as an agent that simply buys a needed item rather than requiring the user to learn a new interface.

06 Broader applications and open questions

Wu says Cognition isn’t pursuing pure drug-discovery bio work directly but has seen adjacent demand—Brian Johnson was an early Devin beta user, building personal health-measurement tooling. Wu expects growth in what he calls personalized software: bespoke tools built for individuals or narrow needs that wouldn’t justify hiring a human engineering team. On vendor and software supply-chain strategy amid rising AI-driven cyber risk, he says Cognition is selective about which vendors touch customer data and applies tiered diligence, but doesn’t believe cheaper software-building means companies should insource everything—core competency still dictates what’s worth building versus buying, citing Salesforce-scale engineering effort as something most companies shouldn’t replicate. Finally, he notes product naming is genuinely difficult and unpredictable across the industry, recounting that Cognition considered alternatives like “Super Devins” and “Auto Devs” before settling on its final name.

  • Prediction-market odds cited on the show put a Huang return at about 1%, briefly 8% on a rumor until a board letter knocked it down, then 11% after his statement; Kalanick says he’d still be a buyer.
  • The hosts said they flagged Megaspeed and the shell entities 13 months earlier, and a short seller published around April or May with little traction, so the information sat public before it moved the stock.

01 The ‘op’ thesis

Kalanick, drawing on his own experience fending off what he calls corporate attacks, argues the campaign against Jensen Huang followed a familiar pattern: a kernel of truth gets amplified by a mob into something larger and more damaging than the underlying facts support. He says he can recognize these campaigns almost instinctively, comparing it to a psychic’s intuition built from personally experiencing roughly six months of weekly attacks, each one landing predictably on Sunday nights. The hosts note they had surfaced related details — Megaspeed, shell corporations, export-control questions — more than a year before the story gained traction, and Kalanick suggests the timing gap implies the issue was shelved until someone chose to weaponize it, though he stops short of naming who.

02 Twelve principles for fighting back

Kalanick published and reads from a public list of principles for responding to a coordinated attack. Key points he elaborates on: (1) own what you actually did, but resist the psychological pull to accept the mob’s exaggerated version of the truth; (2) recognize the attack is driven by enemies seizing an opportunity, not strictly about the facts, which requires standing up a war room rather than reacting piecemeal; (3) no matter how bad it looks, the situation is never as bad as it appears — but you can’t act on that principle until you’ve processed the personal shock. He admits he personally handled his own situation poorly by retreating defensively rather than engaging strategically, describing how that passive, reactive posture became a conditioned, almost physical source of dread every week.

03 NVIDIA without its founder

Kalanick’s central worry is that NVIDIA shifts toward board-driven, risk-averse management rather than founder-led decision-making, which he says is clearly not the best path forward for the company. He explicitly distinguishes this from Apple’s post-Steve-Jobs transition, noting Apple’s handover was planned and the company publicly honored Jobs’s legacy (including Apple University) rather than purging loyalists, which let continuity persist even as innovation reportedly slowed. He argues NVIDIA’s situation is structurally different because any new CEO approved by the board has strong incentive to protect their own job, creating built-in resistance to reinstating Huang — a dynamic he expects could play out at an annual shareholder/proxy vote he believes is likely in June, possibly sooner.

04 Momentum for Huang’s return

Kalanick and the hosts suggest NVIDIA’s most recent earnings call already showed a noticeably different character without Huang’s direct leadership. He emphasizes that Huang’s own public statement that he’s open to returning is the most consequential recent development because it removes any stigma around a comeback and gives employees, shareholders, and what Kalanick describes as the vast majority of people something to rally behind. He notes NVIDIA employees have already begun publicly posting support, and suggests a mass walkout — not just a petition — would be a far more forceful signal to the board.

05 AI centralization and broader stakes

Beyond NVIDIA specifically, Kalanick frames the episode’s stakes as about founder support generally and the risk of centralized AI control, asking rhetorically whether people want an overlord or the ability to use AI on their own terms.

06 Mining and food-robotics updates

Asked for updates outside NVIDIA, Kalanick says his mining venture is poised for dramatic growth over the next twelve months, describing it as entering a hyper-growth phase. He also says the factory manufacturing his food robots is going up in Pittsburgh in Q1, which he frames as helping restaurant owners improve their economics, and mentions plans for a “robot day” event roughly in that same timeframe.

THE SIGNAL

  • Ittycheria says MongoDB’s cloud business was a tiny share of revenue at IPO, mocked as competing with AWS, yet now drives all its value; Halligan says HubSpot IPO’d with just $1M CRM revenue, now its valuation basis.
  • Ittycheria says database decisions remain longer-term, so AI-era volatility has hit MongoDB’s business less than others’; it still recasts its 12-month plan every 90 days, using revenue as the expense proxy.
  • He asked 40 AI-company CROs, pressured to raise quotas, how much productivity survives if inbound vanished; silence followed (our read: AI-app sales efficiency may be overstated, echoing MongoDB’s admitted COVID overhiring).
  • Ittycheria says even the best-run public software companies’ European teams are underperforming amid a weak economy; he views startups’ early Europe expansion (e.g., Legora vs Harvey) as a drag, not existential.

01 The boomerang return

Ittycheria ran MongoDB for 11 years, taking revenue from about $35 million to more than $2.3 billion, before retiring to spend time with his wife as empty nesters. Ten months later, while in Tuscany, he got a call from the board chair saying his successor was considering leaving and asking if he’d return; he said yes largely because he didn’t want to leave 6,000 employees, thousands of customers, and investors in limbo. Within roughly a week he was back in the chair, and the company held an Investor Day and then its largest user conference in New York on back-to-back days.

02 Team, truth, and speed

His first priority on return was team composition, including hard personnel decisions and simplifying an organizational split his predecessor had created. He argues the bulk of time lost in solving business problems isn’t the fix itself but recognizing there’s a problem in the first place—people are not naturally forthcoming, so leaders must narrow the gap between what employees feel and what they say. He credits vulnerability—admitting his own mistakes—as a mechanism that built trust and got his teams to surface issues faster, which in turn compounds decision speed. He explicitly separates “polite” from “kind,” arguing that avoiding hard conversations to be liked does employees a disservice. On public criticism and Glassdoor-style complaints, he notes an asymmetry: unhappy people post, satisfied ones stay quiet, while inside a company good news reaches leaders easily and bad news must be actively sought out.

03 Hiring, firing, and reference checks

Evaluating his own successor search, Ittycheria pushed back on generic job-description traits in favor of specific behaviors: an ownership mindset (invoking Warren Buffett’s framing of owning 100% of a business for 100 years), balancing short- versus long-term decisions, being detail-oriented enough to justify weighing in on a part of the business, and a genuine track record of developing (not just hiring) people. He says the toughest CEO decisions test courage more than intelligence—particularly letting go of long-tenured executives who helped build the company but can’t scale, warning that hesitation lets leaders convince themselves things will improve on their own. On hiring CROs, he stresses that a good revenue chief builds a custom playbook around the three whys (why act, why you, why now) rather than copy-pasting a prior employer’s methodology (e.g., MEDIC from PTC), and that infrastructure sales—requiring buy-in from developers, security, ops, and economic buyers—differs fundamentally from simpler application-software sales. He also flags a common hiring mistake: crediting candidates for a past company’s brand-driven inbound demand rather than their actual selling ability, and urges reference checks beyond the references candidates supply.

04 Talent, judgment, and Sequoia founders

Now at Sequoia, Ittycheria pushes back on a partner’s preference for founders with Math Olympiad or chess-prodigy pedigrees, arguing that in a world where raw intelligence becomes cheap and ubiquitous, wisdom, taste, and judgment matter more. He notes that exceptionally bright people are often identified early as the brightest in their class and can struggle later to admit when they’re wrong, creating dysfunction; intellectual humility, he says, is what separates good founders.

05 What’s changed for CEOs in the AI era

Ittycheria identifies four shifts: decisions now have a much shorter half-life, forcing faster calls; gross margins require understanding token/compute economics, not just traditional software costs; there’s a greater premium on talent since fewer people can do more; and founders must actively modulate team euphoria and panic cycles as news (like sudden model releases) swings sentiment. He also warns AI-era sales leaders to distinguish inbound-driven demand from self-generated demand, noting salespeople’s productivity may collapse if inbound dries up.

06 Scaling from startup to enterprise

Discussing growth from 2 to 2,000+ employees, Ittycheria says leaders must constantly reassess whether their team is right for the next stage over a planning horizon he says is shrinking, spot-check decisions without micromanaging, and recognize that bad news travels slowly upward and gets softened in the retelling—so a leader should always assume news is worse than reported. He also emphasizes self-awareness, using the question of how one is perceived after leaving a room as a test of situational awareness. Host Halligan closes by applying these lessons to his own return as HubSpot CEO: emphasizing high-agency ownership culture, faster “two-way-door” decisions, tracking usage/NPS/unit economics, and aiming to be an emotionally steady presence.
