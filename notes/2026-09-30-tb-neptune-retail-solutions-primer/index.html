Questions

Could you describe what Neptune Retail Solutions does? What products and services does the company provide?

Expert: Neptune Retail Solutions is a digital incentives and shopper marketing platform, marketing company to help primarily CPG brands and retailers drive sales and achieve marketing goals and objectives.

Analyst: What do CPG brands entail?

Expert: CPG brands, primarily consumer packaged goods, sometimes referred to as fast-moving consumer goods, are basically things that are purchased on store in shelf. So, everything from both food products, like packaged goods like cheese and macaroni and peanut butter and candy and those kinds of things to non-food products, including shampoo, soap, toothpaste, all those types of things can move into the dog food and pet food category as well. Then, in general, in terms of the other areas that Neptune gets into, alcoholic beverage is a big and fast-growing segment, as well as pharmaceutical.

What is the value proposition of the company's products or services? What problem is the company trying to solve, in your opinion?

Expert: In general, it really satisfies shopper marketing needs for CPG brands. It helps encourage and complete the sale at shelf. So, while larger marketing and promotional elements may happen on television or digital media or social media that build awareness and consideration, it's often the products that Neptune sells that helps close that sale either at shelf or incentivises purchase through digital incentives, so things that happen within the store or within the store environment.

What is the full range of industries and business sizes that could use the company's products or services? What is its TAM?

Expert: In terms of the small, medium and large, I would say medium and large, primarily. It's definitely open to smaller companies as well. However, the scale and size and cost of entry can be difficult for smaller start-up brands, new brands. In terms of the primary market, it's really the larger in terms of where they are really going after, it's the Procter & Gamble, Unilever, Kraft, PepsiCo, Coke. The major CPG brands are really their primary targets, but they can also certainly help mid-size emerging brands or growing brands to get a foothold in the retail environment.

What is the typical revenue model for each core segment? Is it transactional [one-time payment for a one-time product or service provided], recurring [fixed payments at fixed intervals, similar to a Netflix subscription model] and reoccurring [unpredictable, consumption-based payments at regular intervals, similar to an electricity bill model]?

Expert: I would say each of these are slightly different approaches. Digital incentives are transactional, but across all of these, there's also an element that I think is recurring, which they would say, okay, they have upfront commitments. They would say, "You are going to spend X hundred million dollars," or whatever the number is, "in a calendar year," or, in some cases, two years, but it's primarily a single year. You have to spend this total amount and for that commitment of spending USD 100m, you will get preferential pricing. You have to spend that amount of money to get that preferential pricing. Again, it's a little bit of both of those, so it's definitely transactional. It's campaign by campaign. This will run for X period, one month, two months, three months, and it's an individual thing. The pricing and the overall commitment is often based on a total annual sum that you can then put together and say, we might get to the end of the year and say, "Hey, you still have 41-50% of your budget to spend," and they will spend it all on holiday and that's okay. It's just how they spend it out.

Analyst: Does Neptune get paid on a per-campaign basis?

Analyst: What percentage of times do you think it's going to be transactional vs recurring?

Expert: I think the larger ones, it's almost always an upfront commitment or recurring, but in general, each programme, I would say 91-100% are in that transactional campaign where you run a campaign on X dollars and it comes and goes.

Analyst: How long are the campaigns typically for?

Expert: Usually, 2-3 months.

Could you provide a use case for each core segment? What is a typical example of how customers use Neptune's products and services? Could you describe a customer journey based on what happens within each segment, starting with digital incentives?

Expert: Digital incentives are digital coupons. They are promotional incentives that are delivered to consumers. Usually, primarily through a retailer app, so a CVS app and what Neptune provides in that, we are the middle person between the brand, let's say, Crest Toothpaste and CVS and we process that USD 1 off coupon that then lives in the CVS app. We have got relationships with pretty much the vast majority of retailers, again processing that transaction of and setting up and delivering and monitoring that digital coupon that lives in a retailers' app.

Analyst: What types of digital incentives does Neptune offer? Does the company also provide cash back and rebates apart from coupons?

Expert: Yes. They own a site called Checkout 51, which is a cashback app, which is again a shopping platform, so cashback, digital incentives. They do still operate in the physical paper coupons that are called FSIs. That's very much declining by the second, but they are technically still in that business. Those are the primary ones. They have some others that are similar to digital coupons, load-to-card coupons, but that's really the vast majority.

Analyst: How does Neptune measure whether an incentive actually drives an incremental purchase?

Expert: We see through our integrations with the retailers when those coupons are redeemed and where they are redeemed. We process that data and then we also work with [a] third-party research company that has relationships with the first-party sales data. [It] knows every product that's sold everywhere. We match those things up and say, "What is the purchasing habits prior to the incentive?" "The incentive was during this period in these stores."

Analyst: Where do consumers actually access and redeem the offers? What are the offers primarily on? What are the products that have digital incentives on them, given that Neptune services a lot of industries and products?

Expert: Primarily, again, most of the CPG. The other secondary types of products like pharma and cellular, it's really primarily food and non-food CPG brands that utilise those. Those coupons primarily live on retailer apps, so CVS app, Walgreens app, Albertsons app and so on. They are individual by retailer and retail-specific.

Analyst: Could you provide a use case for in-store media?

Expert: This is physical signage that lives in-store and there are a couple of different products that they have within the four walls of the store. Primarily, ShelfTalk, which is an 8x16 plastic sign that sits at shelf, usually at product. Again, this is to draw attention and highlight specific attributes that the brand wants to communicate at the shelf. Similar products are things called FloorTalk, which are big stickers that sit on the floor. There are other elements that, depending on the retailer, can hang within the store, like signage, whether it be standalone signage or other physical banners and those types of things, could be end-aisle displays as well, which are, think about like a freestanding cardboard stand that may have product on it, may have signage on it, anything that's physical in terms of signage within the store.

Analyst: Where in the store are the advertisements primarily placed?

Expert: Really, all over. They can be at the entrance, at the front doors. They could be, one area was on shopping carts. So, we think about a grocery store, on the shopping cart there's a physical sign, in the aisle within the store, on the floor within the store, really depending on certain areas of the speciality, like the produce section or the bakery or those sorts of things.

Analyst: Is Neptune responsible for physically placing and managing the advertising materials?

Expert: Yes. Neptune has a large like, the last number I remember was, about 3,000 individuals who go and both install them at certain times and then remove them and it's on a store-by-store basis. For instance, CVS has, let's say, 8,000 stores across the country. An advertiser may only purchase 1,000 of them or 2,000. So, specifically based on where those stores are, NRS employees will go in and either install or remove them, basing on the contracted time they are supposed to be up.

Analyst: Could you provide a use case for digital media?

Expert: This is primarily digital advertising that may be on the open web or within app. This is, again, targeted media that is usually targeted around specific retailers or shopping habits, finding audiences that are connected to specific products and then delivering again digital media banners and so on within the Internet environment.

Analyst: What digital media channels does Neptune use to reach shoppers?

Expert: I would say primarily open-web, some entries into e-mail networks and, I believe, they have tried to crack into social a little bit, but it's primarily open-web display.

Analyst: What data does the company use for targeting consumers?

Expert: Usually, we will partner with third parties like The Trade Desk or others that will actually own the targeting and we will arbitrage that media. We will buy it from a third party. Neptune will mark it up in terms of a cost perspective and then run that in conjunction with a media campaign.

Analyst: How is using Neptune different from buying advertising directly through Google, Meta or through retailers' media network?

Expert: It's not very different. In fact, the only difference really if a brand were to want to buy digital media, I'd be surprised if they went to Neptune specifically just for digital media. The value proposition is you have got digital incentives. You have got in-store. You are running this whole campaign. So, to run a holistic one-stop shop, Neptune will provide those services, in this case, digital media, that are part of a larger campaign. But again, in terms of differentiating from a Trade Desk or a Google or whoever, they are really not. In fact, they are probably more expensive. The one actually slightly different thing I would say to that is Neptune does have a lot of data that says you should buy in and around these specific stores. The actual location targeting that we do for in-store media can apply to the digital media, so that piece of it is a little bit unique.

Analyst: Could you provide a use case for digital out-of-home?

Expert: Yes. Similar to digital media, we utilise existing networks of digital out-of-home screens, whether it be gas stations or gyms or nearby digital screens that are near retail locations and then deliver media through those. Again, not very different or unique from a lot of other media companies in that market, but a little bit unique in that you can purchase that as part of a larger programme that would include in-store, digital incentives and so on.

Analyst: Where are Neptune's digital advertising screens primarily located? Are the screens located near retail stores or near other high-traffic locations?

Expert: Primarily near store. That's really the main focus is in and around retail. We don't really do much, let's say, at airports or in restaurants or those types of things where other digital home networks are. We are in and around and, sometimes, even in inside the four walls of a store. Like CVS has a great network of screens near their pharmacy, so we tap into those kinds of things.

Analyst: Does the company own the screens or does it have partnerships for them?

Expert: Partnerships. My understanding is that we don't own, especially in the digital out-of-home, any of them. It's really a partnership. In many cases, we are sharing that inventory. If they are, let's say, CVS, there are five slots where an advertiser could go, Neptune may own 2-3 of them. The retailer may own 1-2 of them and then some other [player would own the rest].

Analyst: Does Neptune sell all the products individually or does the company also sell them in a bundle?

Expert: Both. There are plenty of programmes that are sold individually where you just buy in-store or just buy digital incentives and there are many that bought all products included.

Analyst: What percentage of times do you think the products are bundled and sold vs individually sold?

Expert: If there are four products here, I would say three of them, digital incentives, in-store and digital media or digital out-of-home, one of those two, would be probably in the 61-70% of programmes because they are usually with a larger, so again, a Coke, a Pepsi, a Unilever would certainly buy multiple assets or products as part of an overall campaign and the smaller ones will say, "I can just afford in-store," or, "I can just do digital incentives."

Which of Neptune's four core segments do you anticipate being the fastest growing and potentially driving the largest share of revenue in future?

It's very efficient in terms of EBITDA. In-store will continue to be a large piece of their overall revenue, but the EBITDA on that is very difficult because there are so many physical elements to it, but in terms of the fastest-growing, I would say digital incentives. What has the AAGR been of the retail media and shopper marketing industry over the past three years – less than 0%, 0-5%, 6-10%, 11-20% or greater than 20%?

Expert: I would say 20%-plus. Overall, as an industry, it's been very fast-paced. It's just been growing. We can get into some more specifics, but it's been growing very fast in terms of an overall industry.

What do you think have been the 1-2 key drivers of historical market growth?

Expert: I think it's the need for advertisers and CPG brands to deliver accountability. In the past, the reason this has been growing so fast is its stealing share from large national media, TV budgets and those types of things. What's been happening is brands have been saying, "I need to quantify my advertising spend," and retail media and shopper marketing is very good at being able to say, "I spent X and I saw X increase in sales," or didn't, but being able to match those two things together. So, I think that accountability and transactional elements of analysis is driving that growth. The CMOs and brand managers are saying, "I need to put my money where I know it's working and move it away from places I'm not sure if it's working," and retail media and shopper marketing are really good at doing that.

What do you think the industry's AAGR might be over the next three years?

Expert: I do think it's going to slow across the overall industry. So, I would say 11-20%, probably in that range, or maybe 6-10%, but I still think in the next three years, I could see another high teens and then slowing over time.

What do you think will be the 1-2 key drivers of future market growth? Why is its growth also going to slightly slow down, in your opinion?

Expert: I think in terms of why it's going to continue to grow, the retail environment for CPG brands has been very challenging. Even coming several years out of COVID now, there are still lots of changes within retail. Retail, in general, has been a very difficult market for brands to predict. It's been very volatile. I think the ability for brands to put targeted dollars toward increasing sales or stealing share from a competitor will continue to drive this industry well. I think the decline, again, I wouldn't say decline. The slowing of that growth, I think, will be largely due to a maximisation of the platform. There's just not as much, the number of retailers, the amount of inventory that's able to be utilised does have a limit. I think that growth is going to max out sooner than later.

To what degree do you think the industry is correlated to economic cyclicality? Do you think it's a counter-cyclical, very cyclical, somewhat cyclical, somewhat resilient or resilient market?

Expert: I would say it's somewhat cyclical. I don't know that it's super resilient. I was going to say very cyclical, but I would say somewhat because it's very economy-driven. Then, depending on the brand, they will say, "Hey, our sales are down." Some will just say, "Our sales are down. We can't spend any more money," or some will say, "Our sales are down, we need to spend more money to get those sales up," but I would say it's somewhat cyclical in terms of macroeconomics.

Analyst: Do you think retail media and shopper marketing solutions are need-to-have or good-to-have?

Expert: Personally, I think it's a need to have. I think largely because consumers are also less predictable and more finicky than they used to be. The other part of the need-to-have is your competitors are doing it. I think that the defensive position that a lot of brands will have is super important and this will continue to be an area that battles will be fought between Cokes and Pepsis and being able to do that. I think emerging brands also are starting to utilise this. Again, it's expensive in terms of the overall industry, but I think that's an area where you will see smaller brands will try and get a foothold in.

Analyst: What do you pre-empt the situation to be during an economic downturn when prices are high and people are cutting budgets? Do you think retail media and shopper marketing solutions are still going to be a part of customer budgets?

Expert: I do. I think it will be smaller, their budgets will shrink, but I think in terms of them eliminating it as a tactic or as a marketing channel, it will continue to be there. One of the other things that this channel does very well overall is protect against not only competitors, but private label. Private label is a big concern for big national brands. If you can get your CVS-branded Advil, it's going to be a lot cheaper than your Advil. There's a place that those brands need to continue to defend against that.

Based on public information, what technological evolutions or innovations do you think we should anticipate in the industry over the next 3-5 years?

Expert: I think AI will or should, within the overall industry, I don't know that Neptune will do this, but in general, I think AI could have a very important impact on predicting both consumer behaviour and consumer habits as well as basket and shopper assortment, what people are buying together and being able to drive smarter and more targeted campaigns and media around programmes, more so than the current human analytics that are being put toward it. I think that's going to be a big piece of it. AI is one. I would say another is the continued evolution of digital screens. We often think of digital screens as these big, large units that are at the front of a store or big TV hanging in a retail environment. I think we will see technology with smaller screens at shelf. The ability to drive networked digital screens inside the store, I think, will be an opportunity anyway for both retailers and companies.

Analyst: Could you provide a couple of examples of where you think AI can be used in the industry? In what processes could AI play a role?

Expert: I think a lot of consumer behaviour analysis, basket assortment, what people are buying together. They are buying peanut butter and jelly together, but there are lots of complexities in terms of people buying certain categories together. You think about a drugstore. There are lots of things in that store. Are we seeing baskets, assortments, meaning what else they are purchasing together with medication, but they are also buying school supplies. I think AI will help identify opportunities for brands, both in terms of who they are buying, what they are buying and how often they are buying it. [Also], I think a lot with purchasing cycles, how long does it take to go through that tube of toothpaste or that bottle of shampoo? AI, I think, will be an important driver in answering a lot of those questions.

Do you see any downside risks that might materially impact market growth over the next three years? Do you anticipate any potential regulation changes or tech disruptions in the retail media and shopper marketing industry?

Expert: I think, from a regulation standpoint, not really. There's one area that is within digital signage and in-store signage, I don't see any regulatory elements there. Where I do see and it's starting to pop up in California and I think in one or two other states, digital incentives, making digital incentives more equitable so that the need or requirement to have paper versions of digital incentives is becoming a little bit of a thing. It's not quite an issue yet, but over the next three years, it's something that could be an issue. Then, the other is predatory pricing. A lot of retailers through digital incentives will have different coupon values for different individuals. You might get a coupon that's USD 1 off. How fragmented do you consider the retail media and shopper marketing industry to be? Do 2-5 players capture the majority of market share? Is it a monopoly? Is it fragmented or highly fragmented?

Again, the Neptunes and Inmars and Acostas and a couple of others, they have got a pretty strong hold in there and I think it'd be difficult to unseat them.

One other thing, just to go back on one segment about competitors in terms of others, the one challenger in this is retail-agnostic competitors, especially for digital incentives. We often talk about Neptune and Inmar and Acosta and a couple of others in this larger coupon world, but Fetch and Ibotta have really taken a big chunk out of the industry over the last few years and I see that continuing to grow and taking [share], at least in the digital incentive side. Not so much in the in-store or digital media side, but in terms of digital incentives, Ibotta and Fetch will definitely continue to grow their share of the business.
