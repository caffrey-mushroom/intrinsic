Overview

The discussion focused on the data center power industry, particularly the challenges and implications of community pushback on power equipment purchases. The expert, a seasoned consultant in data center development, provided insights into the regulatory and logistical hurdles faced by the industry. The conversation touched on recent regulatory actions, such as the Texas Governor's halt on state permits for new data centers, and the broader implications for data center development.

Key Insights

The expert emphasized that the regulatory challenges in data center development, such as power procurement and environmental permitting, are longstanding issues. The recent regulatory actions are seen as reiterations of existing processes rather than new obstacles. Community opposition to data centers is multifaceted, involving concerns about zoning, noise, water usage, and grid reliability. These factors can lead to project delays, increased costs, or outright halts. The demand for power equipment remains strong despite regulatory pauses, with significant lead times for electrical gear and generators due to high demand.

JI: Well, with turbines, it's kind of different space concerns. A turbine, you can probably generate a particular amount of power, but you've got a big giant thing that has emissions and noise and is unpopular for those reasons. If you need the same amount of power from, say, a fuel cell array, it's shorter, it's quieter. But you're going to have to spread that out over a substantially bigger footprint just because it's a very different type of technology. So if you've got plenty of room, but you are more sensitive to noise, then, yeah, you could go with a fuel cell deployment because, great, we've got the space, but we have neighbors and so they're not going to let us do that. Also, too, the permitting is different for fuel cells than a gas generator. Their zoning is going to be different, that's a case-by-case, county-by- county, municipality-by-municipality, but the whole thing with turbines is that it's a giant combustion engine and so that comes with all those things that we said: noise, emissions, and that usually comes with a lot more zoning restrictions and ordinances that you cannot comply with, whereas the fuel cells, probably can comply with a lot of those easier, but you just got to factor in the other consequences of changing over to that.

JI: I think generally it is more difficult because of the... for a couple of reasons. Data centers are large usually and so you're asking, traditionally getting any large thing approved in an area that has sensitivities has never been easy. So now just your typical ask is for something that's traditionally been hard to get approved. But there is also that sort of just general headline opposition out there to data centers. If you were to build a warehouse or something that's the exact same size and all that, people may not, but for the size thing or the views, they may not take issue, but because it's data centers and, whether people are concerned about AI or they're concerned about the energy consumption that makes these more of a hot topic. And so I think that has definitely been across the board, a new unique thing that the people that approve these, the city councilors, mayors, the local elected officials, they may have no problem with it. But if there's this general thing out there, they've got to... they're going to oppose it. And so there's a political thing that the way data centers are polling is, especially if you're near an election or something like that. It may generate so much benefit for the community and taxes and things like that. But they don't want to touch that issue because it's been such a hot button. And so the safe thing to do is defer, delay, vote against it, things like that. And that has certainly made things difficult.
