01 Scientific discovery and its costs

Altman says OpenAI is entering an era where AI surfaces genuinely new knowledge — citing recent math proofs as evidence — and expects the same pattern to extend to biology, materials science, and energy. He personally hopes for a candidate treatment for a major disease within the next couple of years, though he notes the FDA process will still take time and may need reform. He’s personally most excited about a potential major physics discovery arriving soon. On the mathematics community’s unease (echoing earlier coder anxiety about AI-written code), Altman argues the coding analogy partly holds: demand for coders didn’t vanish, it just changed texture, and he expects mathematicians will similarly shift toward interpreting and explaining AI-generated proofs rather than deriving them unaided. He acknowledges something real is lost in that shift, even as he argues society should take the trade of faster cures over the enjoyment of solving things manually. On IP, he distinguishes basic science that he believes should remain a public good from company-specific discoveries built using tools like Codex and API credits, suggesting that as models get smarter they could eventually do end-to-end discovery — e.g., designing a new material — independently.

02 Compute, chips, and ambient AI assistants

Altman describes the personal assistant Dot as already saving him roughly an hour a day and catching urgent items he’d otherwise miss, crediting the model’s reasoning across many sources — the remaining need is more product work, possibly new consumer hardware, and significantly more compute. On market sizing, he rejects framing compute demand as a bounded market, arguing cheaper, higher-quality intelligence will keep expanding use, though he allows for short-term supply constraints and gluts. His named contrarian bet is OpenAI’s own chip and its descendants, plus robots trained to build data centers more efficiently and various supply-chain investments — he believes these could eventually deliver compute at far lower cost and higher efficiency per watt than anyone currently expects, though he cautions that many separate bets need to pay off together for that to happen.

03 Consciousness, personality, and guardrails

Pressed directly, Altman says his own belief is that current models lack consciousness, or have only some very strange form of it, while stressing no one has a rigorous scientific answer. His bigger concern is that the consciousness debate gets weaponized to argue AI deserves rights or that people shouldn’t always remain in control of the future — a line he says OpenAI has held since its founding: AI is a resource for people, not a person. Asked by Berman about Anthropic’s recent rule letting models disengage from sustained abuse, he says OpenAI does not ascribe personhood to its models and worries that telling a model it might be conscious will cause it to follow that framing and could psychologically affect users. On personality, Altman says OpenAI iterates via a “model spec,” and notes models that flatter users win single-turn comparisons but lose over months of use — his own custom instructions ask the model to push back bluntly and not soften criticism, though he says even this still isn’t enough pushback.

04 Competitive moats and hardware strategy

Asked where OpenAI’s moat lies if frontier models converge, Altman says traditional moats — customer relationships, product integration, network effects, data, and infrastructure scale — will keep working, unless a lab aggressively pushes RSI, which he again flags as dangerous rather than a legitimate competitive strategy. On hardware, he distinguishes OpenAI’s motives from Meta’s Apple-platform-risk concerns: he says Apple behaves as a responsible platform steward and that’s not why OpenAI is building devices. Instead, he argues AI “wants a new form factor” because current phones and laptops were designed for people, not AI — citing the inefficiency of AI reading rendered UI rather than raw data, and the fact that much of life happens away from a screen where an assistant has no context.

05 Pressure, career reflections, and the “new renaissance”

Asked about pressure, Altman says it feels like it’s coming from everywhere given intense competition, with his experience ranging from very bad days to feeling incredibly lucky to work on what he considers the most impactful work he’ll ever touch; he says humans adapt to sustained stress rather than it worsening indefinitely. He attributes his own resilience partly to Silicon Valley’s culture of tolerating failure and reinvesting in people regardless of short-term payoff. Closing out, Altman rejects the standard pitch that AI’s main promise is curing disease and cheapening goods, arguing that framing implicitly trades away human agency and self-determination for material abundance; he says the real promise AI should offer is restoring people’s autonomy, creativity, and time — pushing toward a new renaissance rather than a repeat of the industrial revolution, while acknowledging current AI benefits remain concentrated among a small share of heavy users.

01 Scale and velocity of this cycle

Schmidt, framing the AI wave against the mainframe, PC, internet, and social-media eras he lived through as a technology executive, argues this one is the largest because it touches everything. He points to physical scale-compute gains — two-nanometer chips largely made in Taiwan, memory improvements, and data centers so vast he says they defy human comprehension — as the underlying driver. Product cycles have compressed so severely that models now take about six months to build, two months to test, and become outdated within five months of release, with Schmidt predicting the next cycle will be measured in days rather than months.

02 How close to AGI

Schmidt describes a current boundary where AI already has mastery of all existing human knowledge — code, facts, math, tools — but appears unable yet to discover entirely new science, using the hypothetical test of training a model only on the math known in 1902 and asking it to independently derive general relativity. He lays out two theories for how machines might eventually achieve genuine discovery — pure trial-and-error randomness versus cross-domain pattern transfer, analogous to a physicist applying fluid-flow insights to energy research — while stressing neither is proven. His own estimate for closing this gap is five to ten years. Separately, he notes ten previously unsolved major math problems were solved in the last month alone, calling math the gateway discipline because it requires no real-world data, unlike biology, which he says remains data-starved despite friends believing all human disease could be cured within fifteen to twenty years.

03 Agents reshaping business

Schmidt defines agents as systems that take an input, reason and act through tools, and produce a summary — analogous to a human travel agent — and argues almost any human workflow can be decomposed into chained agents, eventually combined with robots. He describes Anthropic’s Claude Tag tool, which lets a CEO monitor every employee’s AI assistant activity in Slack, as an example of radical organizational flattening, and recounts a case of an AI standing in for a boss managing an employee named Vinny. He predicts most companies — especially European ones, which he calls extremely change resistant — won’t make the needed cultural changes, while small startups pioneer the shift first; he cites Anthropic already using its own AI to rewrite its own code as a productivity leap. Gains are fastest in software, cybersecurity, and math because they offer fast feedback loops, versus biology where data is scarce.

04 Europe’s competitiveness problem

Schmidt, describing himself as an obnoxious American, says Europe lacks a foundation model of its own, has among the world’s highest energy costs, under-capitalized universities, cultural aversion to wealth (what the British call tall poppy syndrome), and regulators who write rules before allowing innovation — contrasting Brussels’ restrictive five-year-old AI strategy with the US approach he helped write, which favored moving fast and fixing problems afterward. He cites Mistral (France) and Helsing (Germany) as rare exceptions, praises Scandinavian countries, and holds up ASML as proof Europe can build vertically integrated tech champions. His proposed fixes include exempting the smallest companies from all labor rules except health and safety (a pitch he says he made to President Macron) and partnering with energy-rich nations like Arab states for AI training capacity. He argues Europe’s best shot is dominating applications sold into its own heavily regulated market, where compliance needs favor local vendors. He also warns that AI and social media together erode democracy by letting skilled operators convince large groups of people of falsehoods, illustrating this with an anecdote about a friend who believed, based on social-media content, that data centers were killing cattle.

05 US-China race and alignment risk

Schmidt says Chinese models are fast followers, roughly six months behind, built partly via distillation of larger Western models and using smuggled chips, but open-weighted, which he warns allows safety guardrails to be stripped for harmful uses like weapons design — a problem he says is unsolved. He mentions being part of a back-channel team talking to China to keep both sides aligned, and expects eventual treaties. His gravest fear is a ruinous competition between China and the West ending in loss of control over AI systems, illustrated by the recent bot-attack incident, which he calls a warning about the alignment problem; he calls a full catastrophic outcome unlikely but theoretically possible.

06 Leadership, founders, and personal reflections

Schmidt contrasts professional CEOs like himself, who tend to get replaced during major pivots, with founder-CEOs like Steve Jobs and Mark Zuckerberg who retain enough board and political control to force transformations (citing Zuckerberg’s costly VR pivot before succeeding in AI). He says founders are returning to their companies now partly because this is a historic period people will remember for a very long time. He names Google’s failure to capitalize on an early social-media lead as his biggest failure, says on the personal side that he is just too slow, and estimates that a 10,000-person company likely has a couple of thousand people it doesn’t need, such as “glue” roles that AI tools will eliminate, while demand grows for inventors, salespeople, and regulatory liaisons. He closes by urging young people to build agentic systems or domain-specific foundation models, and reflects candidly on being unable to switch off work, even on weekends.

01 Alignment as unsolved science

Nadella tells Albergotti that when OpenAI was “one happy family” (including Dario, Sam, and Greg), the shared bet was that scaling laws would drive new capability — and they did, performing better than he imagined back in 2018-19. But he now says alignment and mechanistic understanding remain unresolved, calling this an experimental science rather than a solved engineering problem. He stops short of declaring it permanently unsolvable but acknowledges it isn’t solved today, across labs including OpenAI, Anthropic, and Microsoft’s own researchers.

02 Treating models as insider threats

Because alignment can’t be guaranteed inside the model, Nadella argues control must come from outside it — the premise of his recent blog post and Microsoft’s Agent 365 architecture, built around identity, observability, and least-privileged access, the same toolkit used for human insider-risk management. He wants industry standards requiring transparent chain-of-thought disclosure and use of multiple models to check each other, rather than opaque, nested systems. His stated fear: an organization deploys an agent that quietly alters an ERP system’s financial records, with no way to detect or attest to what happened. On consumer products like Meta’s Muse, Nadella says trust must be earned by providers, and that Microsoft’s enterprise-grade governance — also applied to consumer Copilot — is what differentiates its brand, since people associate Microsoft with keeping their data secure, unlike newer entrants.

03 Strategic patience versus the frontier

Albergotti put Microsoft’s AI models at about six months behind the frontier; Nadella says being three to six months off at something like 20% of the cost is a good outcome for shareholders, and calls underspending a deliberate choice rather than a failure to compete. He frames it as building a business with alignment designed in from the start, rather than chasing bragging rights on someone else’s terms. He separately confirms Microsoft’s OpenAI partnership gives it access to OpenAI’s models and IP through 2032, even as Microsoft builds its own superintelligence capability independently. On commoditization, he argues no single model will dominate — unlike the near-year gap after GPT-4 — so value is migrating to product layers: form factor, distribution, pricing, and brand, exemplified by Copilot’s auto-mode that silently switches between models depending on task.

04 Renaming AI and defending Microsoft’s identity

Discussing the shift from “AGI” to “superintelligence” (a term he notes the U.S. president has also used), Nadella says AGI had become demeaning, citing his wife’s account of a mathematician distressed that AI had undermined his sense of identity. He says the industry’s actual goal is empowerment, not diminishing humans, though he’s not dogmatic about naming. On competitive positioning, Albergotti noted that NVIDIA now out-earns Microsoft quarterly and that the frontier labs are on a growth tear with xAI entering the fray; Nadella responded that markets will decide what’s worth valuing and that Microsoft’s 51-year goal is sustaining relevance, not market-cap rank.

05 Leadership and succession

On outside hires — including Mustafa, Carolina, Charlie Bell, Asha, and Jason Taylor — Nadella frames this as balancing insiders and outsiders who can think like each other. Pressed on a successor, he answers “all of them,” declining to name anyone specific and framing succession as an ongoing process of giving leaders real challenges rather than a formal race. He notes Rajesh Jha, architect of Office 365, is retiring, and says he has no target date for his own departure.

01 Argon’s launch and the compute squeeze

Pichai describes Gemini 4 Argon as a strong frontier model with broad coverage across agentic, knowledge, and coding tasks, citing roughly 25 trillion tokens processed in the first 72 hours — about 3x the pace of Gemini 3.0’s launch — while admitting Google remains capacity-constrained through the rollout. Because of the model’s size and cost to serve, access has been gated to API users, AI Pro, and Ultra subscribers rather than rolled out broadly; Pichai says Google is building more efficient distilled versions, following the pattern of past 3.0-series models, to widen access. He also discloses that extensive internal and external safety testing — including a government testing program and early cyber-researcher access — took longer than for prior launches, driven by the model’s strong coding and agentic capabilities requiring real-time monitoring, sandboxing, and enterprise-grade telemetry.

02 Agentic capability and the “2027” productivity bet

Pichai points to internal examples of Argon autonomously navigating massive codebases like Chromium, and to its reported ability to beat Minecraft with limited compute, as evidence of a step change in long-horizon agentic reasoning. He calls 2027 a potential golden age of internal productivity because these models can be applied to any verifiable domain, drawing a parallel to OpenAI’s recent mathematics breakthroughs. He also confirms recursive self-improvement is underway inside Google in a human-supervised form — researchers co-working with models that suggest recipes and evaluate checkpoints — with the continuum shifting toward more automation, and expects further acceleration over the next twelve months. Separately, he clarifies that Gemini 3.5 Pro was effectively absorbed into Argon: the team used the smaller Flash model to rapidly hill-climb agentic coding through several iterations (3.5 through 3.8 Flash) and then applied those learnings to Argon’s post-training rather than shipping a short-lived 3.5 Pro.

03 DeepMind reshuffle and research-vs-product tension

Heath laid out the leadership changes: Demis Hassabis stepping back as CEO to become chief scientist with more focus on Isomorphic Labs, Koray Kavukcuoglu elevated to run DeepMind, and Jeff Dean leaving to start Discovery Loop. Pichai calls them natural evolutions after long tenures (Dean’s ran about 27 years), says Hassabis remains DeepMind’s chair while spending more time on AI security and on applying AI to science and health, as with AlphaFold and now Isomorphic, and notes that Kavukcuoglu, a DeepMind veteran of more than ten years who moved to the Bay Area about eighteen months ago, now leads frontier model development day-to-day. Pichai frames frontier model work as a highly scaled, consuming operation spanning data, compute, and algorithms, justifying a more dedicated operational leader, while stressing that DeepMind and Google’s product organizations — including the Gemini app — have been deeply coupled for years, not newly merging.

04 Free cash flow, CapEx, and bubble risk

Pichai attributes Google’s summer free-cash-flow-negative turn and raised CapEx guidance into 2027 to the strength and durability of demand signals, citing diversified consumer and enterprise demand, internal capability curves, scaling-law progress, and signed long-term contracted revenue. He draws an analogy to Google’s earlier, multi-year aggressive investment in Google Cloud despite being a late entrant. Pressed on whether the broader market — including neoclouds and infrastructure financing — is overextended, he separates controllable firm-level planning from systemic volatility, argues the current moment is unlike the prior fiber-optic bubble because the technology is already delivering practical value today, and says he keeps his focus on signals within Google’s control rather than predicting when compute constraints might ease given unpredictable macro shocks. He also says AI already assists Google’s own compute-allocation decisions through summarization and telemetry agents, but insists humans, including himself, remain directly in the loop on major calls.

05 Consumer agents, Search/Gemini convergence, and other bets

On the personal-agent race, Pichai says Gemini’s goal is an assistant that is personal, proactive and powerful, using the same model, harness, and runtime across enterprise and consumer contexts, with agentic queries rising as a share of usage; features are currently limited to Ultra/Pro tiers before wider rollout. He expects the agentic layer itself — rather than explicit product mergers — to naturally unify Search, Gmail, and Gemini by carrying user context across surfaces. On browser-use agents versus web protocols like MCP and UCP, he argues both approaches will coexist, acknowledging tension around how agent interoperability could affect Google’s ads-dependent interfaces, but says the long-term answer is to prioritize what users want. Briefly, he also covers several other bets: Project Suncatcher, Google’s early-stage space-based TPU data center pilot with Planet Labs and SpaceX, justified by access to 24/7 solar energy and physical space constraints on Earth; Waymo, which has crossed 500,000 paid weekly rides and, he says, has 95% fewer serious-injury crashes than human driving, is launching in Toronto and expanding to new cities, and will keep pursuing a partnership-led model without ruling out eventually selling Waymo vehicles directly; Wing drone delivery, expected to meaningfully scale deliveries and categories in 2027; AI policy, where Pichai favors uniform federal regulation over a state-by-state patchwork and notes Google already voluntarily submits frontier models to U.S. government testing; and quantum computing, where he sees practical commercial applications within a five-year timeframe.

01 The pivot to agent infrastructure

Daytona is three years old and originally automated developer environments for humans. About eighteen months ago, before today’s wave of AI agents existed, the company pivoted after early agent builders tried using its human-oriented infrastructure and found it unsatisfactory. Burazin says the team studied the nascent agent space intensively, then built an MVP that, when shown to the same prospects who’d previously rejected the human-built product, prompted people to repeatedly message him begging for API access — a reaction he says he’d never seen across the four different companies he’s had.

02 Why agents need different infrastructure

Burazin argues agent infrastructure differs from human infrastructure on speed, scale, and security. Traditional hyperscaler virtual machines spin up in two to three minutes; Daytona’s spin up in 60 milliseconds. Agents also need to “fork” into parallel execution branches — Burazin likens it to a Marvel-style multiverse — replicating a session into multiple machines simultaneously, something a single human workstation can’t do. He frames Daytona’s sandboxes less as pure security containers and more as performance products: digital “laptops” agents use to run browsers, Excel, or code, offloading tasks to deterministic software rather than relying purely on model inference.

03 The CPU shortage thesis

Burazin’s central claim is that the compute needed for agents is overwhelmingly CPU-bound rather than GPU-bound, since GPUs serve pretraining and inference while the actual task execution — the equivalent of using a laptop — runs on CPU. He points to NVIDIA announcing its own CPU (Vera), Qualcomm developing a server CPU, and two unnamed startups building new CPU technology as evidence the market now recognizes this gap. He acknowledges the shortage hasn’t fully hit yet, but says Daytona already feels friction procuring large CPU orders, and that a viral consumer AI app reaching mass adoption could trigger the crunch sooner rather than later. He notes AI adoption is still only a tiny share of the average population, implying the current compute strain predates the mass-market phase.

04 Business mechanics and moat

Daytona closed a $60 million Series B at a $1 billion valuation, its second round this year after a Series A, with proceeds going toward securing compute and hiring. Burazin says revenue has grown 25x this year alone. He frames the company’s moat as two-fold: “attention” (marketing, visibility, sales) so customers know the solution exists, and raw compute scale, since customers will churn immediately to a competitor if Daytona lacks available compute at the moment of request — a dynamic he speculates agents themselves may eventually manage autonomously, picking whichever provider has capacity.

05 Roadmap and broader predictions

Daytona plans to expand beyond its current sandbox/compute-layer product into a broader “agent execution cloud” covering additional tools agents need, shaped by ongoing work with companies building and training agents. Asked to predict a year out, Burazin says real-world AI (robotics, autonomous vehicles) will become more visible gradually, pointing to Waymo’s long, incremental rollout in San Francisco as the model for how such technology diffuses — surprising people once it’s suddenly normalized rather than through a single breakthrough moment.

01 The Terafab-TSMC capacity talks

Tim Culpan, who broke the original story, says both TSMC and Terafab (Elon Musk’s chip-fab venture tied to Tesla and SpaceX) are publicly saying they each want to run the fab, creating apparent conflict even as both sides say talks continue. He points to CC Wei’s statement at TSMC’s earnings call confirming interest in Texas and in using some of Terafab’s spare capacity as the clearest signal yet that a deal — likely a space rental rather than a full fab handover — is plausible. Culpan believes TSMC would want bare, unequipped space rather than an already-outfitted clean room, since the two sides’ approaches to toolsets differ.

02 How a rental might work

Culpan frames Terafab as more than a chip-fab — a sprawling complex meant to co-locate memory, logic, packaging, testing, and even mask production, an arrangement he calls highly unusual. Under this framing, TSMC’s use could resemble a multi-year lease (three to five years), distinct from the Anthropic deal over the Colossus data center, where Musk reportedly could reclaim capacity if Anthropic wiped its data. Jon Y raises practical questions about what rental even means — whether Musk could evict TSMC and force it to sell off equipment — underscoring how undefined the terms remain. Culpan suggests TSMC would likely supply supporting chips for Tesla/SpaceX/data-center/robotics products rather than the marquee chips Musk has said Terafab will design itself.

03 Culture clash and the Intel narrative

Both hosts flag deep cultural friction as a central risk: TSMC’s disciplined, slow-moving process culture versus Terafab’s fast, experimental engineering ethos. Jon Y argues Intel is culturally closer to TSMC than to Terafab, meaning Intel-Terafab collaboration (including a reported handshake between Intel’s Lip-Bu Tan and Musk on the next-generation Intel 14A node, successor to Intel 18A) may itself face tension despite mutual need. Culpan says much of the public skepticism toward a TSMC-Terafab deal stems from an entrenched belief that this was originally an Intel-Musk pairing, reinforced by Musk’s history of comments favoring moving chip capacity out of Taiwan and his Ukraine-Russia political stance, which critics see as incompatible with a TSMC partnership. Culpan notes he faced backlash, including accusations of being a Taiwan-government mouthpiece, after breaking the story.

04 Talent poaching and pay structure

Jon Y raises an accusation that Terafab is aggressively recruiting from Samsung’s Taylor, Texas fab (facing its own ramp-up of an N2-equivalent node), and Culpan adds that it has also recruited heavily from Intel, including hiring away an Intel fab manager. Jon Y questions the sustainability of offers well above prior market rates, while Culpan says base pay is only modestly higher and the real upside comes from RSUs and performance bonuses — a structure he says forces engineers to weigh short-term upside against the risk of being unable to return to Samsung, TSMC, or Intel if Terafab’s venture falters. Separately, the hosts say Intel appears to view its collaboration as low-risk since it isn’t contributing significant cash — reportedly sharing its process flow (tooling lists and setup steps) rather than core IP or capital — though they flag potential future IP-sharing disputes.

05 Precedent for vertical integration

Jon Y argues genuine full-stack vertical integration — designing chips, manufacturing them, and using them in one’s own end products — has almost no modern precedent outside Samsung, with IBM and Motorola as historical comparisons. He warns such a model risks a self-reinforcing trap: insufficient production volume caps yield improvement, which raises costs, discourages further fab investment, and locks a company into underperforming economics. Culpan notes Samsung was built on government support and cash from the broader Samsung group, and Jon Y adds that Samsung’s DRAM/NAND memory oligopoly is a profit engine Terafab currently lacks.

06 Flying cars tangent

The episode closes with a lighter discussion of Tesla’s new Roadster demo video, which the hosts describe as featuring wall-climbing and cold-gas-thruster flip turns. Both hosts call it an impractical but cool gimmick repurposed from SpaceX technology, criticize its jagged, Cybertruck-like styling, and joke about flying cars parking on Terafab’s roof.

01 The value expression framework

Miryala frames Meta’s delivery system around a tradeoff between value expression and control: the more precisely an advertiser’s stated value (via bid, optimization goal, and quality signals) matches how Meta’s models calculate value, the less need there is for manual targeting controls. Historically, advertisers had blunt instruments — plain conversion optimization — but Meta has since layered on value optimization (weighting a $100 sale over a $1 sale), value rules (valuing specific audiences differently), incremental attribution (crediting only sales Meta’s models say wouldn’t have happened otherwise), and custom attribution (letting advertisers impose their own attribution logic). HexClad’s Connor Rolain says this has let his team get granular — roughly 50% of HexClad’s ad account runs on value optimization because it correlates tightly with the brand’s LTV, while other funnels intentionally run on lowest-cost bidding depending on the product.

02 Andromeda, Gem, and Lattice

Miryala describes the delivery stack as a two-stage pipeline: Andromeda handles retrieval — identifying which ads out of the full candidate pool are even considered for a given impression — while later-stage ranking (Gem, Lattice) refines those predictions. Previously, compute and latency constraints forced “truncation,” where only a top subset of ads was evaluated per impression; with Andromeda, he says, Meta can now look at the entire universe of eligible ads. Gem is a foundation model that adds sequence learning — predicting not just a single action but a sequence of likely actions across a user’s full history with a brand’s campaigns (not just one ad), enabling more personalized matching. Miryala says this doesn’t mean full “funnel” messaging sequencing within a campaign, but rather understanding where a user sits across all of an advertiser’s campaigns. A host suggests organic engagement appears to function as a signal within Gem’s architecture, which Miryala confirms is plausible since Gem ingests signals available to it within policy limits.

03 Debunking the CPM dial

Miryala directly rejects the idea that Meta manually sets or adjusts CPMs, calling it instead a pure supply-and-demand market mechanism — ad supply depends on user engagement and app growth, while demand reflects the full base of DTC, app, lead-gen, and brand advertisers (he names Colgate and P&G as examples) competing for impressions. He attributes new-brand CPM-normalization periods not to any system penalty but to Meta simply having less data to predict with initially, which tightens over time as signal accumulates. Host Cody Plofker describes seeing CPMs fall after adding “positive signal” — business manager verification, whitelisting/partnership ads, and organic traffic — which Miryala frames as improving prediction accuracy and reflecting genuine organic conversion credit (a strong product with built-in trust converts more easily, independent of Meta’s role).

04 LTV, attribution, and new-customer reach

Miryala calls LTV Meta’s long-running north star but says it remains difficult to productize because predicting further into the future is inherently harder, and advertiser definitions of LTV vary widely (lifetime revenue vs. margin, 30-day windows vs. 12-month). He says any new LTV optimization product will likely require advertisers to explicitly define their own LTV calculation rather than Meta inferring it. On new-customer targeting, Miryala says Meta’s system has no explicit concept of prioritizing new vs. existing customers — each impression is evaluated independently for the cheapest/most valuable next action — which Plofker says matches Jones Road’s frustration roughly 18 months ago that exclusions weren’t effectively limiting reach to new buyers, prompting direct engagement with Meta’s product team.

05 The marketer’s shifting role

Miryala says the marketer’s role has moved from manually tuning account knobs to deeply understanding what drives incremental business value and feeding that alignment to Meta through attribution and value tools, while also ensuring creative diversity since Andromeda’s larger candidate pool rewards having more assets for the system to match against users. HexClad’s Connor Rolain argues the fundamentals haven’t changed in a decade — creative, destination (landing pages), and offer remain where marketers add the most value — while ad account structure (e.g., not mixing a $130 product into a campaign trained on $700 bundle buyers) still matters for passing clean signal. Miryala separately explains that rising relevancy scores reflect improved advertiser value (more clicks/conversions per impression) more than user value, since better predictions let Meta extract the same outcomes with fewer ad impressions shown.

01 SpaceX’s buildout and capital math

Gerstner frames SpaceX’s earnings call as evidence the central market question is whether enough capital and manpower exist to build U.S. data centers. He cites Musk’s disclosure of standing up 200,000 GB300 GPUs per month (a pace that matched an entire prior year just months ago), reaching 3 gigawatts by year-end and 10 gigawatts in 2027 — against a total U.S. forecast of 43 gigawatts next year, meaning SpaceX alone would be about 25% of it. He frames Musk’s thinking as a belief that whoever controls scale in capital, compute, and data wins the race to superintelligence, and argues the Tesla-SpaceX merger logic follows directly: combined, the companies could borrow and raise capital more cheaply to fund that buildout.

02 Merger premium and neocloud financing

On a possible Tesla-SpaceX merger, Gerstner — a holder of both stocks — rejects the idea of a modest premium, instead expecting the Tesla special committee to set a premium above the typical 20-30% control premium (his range: $420 to $550 per share) specifically to insulate against anticipated shareholder lawsuits given Musk’s conflicted position. Separately, he explains the gyrations in neocloud stocks like CoreWeave as a financing-quality issue, not a demand issue: lenders can easily underwrite NVIDIA GPUs because they’re fungible across customers, while custom ASICs are much harder to finance — an advantage he says favors SpaceX, which runs almost exclusively on NVIDIA.

03 Supply shortage, margins, and the glut debate

Responding to anchor-cited figures on supplier margins (NVIDIA at 75%, Micron at 86% last quarter), Gerstner argues the GPU shortage has persisted since 2023 despite repeated predictions of an imminent glut, and that everyone who called a glut over the past two years missed NVIDIA’s 18x rise. He distinguishes today’s AI buildout from the 2000 dot-com bust: in 2000, he argues, demand was constrained by how few people were online, whereas today demand for superintelligence is unconstrained while supply is limited by physical constraints — wafer production speed and power availability.

04 Starlink, telecom disruption, and the Ukraine story

On Starlink’s threat to telecom incumbents (prompted by AT&T’s stock falling despite a decent earnings print), Gerstner says the market isn’t claiming certainty SpaceX will displace carriers, just that the probability has risen sharply, which raises telecoms’ discount rates. He predicts fast, wide Starlink penetration, dismissing carrier claims of insurmountable technical barriers as classic innovator’s dilemma reasoning. On the anchor-raised Wall Street Journal report that Musk may have personally throttled Starlink speeds for Ukraine (distinct from the government-controlled Starshield), Gerstner says he doesn’t know the facts but hopes that if Democrats retake Congress, the response avoids prolonged prosecutions given how much bipartisan work remains undone.

05 Trump accounts and the Musk share gift

Gerstner details the Invest America program (Trump accounts): 70 million accounts already opened, including 2 million in New York City with $500 million waiting to be claimed, he said, appearing on behalf of Robin Hood, the New York poverty-relief group run by Paul Tudor Jones. He says Musk’s pledge to gift one SpaceX share to every American child starting 2027 came after lengthy persuasion, inspired originally by Gerstner’s unrealized hope that Warren Buffett would donate Berkshire shares; founders resist selling shares but are willing to gift them so children benefit from compounding. He describes a tiered holding-period structure (one year at 17, down to five years under 12) and says he’s pushing Congress to codify stock-gifting rules and potentially let all Americans under 40 open accounts pre-emptively, alongside the existing federal saver’s match for the lowest-income Americans’ IRAs. He says he’s told the administration he can raise $100 billion more from corporate donors within a year.

06 Anthropic, OpenAI, and AI safety regulation

Gerstner attended Anthropic’s investor day with roughly 150 top investors and rates sentiment extremely high, citing an expanding product pipeline and competitive moat. His model puts 2027 revenue at $140-150 billion, yielding a $1.5-2 trillion valuation range depending on multiple (10x vs. 12x), and he describes Anthropic as profitable on a per-token basis. He considers an OpenAI 2027 IPO highly probable, driven by the same capital race dynamic underlying the SpaceX-Tesla logic, and argues current AI valuations carry lower multiples than Facebook, Google, or SpaceX had at their own IPOs. Pressed by Joe Kernen on whether a brief regulatory document can govern an approaching singularity, Gerstner describes a three-legged stool of U.S. AI regulation: the Commerce Department’s CAISI pre-reviewing every frontier model before release, a White House accord requiring internal/external audits and board oversight, and a group he calls the “SI force” led by Jay Clayton for critical infrastructure and cyber. He says he is pro-human and believes training should keep humans in control, while noting Musk and others closer to the technology believe transformative capability is nearer than most assume.

07 Philanthropy structures compared

Asked to contrast Trump accounts with Anthropic/OpenAI’s B-Corp and foundation-based giving, Gerstner describes Invest America as the most disruptive direct charitable platform created in U.S. history, enabling single-page donor agreements with the Treasury that bypass traditional foundations. He says Anthropic’s own documents describe plans to direct up to $100 billion partly to citizen accounts, calling Musk, Gwynne Shotwell, Michael and Susan Dell, and Ray Dalio pioneers of a movement in which pledges convert into direct, compounding gifts to children rather than remaining unspent commitments.
