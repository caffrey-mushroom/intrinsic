SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion assessed current systemic lupus erythematosus treatment practices, unmet needs, and early Phase I results for an autologous CD19 CAR-T therapy. Standard care remains centered on glucocorticoids and hydroxychloroquine, supplemented by immunosuppressants and biologics.

The expert viewed existing biologics as slow acting and insufficiently transformative, particularly in long-standing disease. Early CAR-T efficacy appeared encouraging relative to current options, although the small trial and limited follow-up prevent conclusions about durability and uncommon safety risks.

Key Insights

Approximately 20% of patients were estimated to have inadequately controlled or refractory disease, although relapse eventually occurs in nearly all patients. Existing biologics may reduce disease activity and steroid use but generally do not deliver dramatic clinical improvement. Treatment within six months of disease onset tends to work better than treatment after more than five years, when organ damage may be irreversible. The CAR-T results were considered impressive because participants had long-standing disease, renal involvement, and prior exposure to multiple therapies. Low disease activity was viewed as relatively achievable with conventional care, making durable remission and renal improvement more meaningful differentiators. The expert was optimistic about cell therapy but identified response durability as the central unanswered question. Remission lasting over five years would represent a highly valuable outcome.

KH: What are the current unmet needs in the treatment of SLE, particularly for patients with relapsed or refractory disease?

YX: For patients with SLE, I think the primary unmet need is that, as I mentioned earlier, the efficacy of biologics and immunomodulatory therapies is still not particularly satisfactory. Biologics are widely used in dermatology. For example, dupilumab is used for atopic dermatitis, and secukinumab is used for psoriasis. These agents are used very extensively. In those diseases, treatment can clear more than 90% of skin lesions, and sometimes even achieve complete clearance. The clinical benefit is immediate and dramatic, and the difference between receiving treatment and not receiving treatment is enormous.

However, in SLE, whether it is belimumab, telitacicept, or rituximab, the treatment effects are not nearly as pronounced. My impression is that they can reduce disease activity scores and lower corticosteroid requirements, but they fall far short of creating the kind of dramatic difference, both from the patient's subjective perspective and the physician's assessment, that we see when comparing treated versus untreated patients in some other diseases. They are nowhere near that level, which is a significant limitation.

The second drawback is the relatively slow onset of action. These therapies typically require one to two months or even longer before meaningful clinical effects become apparent. Some patients may not have the patience to wait
