00:01:56] In the field of SMRs [small modular reactors], what are the advantages and disadvantages of fourth-generation-based AMRs [advanced modular reactors] vs third-generation SMRs in terms of technological approach and application scenarios?

Expert [00:01:56]: The advantages of the fourth-generation AMR are mainly that the resource utilisation rate is relatively high. Take the Xiapu fast reactor as an example. It is a sodium-cooled fast reactor, and its neutron flux is relatively high. With the same fuel, its nuclear fuel utilisation rate is dozens of times or close to a hundred times that of traditional PWRs. Some fast reactors can also transmute the spent nuclear fuel and nuclear waste produced by existing nuclear power plants, which is another way to dispose of spent fuel. On this basis, the treatment rate of spent fuel is continuously improved, and the utilisation rate of nuclear fuel is also very high.

The traditional way of treating spent fuel involves sealing radioactive waste and burying it deep in the desert, and this waste has a long half-life. Now, through the use of fourth-generation AMRs, radioactive waste that originally had a half-life of tens of thousands of years can be transmuted using high-flux neutrons, ensuring that it decays to a safe level within a few hundred years.

The second is the inherent safety attribute. Most fourth-generation AMR designs dispense with the water cooling system used by conventional pressurised water reactors and rely on passive physical mechanisms to achieve autonomous shutdown, in principle completely avoiding core meltdown and the extreme safety accidents of conventional nuclear power. China Huaneng Shidao Bay Project previously carried out a complete power cut experiment, and the operating nuclear power plant can achieve autonomous shutdown when the power is cut off directly.

Another advantage is that China Huaneng’s high-temperature reactor has many uses because its gas outlet can reach temperatures of 700-900 degrees Celsius, which is much higher than the original pressurised water reactor. In addition to power generation, it can also supply high-temperature steam to petrochemical processing and the steel industry. Industries with a high demand for high-temperature steam can use it for low-cost hydrogen production, seawater desalination and other application scenarios that traditional nuclear power cannot provide. In addition, it can also provide district heating and use high-temperature steam for it, which is more efficient than traditional heating.

Another feature is that the deployment of fourth-generation AMRs is relatively flexible. A single reactor generally has a power output below 300MW. Most core components can be prefabricated in the factory and installed and assembled on site, enabling capacity to be expanded according to on-site demand. Take the high-temperature reactor as an example. For the 200MW Shidao Bay project, if 600MW is needed on site, three units can be directly synchronised and connected in parallel for flexible deployment. On this basis, the threshold has been greatly lowered compared with the initial bespoke design, enabling direct deployment in industrial parks or near data centres that are currently needed.

It also has some disadvantages, such as relatively low technological maturity. At present, the Shidao Bay high-temperature reactor is the only fourth-generation AMR in the world to have achieved commercial operation. All other mainstream designs, including thorium-based molten salt reactors and lead-cooled fast reactors, are still at the experimental or design stage. For example, the reactor at Gansu Wuwei is still an experimental reactor, and so is the sodium-cooled fast reactor. The fast reactor at Xiapu is currently at the hot and cold testing stage and has not yet achieved commercial operation. A large amount of data needs to be collected, and verification during subsequent operation is needed to provide the design institute with a basis for further optimisation. Fuels with special requirements for fourth-generation AMRs also require further optimisation.

Another disadvantage is that the supply chain may not yet be fully developed. For example, the circuits of sodium-cooled fast reactors and lead-based fast reactors both use lead and liquid sodium. In this environment, there are high requirements for special alloys resistant to metal corrosion. There is also molten salt technology, such as thorium-based molten salt reactors. Molten salt reactors are specifically used for thorium-based molten salt extraction, that is, fuel extraction, and this also needs to be further optimised. Because there are not many reactors for market verification, a mature industrial system has not yet been formed. At present, they are all experimental reactors or demonstration reactors. The initial construction cost is certainly much higher than that of traditional PWRs due to the lack of economies of scale.

As for regulation, most countries have not yet developed a comprehensive set of safety standards for AMR. The approval process is still at the stage where everyone is feeling their way forward, so there is considerable uncertainty in the overall implementation timeline. The domestic situation is relatively fine, but uncertainty overseas should be much greater than at home. In addition, to put it simply, AMR nuclear power technology is certainly superior to third-generation nuclear power and SMR before the full popularisation of nuclear fusion. It has long-term potential and deserves recognition. However, it may take about five years for large-scale commercialisation in China and at least 10-15 years abroad.

Client 2 [00:14:48]: A milestone on the path to commercialisation is zero-power criticality. What does this mean?

Expert [00:14:48]: Criticality, for example, means that after the engineering principle and technical feasibility of an experimental reactor are verified, nuclear fuel is loaded. After loading, criticality refers to a physical state in which the emitted neutrons have undergone positive feedback and can generate electricity. The critical state definitely requires completing the verification of the engineering prototype and then conducting criticality experiments in the demonstration reactor. Without engineering prototypes and actual demonstration reactors, it is impossible to carry out criticality operations. Criticality can only be achieved after loading.

00:15:51] What observable milestones can help determine the time to commercialisation?

Expert [00:15:51]: First, in China, there are several important milestones. One is that the core design passes regulatory pre-review. For example, when the core design passes the preliminary safety assessment by the National Nuclear Safety Administration and the enterprise obtains official approval, it marks the first threshold for an enterprise to move from design blueprints to engineering prototype experimentation.

The second important point is that the first engineering demonstration reactor has obtained its construction permit and project approval, marking that this technology has fully passed review. This is equivalent to moving from the experimental stage to the engineering stage. The third node is full-power operation plus cold test and hot test. After the project construction is completed, the pressure of the primary circuit and the working temperature of the secondary circuit are simulated first. The hot test is a simulation at the actual working temperature. After the simulation, these parameters are collected, and once safety requirements are confirmed to be met, the standard is verified.

Another point is to achieve first criticality. When the reactor reaches criticality, it can maintain a self-sustaining chain reaction, demonstrating that the core model, including the fuel system as a whole, is feasible. This is a key milestone. Then comes the first reactor, namely the first engineering demonstration reactor, which is connected to the grid and can generate electricity, and then obtains the operating licence. This is the landmark milestone that marks the point at which a normal reactor can truly make the business model work.

Later, for example, once batch orders are obtained and the supply chain is modularised and scaled, large-scale batch production can be achieved and manufacturing costs can be further reduced. These are mainly the six steps. If Oklo has not completed the first few steps I mentioned, then given the pace of progress in China and the US, I think 2028 is basically impossible.
