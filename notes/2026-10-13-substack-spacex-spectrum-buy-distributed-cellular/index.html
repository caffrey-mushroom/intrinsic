SpaceX Buys More Spectrum, Musk and Market Structure, Distributed Cellular

Tuesday, October 13, 2026

Good morning,

On last week’s episode of Sharp Tech, Andrew and I discussed the two paths of consumer computing in the AI era, and why I feel increasingly disconnected from the Apple path, plus our reactions to OpenAI’s math breakthroughs.

On to the Update:

SpaceX Buys More Spectrum

From the Wall Street Journal:

SpaceX reached a deal to buy cellular spectrum licenses from investment firm Grain Management, its biggest move yet into a wireless market dominated by traditional telecom companies. The Elon Musk-led space company will pay about $8 billion in cash for the assets, according to people familiar with the matter. Grain and SpaceX had no comment on the value of the transaction.

The U.S. licenses, which are in the 800-megahertz band, are tailored for wireless service from cellphone towers rather than satellites. The spectrum could serve SpaceX’s stated ambition of moving its Starlink satellite service beyond broadband into the wireless market to compete with the likes of AT&T and Verizon. U.S. cellphone carrier stocks sank on the news. Shares in AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile all fell more than 6% in post-close trading. SpaceX shares climbed about 3% to $165.10 after hours.

I’ve covered previous SpaceX spectrum purchases, and in both cases I’ve poo-poohed the overheated rhetoric about SpaceX being a genuine competitor to the traditional telecom providers. In September 2025 SpaceX acquired H-Block and AWS-4 spectrum from EchoStar, which had three problems in terms of mobile coverage:

  • While AWS-4 originally had both an uplink band (2000–2020 MHz) and a downlink band (2180–2200 MHz), EchoStar had designed the uplink band as download (which makes sense given EchoStar was broadcasting television); this could be reversed, of course, but…
  • AWS-4 was not approved for satellite-to-handset communication. This could happen, of course, but…
  • H-Block capacity was quite limited and was not supported by any current handsets.

My read on that acquisition was that SpaceX was making a play to be the satellite service partner for all of the major carriers, all of whose satellite plans were, at that specific moment of time, in limbo:

In summary, right at the exact moment that all of the relevant players in satellite connectivity are facing go/no-go decisions in terms of future capabilities, and just as those future initiatives are suffering from delays and a lack of launch capacity, SpaceX is shoving its chips into the middle of the table: everyone knows that SpaceX has the most launch capacity, and now they have not just spectrum but a demonstrated willingness to arguably overpay to have the best service (or at least gain the bargaining capacity to trade for it). Facing this reality, why shouldn’t Apple or AT&T or Verizon blink, and simply use Starlink?

In fact, none of the companies blinked: AT&T and Verizon doubled down on AST SpaceMobile, and Apple threw in with Amazon via the latter’s acquisition of Globalstar. My read: none of the companies wanted to be beholden to Elon Musk.

Musk and Market Structure

Delta CEO Ed Bastian, another of Amazon’s future satellite customers, was surprisingly blunt on this point, according to an X Message from @xJonNYC, a well-regarded newsbreaker in the airline space:

Re: starlink, Ed said, “We do not want to be with Elon Musk. Trust me.”

Side note: at a different, earlier event, Ed also said that DL did test with starlink, but did not want to partner with Elon. “If you know his reputation, it’s all true what they say about him.”

Musk, eager to prove Bastian correct, responded to a message linking an airline industry blog reporting on this news thusly:

You can see Bastian’s point! However, it’s Musk who may have one. Starlink on an airline is a transformative experience; to the extent Delta isn’t feeling the pain is because the United rollout is only 41% complete. And, I would note, I’m not a big fan of United running commercials for a service that you can’t count on.

However, the implication of that complaint is that once you can count on Starlink being available I can’t imagine choosing an airline that doesn’t have it. That’s still a year or two away, but Amazon’s Leo is a few years away too, and Delta has to endure the messy middle where some flights are awesome and some aren’t. Meanwhile, Southwest has started its Starlink installation, and American Airlines has upped its Starlink commitment to its entire mainline fleet. It’s not out of the question that Delta feels the pain in a few years: it’s hard to run a premium strategy when you’re missing the single best airline feature. And, when and if Delta changes its mind, it will pay more than it would have had it signed up at the same time United did.

This is all speculative, to be sure, but it’s pretty plausible, and the actual reality of how Musk operates. Bastian is playing with fire if he’s truly making corporate decisions based on his personal dislike of Musk; the best way to understand Musk’s approaches to markets is that his goal is to fundamentally reshape the market structure such that his companies’ victory is inevitable, whether you like him or not. I think that SpaceX has pulled that off with airline Internet connectivity, and my working assumption is that is what the company was trying to do with handset satellite connectivity.

The question is (1) is the market big enough to matter and (2) do consumers care enough about this feature to make major purchase decisions — like their carrier — based on this feature? I’ve long suspected that the answer for both is “no”, which is why I wondered if SpaceX was just as likely to sell spectrum as it was to acquire more.

In fact, however, SpaceX kept buying: in November the company bought the AWS-3 spectrum block from EchoStar that provided much more expansive uplink capacity; this matched the AWS-4 spectrum SpaceX had already agreed to buy, but it had the same limitation in terms of not being classified for satellite to handset communication, albeit a limitation that is, predictably, on the verge of being lifted.

Distributed Cellular

Starlink’s goal for this spectrum became much clearer in August on the first SpaceX company earnings call; this was President Gwynne Shotwell in response to a question about the company’s mobile ambitions:

The spectrum that we purchased from EchoStar does have terrestrial components. So we definitely intend to build out the terrestrial component. So you will have not only the capacity from the satellites themselves, but you will have a build-out of the terrestrial; basically the hardware and systems necessary to make a true mobile service exactly what you want it to be.

I’m not going to talk about the CapEx for that. It’s a little — well, I don’t want to say it’s immature. I’m just going to say I’m not going to talk about the CapEx for that right now. But let’s talk a little — let me give a little hint, and that is you could put a base station, a cellular base station on the — basically the gear that holds a Starlink broadband dish. So you can have kind of these little femtocells around the country and you deploy that as you need it. You don’t have to necessarily spend many billions of dollars in low-band spectrum all upfront before you deploy your system. So that’s why I don’t want to talk about the CapEx for building out the terrestrial because we have a lot of really kind of great and new ideas for how we’re going to do that. And I think it will be quite CapEx efficient.

Musk added:

That point is probably worth just a few more words, which is that — because this is quite an important point. Instead of having to deploy these very expensive and difficult-to-locate large cellular base stations, we feel reasonably confident that we can deploy a large number of sort of small stations. Essentially, they’re really just Starlink dishes that also provide connectivity in the mobile spectrum bands and have them be all over the place. And the Starlink antennas are located on the roofs of houses and businesses. So there — they’ve got sort of a clear view to providing connectivity directly to cell phones on the ground. And in fact, providing connectivity that is, we think, probably better and higher bandwidth than what is currently available from cellular providers. This is an important point.

I’m honestly annoyed I didn’t think about this while writing about SpaceX’s spectrum acquisitions last year, but it’s very compelling. One of the biggest expenditures for traditional telecom providers is finding cellular sites, running fiber to them, mounting equipment, etc.; Starlink is approaching the problem from the exact opposite direction. Thanks to the company’s satellite constellation its future network basically has backhaul everywhere; the way to leverage that is not with ultra-powerful nodes but rather what is basically a mesh network of smaller scale access points. Moreover, those access points could, at least in theory, not only use cellular spectrum but also Wi-Fi; Wi-Fi’s limitation is its range, but the entire point of having far more access points in far more places is that in the best case scenario you are close to an access point all of the time.

Still, Wi-Fi isn’t enough; that’s where this latest spectrum purchase comes in. This latest spectrum purchase was previously used for Sprint’s old Nextel push-to-talk network. It’s relatively low frequency compared to SpaceX’s other purchases, which is important as far as penetrating walls is concerned; generally speaking, there is a trade-off between reach and penetration versus bandwidth.

What is fascinating about SpaceX’s approach is that the best way to solve its reach problem is through many more access points; still, it can’t have access points on every building, so this spectrum purchase fills in the gaps in letting those sites reach more places. This spectrum, for the record, is also not yet licensed for satellite-to-handset, although it seems likely SpaceX will get that license too; what is much more compelling is using this spectrum for a future network of SpaceX receivers.

There is, to be clear, a long path to making SpaceX’s vision a reality; that vision, from the company’s blog:

Once we receive final FCC approval, Starlink Mobile will deploy a flexible architecture combining our satellite-to-mobile constellation in space with an advanced terrestrial deployment that can seamlessly connect devices everywhere. While Starlink Mobile’s global 2 GHz mid-band spectrum will provide high-bandwidth capacity in the United States, this new low-band spectrum will provide a coverage layer that ensures Starlink Mobile’s signal penetrates through obstacles, such as walls, and can provide service to customers’ devices even when they are in buildings. Critically, most existing mobile devices already support this underused band.

Starlink already operates the world’s most advanced broadband network from space, serving millions of customers across the United States and around the world with high-speed internet. With this new low-band spectrum and our Gen2 constellation, Starlink Mobile can now become the first network operator to deploy both satellite and terrestrial spectrum. One network that provides Americans with a reliable service indoors, outdoors, in cellular dead zones, and everywhere in between.

That is why, in the end, this is a very Muskovian endeavor. SpaceX was the first to deploy massive numbers of low orbit satellites, overwhelming relatively small numbers of high capacity high orbit satellites with a swarm of lower capacity satellites, all of which work in concert thanks to software, both on the satellite layer and the dish layer; the company’s approach to mobile is in the same vein, leveraging its satellite advantage to build out massively more ground access points with relatively lower capacity, and by virtue of being first making it impossible for anyone else to compete, at least with this architecture.

To be clear, SpaceX has a lot of work to pull it off; its competitors have far more compelling and built-out alternatives than competing satellite services ever did. Moreover, when it comes to consumer cellular services, the decision makers are not airline executives but individual customers. Everything Musk does, however, requires a lot of work; what is consistent is the impenetrable moat that could result if SpaceX pulls this off, one that could be irresistible no matter what you think of Musk himself.
