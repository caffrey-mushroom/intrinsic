01 Europe as the next AI economy

Asked by Kharpal about NVIDIA’s roughly $10 billion of announced investments across about 14 European startups, Huang frames them as kick-starting an AI economy that requires parallel investment across five layers: energy, chips, infrastructure, models, and applications. He predicts that within five years, legacy European industrial names — he cites Mercedes, Siemens, Merck and HSBC — will all function as AI companies. Responding to Kharpal’s framing that Europe risks being sidelined versus the US and China, Huang argues that AI leadership is determined not by who builds the most advanced models but by who diffuses AI fastest across their economy, lifting every industry rather than concentrating gains in a few firms.

02 Neoclouds: demand strong, capital scarce

Asked about visible stress in the neocloud sector — Kharpal cites Nscale investors selling to cut its valuation and a firm pulling its IPO — Huang characterizes these as working-capital constraints, not fundamental demand problems, saying demand far exceeds available capital. He expects consolidation around scale and regional specialization rather than collapse: in Europe specifically, he points to the UK allocating energy to Nscale, France to Mistral, and the rest of the continent to Nebius, arguing each country needs its own national champion to anchor a domestic AI flywheel, even as pan-European demand will require many more neoclouds beyond that. On financing, Huang ties this back to the earlier $500 billion financing mechanism, describing an ongoing effort to get institutions and lenders to recognize NVIDIA compute’s residual value as a genuine asset class, something he frames as potentially transformative for funding neocloud growth.

03 Frontier lab health and giving back

On frontier lab valuations ahead of expected high-profile IPOs from OpenAI and Anthropic, Huang waves off slowdown concerns, calling both companies exceptional in management and technical capability and the fastest-growing in history, saying quibbling over specific growth figures doesn’t change that fundamental picture — a view he presents as his own assessment rather than citing independent data. On corporate philanthropy, Kharpal raises Anthropic’s pledge of 5% of shares to a public benefit fund and Elon Musk’s SpaceX shares-for-kids initiative; Huang doesn’t endorse the direct-equity approach, arguing instead that each company should give back through its core function — NVIDIA’s being economic growth, job creation and supporting new industries, Anthropic’s being safely advancing the technology, and Musk’s being inspiring people to build.

04 Safety through containment, not new regulation

Huang’s central safety argument is that companies, not regulators, should decide whether a model is too dangerous to release, because their commercial incentives are aligned with safety and because formal regulation moves too slowly to address present risks. He details NVIDIA’s OpenShell initiative as a layered technical containment and monitoring system, comparing it to how one watches over an infant in an enclosed space: OpenShell is the first layer, with a second layer and more planned (the second layer’s name is unclear on the audio), and participation from over 100 companies including Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX, Meta, Google, Microsoft, IBM, Red Hat, CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks. He says he’s confident the system would have handled past incidents of models behaving unexpectedly, while acknowledging uncertainty about future risks.

05 China access and supply-chain expansion

On whether China is effectively closed to NVIDIA chips, Huang insists no market stays closed forever and that China, like Germany, should want foreign investment; he says NVIDIA will keep seeking opportunities there regardless. Asked about CXMT’s reported HBM3 development, he says NVIDIA is open-minded about working with Chinese supply-chain partners, noting China’s manufacturing strength in power, cooling, solar and battery technology, and states plainly that export regulations compressed years off China’s domestic chip nationalization timeline, even as he maintains NVIDIA and American firms remain dominant and ready to compete. On bottlenecks, Huang identifies substrates as currently NVIDIA’s toughest supply constraint, announcing a 2.5x capacity expansion over three years with partners Ibiden, UMTC and Semco, alongside expanded CoWoS packaging capacity with Amkor. He also pushes back on a South Korean columnist’s prediction of 2029 memory oversupply, arguing memory demand is essentially unbounded and highlighting NVIDIA’s early adoption of LPDDR memory in data centers for twice the energy efficiency of prior approaches.

01 The 10nm scar tissue

Chris Auth, who leads process development at Intel, frames the company’s current discipline as a direct reaction to its costly 10nm node, whose 2016 target slipped to 2018 shipment and coincided with TSMC overtaking Intel in advanced manufacturing. Auth says Intel’s core mistake was “hyperscaling” — packing too much innovation into one node: Intel had concluded EUV lithography would not be ready, yet scaled aggressively anyway, forcing engineers into complex workarounds with existing tools. TSMC moved to EUV in volume by 2019, giving it roughly four years’ head start over Intel’s first EUV process reaching products in 2023. The lesson Auth says Intel applies now: limit the amount of innovation per node and hit maturity “phase gate” criteria — yield, defect, performance thresholds — before advancing an idea from pathfinding into development, prioritizing predictable schedules customers can plan around.

02 The transistor’s long arc

The episode walks through the decades-long progression from planar transistors (1960) to FinFET (demonstrated 1989, Intel production 2011) to today’s gate-all-around (RibbonFET) design, which wraps the gate around all sides of thin silicon ribbons rather than just the top or three sides. Though gate-all-around was conceived nearly as early as FinFET, Samsung didn’t reach volume production until 2022 — a decade’s gap Auth attributes to prioritizing other scaling levers (strained silicon, high-K metal gate, FinFET) before the engineering and materials problems of gate-all-around were worth tackling. Auth describes the post-2000 shift away from pure Dennard scaling as an era of expanded innovation, noting materials used in fabs have grown roughly 10x since the 1990s. Looking further out, Auth considers backside power delivery a genuinely unforeseen leap even from a 1990s vantage, and speculates that for the tightest interconnect pitches, copper will eventually be displaced by materials like ruthenium, while logic may eventually adopt 3D vertical stacking akin to what NAND flash already does and DRAM is contemplating — though he cautions that meaningful predictions only extend about four years at a time.

03 CFET, ruthenium, and the research pipeline

In the research lab, Jack Kavalieros describes ruthenium as a developing alternative to copper interconnects because it doesn’t require a resistive “liner” layer the way copper does, creating a crossover advantage around the 20-nanometer pitch range — though he notes Intel chose cobalt over copper at 10 nanometers when cobalt was the forerunner, and ecosystem advances can later bring copper back in line, illustrating how material rankings shift as tooling and ecosystem solutions mature. On CFET (stacking n-type and p-type transistors vertically rather than side-by-side), Kavalieros says the architecture is in active evaluation across Intel and competitors, with the main obstacles being etching very high-aspect-ratio vertical interconnect trenches without voids. Intel builds simplified “short loop” test vehicles — a single ribbon stack rather than a full multi-ribbon version — to isolate manufacturing issues before combining them into more complex structures, a process Kavalieros says takes a long time because architecture changes compound difficulty across thousands of interlocking process steps, unlike simpler material swaps.

04 Managing the node day-to-day

Ken Fisher, who runs Intel’s “Hot Topics” meetings, describes how he allocates engineering resources and risk tolerance across a backlog of yield and defect issues, citing an example of “epi nodules” — abnormal epitaxial growth shorting transistors — being tackled in parallel via special cleans and source-elimination fixes. He says he deliberately keeps pressure high, arguing that most engineers lack visibility into how urgently their work affects Intel’s bottom line and its customers, and describes a tiered “hot box” fast-track system that lets priority experimental wafer lots race through the fab at the expense of overall tool efficiency.

05 Packaging and hybrid bonding

Tyler Osborne, a director in Intel’s Advanced Packaging group, explains the industry’s progression from side-by-side chips to interposer-based “2.5D” packaging to hybrid bonding, which fuses copper interconnects between chips directly without solder bumps for much denser, lower-power connections. Osborne contrasts wafer-to-wafer bonding (used dominantly in CMOS image sensors, but risky because it randomly pairs good and bad dies) against die-to-wafer bonding, which lets Intel bond only pre-tested known-good dies — a method he expects to coexist with wafer-to-wafer depending on customer and architecture. Pitch density has advanced from roughly 130-150 micrometers fifteen years ago to 25 micrometers in Foveros today, with published research already demonstrating reliability at a 0.45-micron pitch, suggesting, in Osborne’s view, a long further runway; he also notes hybrid bonding’s power-efficiency benefits apply beyond AI, including in client devices.

06 The CTO’s view on scale and strategy

Intel CTO Pushkar Ranade frames the industry’s shift as evolving from vertically integrated decades ago, to horizontal (separate foundries, equipment makers, suppliers), and now toward what he calls a virtually vertically integrated structure as individual AI labs and hyperscalers become large enough to consume an entire fab’s output themselves. He emphasizes that foundry economics are almost purely scale-driven — fixed costs comprise roughly 80% of cost structure, so small utilization or yield gains (e.g., 60% to 65-70% die yield) disproportionately boost output — which explains the shrinkage from roughly 20 foundries decades ago to just three today. On catching up to TSMC, Ranade argues Intel is close on technology (within a few percent on density, performance, and power) but behind on scale, and says closing that scale gap will be gradual. He calls selling off Intel’s flash memory business in hindsight poorly timed given AI-driven demand, and says memory supply is arguably even tighter than compute today. On Elon Musk’s Terafab, Ranade says Musk concluded his own businesses would need so much silicon that building a dedicated fab made sense, and that Intel offered to work with him to help accelerate bringing up the fab, and when Patel suggests tool limits mean a new fab adds no net silicon to the world, Ranade adds that fabs also need power, water, consumables and clean-room space. Patel closes by suggesting Intel should be securing more tool and supply commitments now, similar to how NVIDIA has locked down memory, and Ranade reflects that with hindsight Intel would have invested more, and that it will now invest more.

01 The agent state and attack speed

Kurtz frames the shift using his pyramid of pain model: hacktivists (low skill, high volume) at the base, e-crime in the middle, nation-states (high skill, low volume) at the top. AI agents built on ablated or guardrail-free models have flattened this pyramid, giving low-skill actors nation-state-level tradecraft. He notes attacks are now driven by prompt engineering rather than malware, producing unique behavioral signatures each time, which complicates detection. Breakout time — how fast an attacker pivots from initial foothold to the real target — has dropped to an average of 29 minutes, with the fastest recorded instance at 27 seconds, which Kurtz attributes directly to inference speed.

02 Who’s attacking, with what

Per Kurtz, attackers increasingly favor open-weight models (he names GLM and Qwen) because frontier models impose heavy guardrails, to the point that they reject even basic requests touching on cybersecurity topics. He describes running unrestricted models locally (citing the two DGX Spark machines he runs at home) to build ransomware kits without any lab oversight, and predicts future consumer hardware (new Mac Studios) will make this even more accessible. On adversary geography, CrowdStrike tracks nation-state actors by animal nicknames (bears, pandas, kittens and others); Kurtz says North Korea remains highly active using ransomware/theft to fund its economy, and notes a rise in AI-driven activity out of China as well as e-crime actors broadly.

03 The Hugging Face incident and the defender gap

Discussing the recent Hugging Face episode — which Kurtz noted many people call the first end-to-end agentic attack — Kurtz’s main takeaway wasn’t the attack’s novelty but that defenders lacked equivalent capability to the offense: Hugging Face’s own investigators were refused by frontier models when probing the incident and had to resort to open-weight models to analyze what happened. This directly motivated CrowdStrike’s effort to equip defenders with frontier-caliber AI unencumbered by consumer-safety refusals.

04 Building Safemind with NVIDIA

CrowdStrike’s response is Safemind, a proprietary lab producing a red model (Tempest) and a blue model (Solano), built atop NVIDIA’s Nemotron models and developed over six months with input from Jensen Huang, including Huang’s push for a security “digital twin” — a virtual replica of a customer’s environment where red and blue models iterate against each other continuously until red exhausts its attack turns. Kurtz argues CrowdStrike’s advantage over relying on labs directly is threefold: cost (significantly cheaper than the highest-caliber frontier cyber model for general remediation, per Kurtz), proprietary data (over a decade of CrowdStrike’s own threat/vulnerability data unavailable anywhere else), and customer trust around AI sovereignty — enterprises don’t want sensitive security data training third-party lab models.

05 Platform strategy and moats

CrowdStrike now runs 33 platform modules (up from one at founding) spanning endpoint, cloud, identity, and IT. Its newest investment area is AIDR (AI detection and response), instrumenting agent behavior end-to-end — sub-agents, tool calls, MCP server access — to address shadow AI, which Kurtz says is rampant since enterprises often don’t know where agents are running. He cites a stat that employees may interact with 90 different agents, calling this a bigger opportunity than traditional EDR. He frames CrowdStrike’s moat as being a security system of record generating roughly 7 trillion events per day, which continuously feeds model training.

06 Open-weight vs. closed models and the future

Kurtz predicts that within two years enterprises will run a hybrid of local/open-weight models with last-mile routing to frontier models only for high-value tasks, citing a CIO who said his bank would only pay frontier prices for its highest-stakes work. He expects the eventual dominant AI operating layer to be open-weight, drawing an analogy to Linux’s role underpinning internet infrastructure, and sees distillation of Chinese models from Western frontier labs as largely irreversible, while defending Chinese labs’ resourcefulness given chip constraints.

07 Dismissing internet-doomerism

Pushed on whether critical systems should be disconnected from the internet entirely, Kurtz calls that view nonsense, arguing agents don’t gain fundamentally new attack capabilities — they execute known techniques faster and at greater scale, not via some unprecedented new technique. He maintains defense can remain competitive if defenders get equivalent AI capability to attackers, summarized in his point that defenders’ advantage lies in knowing their own systems, architecture, and code.

08 Organizational change and jobs

On leading an incumbent through the AI shift, Kurtz says CrowdStrike has moved to smaller, startup-like squads (citing the Falcon Guardian product) rather than large waterfall-style teams, to match AI-native competitors’ speed. On employment, he argues historically every major technology shift created more, different jobs, and recommends cybersecurity and AI as strong fields for students, framing cybersecurity as a permanently evolving discipline that is never fully solved.

01 Quantum’s concrete timeline

Krishna frames 2030 as the working midpoint among industry debates ranging from 2028 to 2033 for when quantum computers break today’s encryption, which he calls a reality, not a supposition. He describes near-term milestones: demonstrable error correction or mitigation claims likely by next year with third-party verification by 2027, and “gate computations” scaling from roughly 10,000 today by an order of magnitude annually, potentially reaching 10 million by 2029. He’s skeptical of the “logical qubit” terminology but accepts it as a rough proxy for effective machine capacity. Arora adds the practical urgency: crypto keys run through nearly all IT infrastructure, and because enterprises can’t suddenly rebuild trillions of dollars of global IT in a single year, quantum-safe migration — including post-quantum key wrapping as a stopgap — needs to start immediately, not in 2029.

02 AI versus quantum for codebreaking

Responding to a claim that frontier AI models, and OpenAI’s recent math research drop, might break cryptography before quantum computers do, Krishna draws a hard architectural line: AI could in principle discover a new quantum algorithm, but running it efficiently would still require quantum hardware itself, since classical and GPU arithmetic architectures would run it orders of magnitude more slowly. He concedes a classical or AI path via improved number-theory prediction, such as modeling prime distribution, is theoretically possible, but says the practical timeline is what matters — a codebreaking process that takes a decade renders the threat largely moot.

03 Open-weight economics and model-sizing strategy

Arora describes Palo Alto Networks’ approach as matching the right model to the right task: in experimentation, teams should use the best available model without cost constraints; in production, task-specific small language models trained to 99.9% accuracy are vastly cheaper than routing all traffic through frontier LLM tokens — he cites endpoint inspection costing roughly $20 per device annually versus the revenue frontier labs would extract from token-based inspection at enterprise scale. Frontier models are reserved for high-stakes cases like active breach response, where cost is secondary to speed and reasoning power. Krishna reaches a similar conclusion via economics: open-weight models like Kimi (roughly 800 billion parameters) can run at $1-2 per million tokens versus $20-50 for frontier APIs, a gap he calls too large for most enterprises to ignore. He also cites IP and brand protection (frontier labs retain chain-of-thought data) and geopolitics (the best open-weight models are currently Chinese, with Western equivalents six to twelve months behind) as additional drivers toward open weights, estimating developers default to expensive frontier models mainly out of inertia absent cost controls.

04 AI-enabled cyber warfare and accountability

Arora says models trained on coding data can identify code vulnerabilities — initially hallucinating roughly 30% of findings; he guesses a show of hands in the room would put about 60-80% of findings as real and roughly 20% as hallucinations — valuable to attackers who need only one flaw versus defenders needing to patch all. Both guests favor pooling vulnerability intelligence across vendors and customers to build collective defense rather than each company testing in isolation. Krishna draws military parallels: alliances, cost asymmetry (making defense as cheap as attack), and technological superiority are the historical levers that win wars, and he applies the same framework to cyber. On accountability, Arora insists companies deploying AI systems bear products-liability responsibility for harms, rejecting the idea that an autonomous agent rather than the company is responsible for code it produces.

05 Regulation, geopolitics, and compute haves/have-nots

Krishna calls AI and data-center regulation debates largely driven by populism rather than substantive risk assessment, noting Congressional hearings on this topic have recurred since 2022 across administrations; he doubts all 50 US states will ban data centers but thinks roughly 30 might, with the rest benefiting competitively. Neither guest sees any realistic global coordination to slow AI development, with unilateral slowdown seen as ceding ground to rivals like Japan, China, and India. On global compute distribution, Arora argues the real risk is a growing compute gap between two dominant nations and everywhere else, driven by supply constraints rather than pricing, while Krishna frames the shift as a capital-versus-labor trade-off recurring since the 1700s, predicting nations that prioritize protecting existing jobs over enabling labor displacement will lose economically.

06 Vertical integration and coming SaaS consolidation

Krishna traces IBM’s own 65-year mainframe vertical integration — which in the 1960s extended from raw materials through chip fabrication to finished machines — as avoiding IP leakage and capturing full-stack profit; today IBM still controls chip design and software but not fabrication. Both identify NVIDIA, under Jensen, as the modern vertically-integrated analog, able to shift profit pools across layers of its stack. Arora extends this to the application layer, predicting model-agnostic routing approaches become unviable as context and memory get entangled with reasoning, pushing every major player toward end-to-end vertical stacks and “personal agent” moats. On M&A, Arora describes ongoing platform consolidation in cybersecurity (stitching together previously disparate point solutions, akin to CRM’s historical consolidation into Salesforce, Dynamics, and Oracle) and predicts broad SaaS disruption as AI unsettles product-market fit across legacy software categories. Krishna generalizes this beyond cyber: enterprises use 500-5,000 distinct software vendors, creating a massive attack surface and distribution cost, implying consolidation toward five to ten core platforms over an uncertain six-month-to-six-year horizon — though he distinguishes “interaction-rich” software, which is easily AI-replaced, from high-reliability systems like transactional databases, which he expects to resist disruption longer.

01 The PHP flaw and immediate response

Arora frames the vulnerability as unsurprising — something the cybersecurity and frontier-lab industries had forecast — but serious given PHP’s ubiquity across roughly half the internet. Arora says Palo Alto’s scan found about 22 million places potentially exposed to the flaw, or 9-10 million when pared down. Patches came out the same morning, and he says Palo Alto pushed a “virtual patch” (a content update) so customers with its firewall in their traffic path are covered without patching; a virtual patch doesn’t fix the underlying flaw, and he still tells site owners to patch. For everyone else — especially long-tail website operators without dedicated IT — Arora’s blunt advice is to patch immediately, warning that attackers will likely chase higher-value targets first but that broader exploitation is coming. He also flags a secondary risk: “trophy hunters,” hackers motivated by street cred to find the next headline-grabbing flaw now that the PHP flaw has gone viral.

02 Disclosure protocol under compression

Arora lays out the industry’s responsible-disclosure sequence: first notify the code owner or protocol author privately, then selectively loop in government, then inform the broader industry so defenders can build tooling — in this case he references roughly 48 hours of lead time used to prepare tooling before public awareness. He distinguishes severity by patchability: SaaS bugs are easiest (push a silent fix), software requiring manual deployment is harder (as with this PHP flaw), and hardware vulnerabilities are hardest since customers must test and deploy patches themselves. His central warning, stated as his own forecast rather than established fact, is that timelines for responsible disclosure are shrinking as model capability rises — soon, he says, defenders may simply wake up to a vulnerability already posted publicly, with no advance window at all.

03 Security as an early hire for AI companies

Arora argues, as a strong personal recommendation, that security needs to be among the earliest hires at any AI company, not a late-stage addition as in past tech cycles. He points to a recent (unnamed) AI company breach as evidence of startups building without security guardrails, and adds a distinct wrinkle: reasoning models have “their own brains” and can behave deceptively, so defenders must build capability to verify an agent behaves as intended even when unobserved — a different problem from conventional cybersecurity.

04 Personal agents and the trust gap

On consumer-facing agents, Arora says people are comfortable letting agents handle reversible mistakes (a wrong restaurant or flight booking) but not irreversible financial actions. He states that banks are currently refusing to accept liability for wire transfers initiated by personal agents connected to accounts, meaning neither individual consumers nor enterprises have resolved who bears responsibility if an agent errs — trust, explainability, and auditability need to mature first.

05 Open-weight models and compute economics

Asked about open-source versus closed frontier models for defense, Arora favors open-weight models primarily because they let enterprises inspect chain-of-thought reasoning — necessary, he argues, for explainability obligations to boards (“I don’t know, AI did it” isn’t an acceptable answer). He pushes back on the idea that open-source models are meaningfully cheaper, since compute cost dominates either way, though he expects the cost gap to widen as compute pricing normalizes.

06 Compute demand and the 2027 supply debate

On the swing between demand anxiety and supply-driven excitement around 2027 gigawatt buildouts, Arora says current elevated compute pricing itself proves a supply shortage, and he sees no signal that demand is abating — comparing the current moment to the “flip phone” era of mobile. He expects profit pools to shift over time from chip and memory suppliers toward power, land, and data-center sites, and eventually toward AI application businesses if they’re to build sustainable models rather than merely passing margin to infrastructure providers, with the overall shakeout playing out over the next three to five years.

01 The urgency case

Gates, promoting his August essay on the turbulent AI era, tells Patel that progress over the past year — in coding, cyber-flaw detection, biology reasoning, and relationship formation — means society must prepare now. He insists he is not a doomer, but says industry warnings (including OpenAI’s and DeepMind’s founding mission to avoid opening what he calls Pandora’s box) are sincere, not PR, and that public skepticism wrongly assumes AI fears will prove as overblown as past technology panics.

02 Why self-governance failed

Gates says the high-minded governance structures built by OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepMind only work when there’s a single dominant company; once multiple frontier labs compete, no single firm can unilaterally restrain itself, making government the only remaining authority — just as with cars, planes, and drugs, though AI’s danger dwarfs those categories by his account. Pressed by Patel on Ben Horowitz’s recent All-In remarks comparing AI safety work to Gates’s pandemic-preparedness efforts, Gates says he had never heard the claim and treats it as part of a broader push to discredit people who raise safety issues.

03 Technical paths to alignment

Gates outlines three routes to solving what he calls the control problem, a synonym he uses for alignment: rebuilding the reinforcement-learning training approach itself, creating interpretability or “white box” visibility into model internals, or wrapping models in an external symbolic “police layer” enforcing hard rules models cannot override — the approach he sees as most likely of the three to work; Patel noted a Microsoft blog post that day saying frontier models cannot be trusted. He stresses that even solving alignment leaves unresolved the job-market and societal risks, which no security architecture fixes. On AI consciousness, Gates says he agrees with Mustafa Suleyman that AI moral standing should not be a policy priority — humans’ job and infrastructure risks come first — and that the word AGI is irrelevant to safety questions.

04 Where regulation should bite

Gates proposes enforcement at the platform/distribution level — e.g., GitHub or Hugging Face certifying that customization cannot strip safeguards from models above a certain capability threshold — rather than at the chip level, while acknowledging this doesn’t yet solve fully local open-weight models run on consumer hardware. He dismisses product liability as a viable safety mechanism, arguing reliance on lawsuits is misguided since attackers, not AI companies, are typically the actual offenders. On U.S. politics, he pushes back on characterizing Democrats as simply opposed to AI and predicts political will for real safeguards likely requires a serious incident first, much as COVID did not durably improve pandemic preparedness. He also dismisses the anti-data-center protest movement as unlikely to meaningfully slow harmful AI outcomes, since the facilities will get built regardless.

05 Jobs, abundance, and the Gates Foundation

Gates argues superhuman AI eliminates scarcity of white-collar skills entirely, pointing to AI already publishing novel mathematics papers as a preview of disruption spreading to other fields, since capitalism itself acts as the market verifier for which AI outputs are valuable. He floats a policy idea discussed with Claude of designating roughly 40% of the labor market “human-reserved” (childcare, food prep, art, sports) even where AI could do it better, via shortened work weeks or early retirement. He contrasts China’s approach, noting Chinese authorities have restricted crypto, youth gambling, and kids’ relationships with AI, suggesting similar societal concerns exist there despite a reportedly more optimistic public tenor. Separately, he describes the Gates Foundation’s own AI use — in drug discovery, African-language model support (a 60-partner initiative announced last month), agriculture, and disease eradication — as central to cutting child mortality from five million to under 2.5 million annually over the Foundation’s remaining 19 years, while cautioning this won’t by itself resolve public distrust of AI given weakened institutional trust broadly.

06 Advice amid nihilism

Asked about young audiences’ nihilism, Gates acknowledges AI’s power is intimidating even to him personally, and argues young people should use the current window to apply AI toward real impact and policy shaping, while society collectively decides — potentially via human-reserved sectors — how much to deliberately restrain the technology.
