1.a] What is a SerDes?

Modern high-end chips tend to run in the 1-5 GHz range.

GPUs, AI ASICS, and other math/throughput/SIMD/matmul heavy machines tend to run around 1-2 GHz.

General-purpose CPUs tend to be in the 4-5 GHz range.

Some designs choose fixed frequency while others dynamically adjust frequency. For the purposes of this section assume we have a chip that always runs at a fixed 2 GHz.

If the I/O of our 2 GHz chip runs at 2 Gbps NRZ, the I/O is by definition a parallel port. Anything faster (4 Gbps PAM4, 4 Gbps NRZ, 32 Gbps NRZ, 224G PAM4, …) makes the I/O by definition a SerDes.

Almost all modern SerDes use differential signaling. So two wires. Modern-day AI systems are blurring the lines between serial and parallel from a practical perspective. The point of SerDes is to reduce the number of wires and mitigate crosstalk and footprint issues. AI systems are so I/O starved that people are effectively building parallel ports using SerDes.

As a rule of thumb, if you put more than 18-24 high-speed (112G PAM4 or faster) SerDes lanes next to each other, you are gona run into fun and interesting problems. Dangerous problems.

Notice the trapezoids in above diagram? Those are muxes.

In order to Serialize and Deserialize, we need a mux tree. Looks something like this.

As an example, suppose our 2 GHz chip has a 16 Gbps NRZ SerDes. Before the transmitter drive (analog) electronics, a 3-layer MUX tree would be present. The selector clock for each stage of the MUX tree would be 4/8/16 GHz.

Designing a PLL that can distribute all these high-speed clocks accurately with minimal skew (phase delay // timing offset) is challenging.

Designing a high-speed MUX (aka final MUX) is also challenging.

The reason I bring up these details is to help you understand that parallel vs SerDes has a hard/rigid definition, but this definition is pedantic and somewhat silly.

Basically, every modern PHY interface you know (Ethernet, PCIe, InfiniBand, OIF, UCIe, USB, …) is a SerDes. Parallel interfaces for off-chip I/O essentially no longer exist in the real world. Note that parallel interfaces do exist in NoC (network-on-chip). For example, it is common for the internals of a 2 GHz clocked chip to have say a 500 MHz internal network connecting compute blocks together.

Even Avicena running their system at 4 Gbps NRZ (on a theoretical host chip running at 2 Ghz) is technically a SerDes.

You should think about this stuff as a spectrum. Faster or slower is not necessarily better. It depends a lot on the context. Going too fast or too slow leads to severe problems that must be overcome.

From a purist system design perspective, my opinion is 32-64G NRZ is the sweet-spot optimal speed for most electrical and optical systems.

1.c] Crosstalk, CDR, Clock-Forwarding, Synchronous Noise, and Current Loops

To understand crosstalk, you must understand that everything is an antenna and this leads to satanic nightmare problems.

Inductive coupling is evil and the source of much pain and suffering. Very difficult to properly simulate. A nightmare to debug once silicon is back. By far the #1 problem that kills projects.

What makes inductive coupling so insidious is the many ways it can show up and all the strange and unusual aggressor paths.

Recall the Synopsys 224G SerDes slides from above section.

  • ADC interleaves lead to complexity and risk of asynchronous noise.- CDR with very complex phase interpolator can have INL/DNL issues which lead to asynchronous noise.- Tx driver slices can induce asynchronous noise.- Complex multi-stage CTLE has large inductors that can pick up asynchronous noise.- Skew from multi-rate clock tree can lead to asynchronous noise.

Naively, noise is bad because it flips data bits, eventually.

Bits get flipped because analog voltages are too high or too low relative to what they should be at a given point in time and relative to a reference.

Ground does not mean 0 volt. There is noise on ground dumped by other things.

Reference does not mean ground. It is common but you can reference against a fixed or dynamic voltage. This is commonly referred to as biasing.

I can’t find a good picture for this so just use your imagination for a moment.

Imagine a design that has 10 different circuits. Each of these 10 circuits has a unique function. Some of them are chained together (A —> B —> C). Others are in parallel (X above A, eventually feed into C).

All of these circuits are powered by the same voltage rail, VDD. They all share the same ground rail, VSS.

Synchronous noise is noise that is time-aligned across all the circuits. Imagine waves of voltage or current that go up and down across all circuits at the same time.

Asynchronous noise changes over time and effects different circuits on at different times relative to each other.

In general, it is easy to mitigate synchronous noise. Most of the problems in modern DSP/ADC SerDes come from clocking/skew/delay/CDR complexities that generate asynchronous noise.

Design complexity (circuit, tuning algo, firmware) is primarily derived from people trying to make asynchronous noise as synchronous as possible.

Before covering Aviciena old-school slicer/analog style 2-4G SerDes, let’s take a look at a middle ground. Nvidia’s 32G die-to-die clock-forwarded SerDes.

Both Nvidia and Avicena use clock-forwarding. This eliminates the CDR and many possible sources for asynchronous noise. They reason they can get away with this is the electrical link is very short so it is possible to transmit a high-frequency clock on a dedicated channel. Clock-forwarding is in general not practical for longer reach links.

Notice how simple the Nvidia D2D clock-forwarded architecture is. No ADC interleaves. No DSP processing. No CDR. No complicated clock tree.

Minimal opportunities for asynchronous electrical noise to show up and ruin the system. Instead, they have optically induced async noise from the micro-rings.

Avicena SerDes is even simpler due to its much slower rate, lack of need for thermal tuning of optical impairments, and lower gain requirements.

In other words, it’s so simple that it is unusually easy to mitigate electrical crosstalk.

Clock forwarding does not automatically fix all problems. Nvidia 32G D2D and other similar D2D (Broadcom Max, UCIe, Marvell D2D) have to put in a non-trivial amount of effort to keep everything clean. At 2-4G, Avicena has a much easier time.

Intuitively, more stuff means…

  • More things to synchronize and back out phase delay.- Higher risk of inductive coupling.- More electrical loss, requiring more amplification stages in Rx, leading to more stuff…

You will see in section [3] that the vast majority of the bad lanes in Avicena demo systems are from optical and assembly issues. There is only one bad lane/eye that seems to be suffering from electrical crosstalk. And even then, that one bad “failed due to crosstalk” lane might be from wirebond which goes away in next gen anyway.

1.d] Forward Error Correction and Striping

https://www.signalintegrityjournal.com/ext/resources/PDFs/PAPER_Track9_224GEthernetFECAnalysis_liu_revised.pdf

The gold-standard in FEC analysis by the legendary Cathy Liu of Broadcom shall be used as a crash course here. Read the full paper if this stuff interests you.

In general the way you read these charts is pick a target post-FEC BER (1e-13 in this case) and see where the curves intersect in terms of SNR value. Higher SNR requirement is bad. A 1 dB SNR gap between two scenarios is a lot while 0.5 dB is meaningful.

Additionally, burst errors (usually… other major source is CDR) from DFE propagation are very bad. You can see the over 1 dB difference between red curve (no burst errors, all errors are truly random) and blue+black curves which model 1-tap DFE back-propagation with varying assumptions that we don’t care about for the purpose of this section.

Finally, note that a pre-FEC BER of 1e-12 is broadly considered to be the minimum bar to operate a link without any FEC at all, saving massive power and latency.

Simulation curves like this don’t capture the reality that measuring BER at error-free scenarios results in measurement time issues. (it is impractical to measure very low BER because you have to wait a long time… eye masks are used in slower NRZ links to get around this problem)

Bluntly, my view is 3-orders of pre-FEC BER margin is needed on worst PVT corner of a characterization lot on a representative channel to consider a system commercially viable at high volume.

However, both uLED and uVCSEL slow-and-wide systems use a light FEC (typically a 2-bit BCH derivative) so let’s keep the pass/fail bar at 1e-12 pre-FEC.

A “light” FEC involves low amounts of math and is thus energy, area (digital logic), and latency efficient.

A “heavy” FEC is opposite, obviously. RS-FEC aka RS(544, 514, 15) is a heavy FEC used by Ethernet, OIF, and most 112G PAM4 or higher standards.

In above plot, note that purple a lot better than yellow which is in turn a lot better than orange.

That is FEC weight for you. uLED and uVCSEL systems tend to use a FEC that is even lighter than orange Hamming(128,120).

Microsoft was one of the hyperscalers who told IEEE they are idiot clowns with this 2e-4 pre-FEC threshold. This is why IEEE 802.3dj switched to 2e-13 POST FEC.

Interleaving can be done at a codeword and lane level. Idea is to have errors spread out across time-domain chunks that are uncorrelated (or at least less correlated) with potential burst error mechanisms.

Both uLED and uVCSEL suffer from chromatic and modal dispersion.

The slope of uVCSEL BER degradation is shallower than uLED.

This is important to notice. Stems directly from how they react to the same impairments with differing severity.

Multi-mode fiber is wide enough to let multiple modes (think of them as beams of light… this is not correct but good enough for intuition which is all that matters) of light pass.

This is great for alignment yield/cost (as covered in previous section) but a disaster for channel quality. Those other modes act as severe ISI (inter symbol interferance).

Fastest way to understand ISI is with a real-world impulse response from an electrical channel.

In an ideal world, the above plot would just be a perfectly thin line. In reality, some very near-field ISI creates the main pulse, usually handled by Tx FIR and DFE for the bulk of EQ with Rx FFE making some contribution as well. The farther away ISI gets handled by only FFE and floating FFE taps.

In optics, chromatic dispersion makes the main pulse wider while modal dispersion adds nasty crap spikes farther away. This is oversimplified but good enough.

2.d] Electronics

The electronics are quite simple due to the slow speed. LEDs need current-mode drivers.

Within optics, one nasty problem is voltage swing and bias requirements. Almost all optical modulators (MZI, ring, EMA) need voltage that a modern leading-edge logic node cannot provide. Thus, external amplification of the SerDes is necessary.

The current gen evaluation kit (eKit) needs 1.5 mA drive and around pk-pk swing. These numbers are high because the current-gen has garbage photodiodes. Trash linearity and apparently 0 dB gain lol.

Next-gen they go to silicon PD co-designed with TSMC which are WAY BETTER, with allegedly up to 10 dB of gain. This will in theory allow from drive current to be cut to 1/3rd (0.5 mA / LED) but they need to test it.

Finally in the existing design, Tx is 0.9 pj/bit while Rx is 0.6 pj/bit which is unusual. Almost every system I have worked with has higher Rx energy burn. In Avicena’s case, this makes sense as reh receiver is just a simple CTLE and 1 (going to 2 next gen) DFE tap.

Most of the Tx power burn is from Avicena trying to compensate for the absolutely garbage PDs.

What makes this fiber stuff so interesting is all the tradeoffs. Single-mode fiber is quite simple. You get fucked on active alignment but otherwise the fibers are benign in terms of link impairment.

The key problem to solve when working with multi-mode fiber systems is to maximize coupling efficiency while minimizing modal dispersion.

2.f] Equalization and FEC

As previously mentioned, Avicena current gen does not have a CTLE and relies exclusively on a single DFE tap EQ. Next gen adds a CTLE (capacitor based, no large inductor) and moves to a two-tap DFE which raises concerns on burst error from back-propagation.

I gave Nigel a long list of questions, including some very specific requests on FEC/SNR/BER plots and much to my surprise the Avicena engineer responsible for this (Emad) actually shared exactly what I was looking for.

Let’s start with the obvious question of how many DFE taps is optimal.

The answer is 2. There are absolutely massive gains going form 1-tap to 2-tap in next gen. Diminishing returns going to 3-tap, although 3-tap is effectively a perfect equalizer for their ISI model.

Back-propagation depends on the DFE coefficients chosen. Broadly speaking, DFE and FFE equalizers have rules on the max value of main-tap and pre/post taps. This is why Avicena modeled the worst case as when H1/H0 = 0.6 which is perfectly reasonable.

For the upcoming product, they have two inner-FEC schemes. The outer FEC is of course RS(544,514), standard Ethernet FEC.

There are some details that I want to keep obfuscated on why there are two inner FEC options but let’s just say I think they can go with the BCH (much lower power and latency) and ignore the RS FEC. Debated several Avicena employees on this and got them to agree that most likely once the chips are back they can tune and avoid using the heavier FEC.

Important and somewhat unique to Avicena, they can stripe the codewords very aggressively across lanes. This helps massively with FEC-induced latency and burst-error tolerance. Suppose you have a single lane with a nasty 10-error sequential burst. Each of the 10 errors on that burst are striped in time and each BCH(redacted,redacted,redacted) symbol only sees one error from that lane effectively.

Naively, if you have this dimension BCH FEC running at 8 GHz, the latency would be 36.5 ns which is not great but not terrible.

But because of the aggressive striping (enabled my massive lanes in parallel, all clock forwarded and synced), in theory that drops to 0.14 ns. Of course, full-parallel processing would not be practical from a timing closure perspective.

Finally we have the total simulation. Marco/CEO was arguing they will be at 1e-9 and thus I need to extent the plot. While I normally give people grief for extending plots by multiple orders of magnitude, in this case that is valid. These SNR vs post-FEC BER plots have linear asymptotes in loglog domain.

UCIe spec is a reasonable proxy for all die-to-die PHY requirements.

There is no FEC in UCIe, only a CRC and retry mechanism.

With the FEC Avicena is using, they burn 0.2 pj/bit (UCIe is 0.5 pj/bit for Tx and Rx and has no FEC) and Avicena gets MASSIVE coding gain of 4.5 dB due to the -lane codeword striping.

Thus in order to hit UCIe spec (after using their FEC), Avicena only needs 4e-8.

A standard 3-order BER margin forces pre-FEC BER requirement to 5e-9.

Finally, the simulation makes some optimistic assumptions (because it is very annoying to model otherwise), such as lanes fully independent. In reality, optical crosstalk (light bleed) and many other factors will lead to some lane correlation. Thus, I added another 3-order buffer which leads to pre-FEC BER of 8e-10 or lower.

Interestingly, this is pretty close to what Marco said Avicena is targeting (1e-9). Bluntly, I think they can do even better than this. Their plan seems to be push speed as far as possible until the FEC breaks. My view is from a pessimistic side of what pre-FEC BER needs to be delivered at a PHY level such that CRC and re-try never get executed unless some tail event like cosmic rays flips a bit.

So let’s discuss the 8 Gbit LEDs.

But first, detour on bandwidth and link budgeting!

Let’s say I have a modulator, in this case a normal VCSEL, that has 11 GHz of bandwidth and high bias current. The response is pretty flat and clean.

Just because I can go 11 GHz does not mean I should. There are many reasons why driving the modulator as fast as possible might backfire.

  1. Reliability2. Thermals3. Power efficiency4. Noise performance of the device itself or the driver.5. …

Likewise, is it possible to run this VCSEL at 14 GHz and compensate with a powerful CTLE that has an extra 6 dB of gain at 14 GHz in the receiver?

Sure. Your SNR is going to be crap because the CTLE amplifies noise massively. But sure you and try that.

And this is where we come back to Avicena. One of the topics I wanted to discuss is speed per lane. Is it really a good idea for them to push to 8 Gbps per LED?

Ways this could backfire:

  1. Electronics (SerDes, driver, TIA) need to be much higher quality, adding risk to the next-gen design.2. Larger electrical structures may result in more risk of inductive coupling.3. Power-supply ripple requirements go way up.4. Potential for increased sensitivity to optical crosstalk.

Suresh/Avicena made an excellent counterargument. The point of the 8 Gbps LED is not necessarily to run it at that rate. If something goes wrong and they can’t run it at scale, so what? The modulator has more bandwidth and thus higher margin from both linearity and link-budget perspectives.

Avicena states that the Rx analog front-end has tuning knobs that can reduce the bandwidth to minimize noise if the final datarate ends up being slower than the max the design was built around.

In other words, say you have a receiver designed for 8 GHz bandwidth but end up running at 4 Ghz for whatever reason. If the AFE stays at full bandwidth, noise between 4 and 8 GHz is going to show up for no good reason. It is very desirable to be able to tune Rx bandwidth. Here is a random example to help you visualize.

“We are not trying to push a 1 Gbps LED to 4 Gbps here.” - Suresh/Avicena

The above quote is a not so subtle shot taken at all the other uLED companies.

Unexpected problems happen as you scale bandwidth and circuit speed. My worry was these guys are chasing speed without understanding the risks and about to step on rakes.

5.a] Deep-Dive on Mode Partition Enhancement

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_partition_noise

Ok… optical mode problems become jitter. Still don’t understand the root cause.

Nigel (I think Howard wrote this TBH) send some starting material. A point in the right direction.

https://opg.optica.org/oe/fulltext.cfm?uri=oe-34-3-4576

Ok this paper is useful for building intuition. They are trying to design various VCSEL geometries (shape of the laser itself) to suppress mode partition noise (MPD).

Linewidth is clearly effected.

RIN too.

Ok to recap….

  • VCSEL spectrum is spiky with gaps in-between.- If you don’t couple the VCSEL correctly into a fiber, the modes will be phase delayed and show up as both jitter and RIN.- If the multi-mode fiber is not precisely designed and manufactured (expensive), same problem.

So why are LEDs not effected by this? The answer is in the fact LED are not coherent light while all lasers are coherent light. The spikes you see in the VCSEL spectra are mode hops where the light jumps spontaneously from one wavelength to another.

LED just vomits incoherent light in a smooth, random manner. In a way, this is like free (legal/intrinsic) waveform averaging lol. The drawback is obviously chromatic dispersion.

So in a way, this comment by an Avicena engineer is a re-hash of the same previously covered tradeoff between uLED and uVCSEL.

uLED severely harmed by chromatic dispersion but mostly unaffected to MPN.

uVCSEL severely harmed by MPN but mostly unaffected to chromatic dispersion.

Thus, uVCSEL must use expensive GRIN OM3 or better fiber while uLED can play tricks with cheaper custom GRIN fiber or even plastic.

At the same time, uVCSEL always has a significant, intrinsic reach advantage because of chromatic dispersion disproportionately harming uLED.
