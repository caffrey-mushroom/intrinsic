Why Now

Following our initiations on BE and FTAI, we are expanding our BTM power coverage to INNIO (INIO), a leading reciprocating-engine OEM in North America and Europe. Its forward EV/EBITDA multiple has declined from a post-IPO peak of ~24x in early June to ~14x. The multiple has compressed despite a strong 3Q26 beat and raised guidance, with Equipment backlog reaching ~$8bn and data centers accounting for ~80% of the new order intake. INNIO’s ~44GW installed base provides a foundation for future Services growth. With the IPO lock-up expiring on November 30, potential sponsor selling remains a near-term overhang. We see an opportunity to assess the business as demand and deliveries ramp. Subscribed

Key Debates and Our Views

Debate #1

How long can equipment demand last in this cycle, and how much of a cushion does the service tail provide?

As with other BTM power providers we have covered, bears argue that INNIO’s equipment demand is being pulled forward by grid delays and limited gas-turbine capacity through 2030. As supply catches up, orders could normalize, and engines may be shifted to standby or redeployed rather than replaced. Lower utilization could also reduce recurring Services revenue and profit, weakening the cushion provided by the installed base.

Our view

We believe INNIO’s equipment cycle could extend beyond 2030, longer than bears expect. The cycle is supported by a multi-year power shortage in North America and appears more durable than a short-term bridge-power market. As the installed base grows, binding service agreements with terms of 10–15 years could provide a meaningful earnings tail. Our model estimates that 15-year service EBITDA per GW could be 2.3x the initial equipment EBITDA, or 1.5x on a discounted basis.

Our reasoning

On the equipment side, orders from VoltaGrid and Liberty support deliveries through 2028. Beyond the current order book, our channel checks suggest that the process from joining the grid-connection queue to receiving power can take about five years, while new gas-turbine delivery slots extend into 2032. This leaves room for BTM providers such as INNIO. We estimate a North American power shortfall of ~15GW in 2027 and ~25GW in 2028. We also expect engines to remain in use after grid access improves. At an estimated ~$36–43/MWh, gas-engine power remains below the ~$63/MWh cost of grid electricity in Texas. Roughly one-third of firm data center reciprocating-engine orders in our BTM tracker are for off-grid sites. Engines redeployed to other projects can continue generating Services revenue as long as they remain in operation.

On the Services side, we believe the revenue and earnings tail could outlast the equipment cycle. With an installed base of ~44GW, Management indicated that ~94% of its data center backlog and reserved capacity was designated for primary power. These engines supply power on a daily basis and accumulate operating hours toward overhauls; related full-service agreements typically run for 10–15 years. Channel checks suggest that proprietary parts, diagnostics and technical access support OEM retention. INNIO remains the exclusive supplier for roughly 80% of service parts across its portfolio, although Waukesha engines use fewer proprietary parts.

Quantifying the service contribution: at 7,000 operating hours per year, our model estimates annual service revenue of ~$49mn and EBITDA of ~$16mn per GW on a levelized basis. Over 15 years, cumulative service EBITDA is estimated at ~$242mn, compared with ~$106mn from the initial equipment sale. This equates to 2.3x on an undiscounted basis, or 1.5x on a discounted basis. Routine maintenance begins at commissioning; minor and major overhauls occur at roughly 40,000 and 80,000 operating hours, respectively. Across our delivery cohorts, demand for minor overhauls begins to scale in 2031, followed by major overhauls from 2036.

Our 2025–30 estimates imply revenue CAGRs of 39% for Equipment and 11% for Services, with EBITDA growing at 46% and 12%, respectively.

Debate #2

Can INNIO sustain higher Equipment margins as competition increases?

Bears argue that INNIO’s Equipment margin could decline as utilization falls and pricing weakens while INNIO and other OEMs expand capacity. Data center customers currently pay a premium for scarce delivery slots, but INNIO may struggle to sustain that pricing as other reciprocating-engine OEMs add capacity.

Our view

We model Equipment EBITDA margin rising from ~15% in FY25 to ~19% by FY28. The improvement is driven by operating leverage: higher shipments spread SG&A and R&D over a larger revenue base. Our base case does not assume another round of price increases; volume growth is the main driver.

Our reasoning

Equipment gross margin remained at ~25% as data center mix rose to ~60%, while EBITDA margin improved to ~17% from ~14% as operating leverage improved. Containerization also increased gross profit per kW. We assume higher deliveries and stable pricing as capacity expands across the current product range.

Our channel checks indicate price premiums of 20–30% on some data center orders, but we do not apply this premium to the full package. It could narrow once another supplier can meet the customer’s required delivery date. Parts shortages may delay new supply, but some buyers already have alternatives. Liberty, for example, has ordered from both Bergen and INNIO. We would monitor whether INNIO’s quotes decline for comparable packages and delivery windows.

Business Overview

INNIO is headquartered in Jenbach, Austria. The company was formed in 2018, when Advent International acquired GE’s Distributed Power business and brought the Jenbacher and Waukesha engine brands under INNIO. The original holding company was incorporated in Austria in April 2018, and the business began operating independently in November.

INNIO sells gas engines and aftermarket services under its Jenbacher and Waukesha brands. Jenbacher primarily serves power generation, while Waukesha focuses on engines used in gas compression. INNIO reports two financial segments: Equipment and Services.

Equipment (67% of 3Q26 sales) includes engines, gensets and packaged solutions across three applications. Segment book-to-bill was 2.8x in 3Q26.

  • Data Centers (60% of Equipment sales) supplies onsite prime, bridge and supplementary power. Data centers accounted for 83% of new Equipment orders, with a 3.9x book-to-bill ratio.- Compression (10% of Equipment sales) includes engines that drive compressors used in natural-gas gathering, processing and transportation. Compression represented 5% of Equipment orders, with a 1.5x book-to-bill ratio.- Power Solutions (31% of Equipment sales) serves industrial generation, combined heat and power, microgrids and grid support. It represented 12% of Equipment orders, with a 1.1x book-to-bill ratio.

Services (33% of 3Q26 sales) supports the installed fleet through parts, maintenance, repairs, overhauls and upgrades. Customers can purchase these services transactionally, paying for individual parts or service events, or contractually through multi-year agreements covering a defined scope of maintenance.

Management says data center-related full-service agreements can extend for 10–15 years, with most including a major overhaul. These agreements may cover direct data center operators, energy-as-a-service providers and rental customers; scope varies by contract. In 3Q26, the Services segment’s adjusted EBITDA margin was 31.7%, compared with 17.1% for Equipment.

INNIO’s products serve both power generation and gas compression markets. For data centers, its core offering is the Jenbacher Type 6, supplied as gensets or packaged power modules. Multiple engine blocks can be combined to meet a campus’s capacity and redundancy requirements. Larger configurations include 20–25MW power packs, which integrate several engines rather than relying on a single larger machine.

For industrial and distributed-power applications, INNIO offers Jenbacher Types 2, 3, 4 and 6, alongside selected Waukesha engines for different power requirements. The larger Type 9, at approximately 10MW, extends the range to larger generation projects. Waukesha’s VGF, VHP and 275GL+ platforms also serve gas compression, where the engine drives a compressor rather than an electrical generator.

Current Orders & Customers

INNIO has secured several large data center customers and is ramping deliveries against a growing order book. Equipment backlog reached $8.0bn at September 2026, up from $6.6bn in June and $3.3bn a year earlier. Q3 Equipment bookings of $2.2bn represented a 2.8x book-to-bill ratio. Data Centers accounted for approximately 83% of Equipment bookings, compared with 60% of Equipment revenue. This mix points to a further shift toward data center equipment as existing orders are delivered.

INNIO serves this market through both direct campus customers and power providers. VoltaGrid is its largest publicly disclosed equipment relationship, with 3.8GW of cumulative announced orders. An undisclosed U.S. campus developer/operator has ordered a further 1.72GW, while Liberty Energy placed a 450MW firm order in Q3. Rehlko and Aggreko provide additional routes to market through multi-year capacity agreements, although these commitments should be distinguished from booked Equipment orders.

Reciprocating Engines 101

A gas reciprocating engine burns natural gas to generate electricity using pistons, much like a gasoline car engine. Combustion drives pistons back and forth inside cylinders, turning a crankshaft connected to an electrical generator. “Reciprocating” refers to this back-and-forth motion.

The engine and generator together form a generator set, or genset. Multiple gensets operate in parallel to supply a larger load. Developers can add units as a campus expands and take individual machines offline for maintenance while the others continue operating.

Compared with gas turbines, reciprocating engines differ mainly in unit size, operating flexibility and plant economics. They typically provide smaller blocks of power, allowing developers to add capacity as campus demand grows. Operators can switch individual engines on or off and maintain them separately. The trade-off is a greater number of machines and maintenance events for a given plant size.

Gas turbines generally provide larger blocks of power, reducing the number of units required for a large campus. Simple-cycle turbines generate electricity directly from the turbine, while combined-cycle plants recover exhaust heat to produce additional electricity through a steam turbine. Combined-cycle plants offer higher full-load efficiency but require more equipment and construction. Packaged aeroderivative turbines can be deployed more quickly and compete directly with reciprocating engines in BTM projects.

For AI data centers, reciprocating engines may be better suited to phased deployment and changing loads, while turbines can be attractive when developers need larger blocks of sustained power. The choice ultimately depends on equipment availability, redundancy requirements and lifetime cost.

Containerized Gensets

Containerization packages a genset and its supporting equipment in a purpose-built enclosure before delivery. Depending on the configuration, the package may include controls, ventilation, cooling connections, exhaust equipment and noise protection. Some auxiliary systems remain outside the enclosure.

The main benefits are less onsite assembly and a more repeatable installation process. Developers can deploy an initial group of modules and add more as subsequent campus phases open. Containerization changes how the equipment is packaged and installed; it does not change the underlying engine technology.

Each module still requires foundations, fuel connections, cabling and commissioning. Multiple modules connect through switchgear and transformers to form the power plant. A containerized genset is one building block of the plant, not a complete power solution on its own.

Competitive Landscape

INNIO competes with established engine manufacturers rather than a fragmented group of new entrants. Its closest competitors in modular gas generation are Caterpillar, Cummins and Rolls-Royce mtu. Larger campuses also bring it into competition with Wärtsilä and Everllence, whose engines deliver more power per unit. Competition increasingly depends on plant architecture and execution: capacity increments, redundancy, delivery slots and service support all influence supplier selection.

INNIO’s roughly 4.5MW J624 sits between smaller gensets and Wärtsilä’s larger engines. Smaller units allow capacity to be added in finer increments and reduce the output lost when one engine is unavailable, but more machines are required for a given plant size. Larger engines reduce the unit count but increase the capacity affected by each outage or maintenance event. Manufacturers also differ in the level of system integration they provide. Equipment may be sold as a genset, a containerized module or part of a broader power-plant package. Distributors and integrators can add cooling, emissions treatment, electrical equipment and site controls. As a result, equipment prices are difficult to compare unless the scope of supply is consistent.

Our channel checks highlight Caterpillar’s U.S. dealer network and Jenbacher’s global technical support and fuel flexibility. Customers also assess local technician coverage, spare-parts availability, maintenance arrangements and response times. Procurement decisions therefore reflect the full operating setup, as well as the engine’s stated output and efficiency.

Valuation & Setup

INIO trades at 13.8x forward EV/EBITDA, ~21% below its three-month average of 17.5x and ~43% lower than the post-IPO multiple of 24x. The stock has not rerated despite a strong 3Q26 beat and raised guidance, an Equipment backlog approaching $8bn and book-to-bill reaching 4x for its data center orders. Among BTM power providers, INNIO appears underappreciated given the performance of its reciprocating engines and containerized solutions, which are well suited to data center buildouts.

We believe the discount reflects concerns about AI infrastructure financing across BTM power providers, as well as the approaching sponsor lock-up expiry. Management has reported no meaningful exposure to neocloud customers and says it prioritizes customers with secured, long-term hyperscaler offtake agreements.

Our reverse SOTP analysis implies 2027E EBITDA of ~$564mn for the legacy compression and industrial businesses, supported by Services revenue from the existing fleet. We view this valuation as attractive given the equipment growth opportunity beyond 2030 and the rising contribution from Services. However, the valuation of the legacy business assumes costs can be reduced if data center demand slows.

The November 30 lock-up expiry complicates the near-term setup. Approximately 646.5mn sponsor shares—86% of shares outstanding and roughly 6.2x the existing public float—will become eligible for sale, although actual selling remains uncertain. Potential supply could weigh on the multiple even as earnings grow. Potential 4Q26 catalysts include the delivery ramp, FY27 guidance, any increase in the capacity target, gross-margin performance and conversion of slot reservations into firm orders. INNIO has not named the hyperscaler customer behind the 1.7GW data center order. Our channel checks point to a healthy pipeline; further customer announcements and upward revisions to Services expectations could support a rerating.
