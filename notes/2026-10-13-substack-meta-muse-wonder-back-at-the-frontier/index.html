2026.10.13 | Substack | META | META Muse Wonder: Back at the Frontier

原标题：Research|META: Muse Wonder Returns Meta to the Frontier as Compute Tightens

#llm #internet

Substack · 2026-10-13

On October 12, Meta Superintelligence Labs (MSL) released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, the models previously referred to in reports as Watermelon. These are Meta’s first frontier models since Llama 4, arriving 14 months after Alexandr Wang reorganized the company’s AI teams in August 2025. Both were trained on Meta’s Prometheus cluster in Ohio, support text, image, audio, and video inputs, and have a 1M-token context window. Neither model’s weights are publicly available.

Wonder is available through the Meta Model API and Muse Code at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens. Wonder Full is available only to Muse Code subscribers on the $50 and $200 monthly plans, with no API access. Our channel checks indicate that Wonder has roughly 3T total parameters, making it smaller than both Astra and Fable.

We believe Meta is back among the leading frontier AI labs, and we expect it to stay there. In our view, a year ago that group consisted mainly of OpenAI and Anthropic. Google rejoined this year, followed by Meta, and a four-way contest is taking shape. The hiring spree in the summer of 2025 is paying off: backed by substantial investment in compute, the new team brought Meta back to the frontier in just over a year.

Compute remains the biggest bottleneck. According to media reports, in July Meta was preparing to rent out surplus GPUs and was in talks with Anthropic; by October, its own needs had forced it to shelve the plan. Anthropic, meanwhile, secured new capacity from other suppliers and raised usage limits when it launched Opus 5.5. We expect the shortage to ease only once Vera Rubin and TPU 8t ship in volume in 2027. We also expect Meta’s 2027 capex to rise again. On open-weight models, we maintain our September view that the gap between the leading open-weight models and the top closed models will widen again to 6 to 12 months.

Meta returns to the top tier

The independent evaluations put Wonder Full among the leading models. As of October 12, the Artificial Analysis (AA) Intelligence Index, a weighted composite of 10 evaluations, scores Opus 5.5 at 58; Wonder Full and Sonnet 5.5 at 56; Astra, Gemini 4 Argon, and Fable 5.1 at 53; and Wonder at 52. AA flags verbosity as a weakness: Wonder Full used 140 million output tokens to complete the full suite, versus a median of 88 million across the comparison group on that page. AA estimates the average cost per index task at $4.52 for Wonder, $5.98 for Opus 5.5, and $3.26 for Astra.

Meta’s model card compares models at the reasoning settings specified in its comparison. Wonder Full’s strengths are in long-horizon agent tasks and professional work. On SWE-Marathon it scores 57.5 at xhigh, against 42.5 for Astra and 46.3 for Opus 5.5; on DeepSWE it scores 79.0, almost 5 points ahead of both. On Harvey’s legal agent benchmark, Wonder Full, Astra, and Opus 5.5 score 13.1, 8.6, and 4.2, respectively. Absolute scores are low, but Wonder Full’s lead is substantial. On JobBench it scores 75.3, essentially tied with Opus.

Wonder Full trails Astra and Opus in terminal-based coding and healthcare. It scores 57.1 on Terminal-Bench 4.0-AA, 2 to 2.5 points behind Astra and Opus, and 62.3 on HealthBench Pro, about 3 points behind. On MemoryBench, Meta’s internal benchmark, Wonder scores 85.6 and Wonder Full 85.5, versus roughly 81.5 for Opus 5.5 and Astra. Memory is a key focus of this release, though these results come from Meta’s own testing.

In June 2025, Meta paid $14.3b for a 49% stake in Scale AI and brought in Wang as Chief AI Officer. Nat Friedman and Daniel Gross joined soon after. In August, Meta reorganized its AI operations into what is now MSL and recruited researchers from OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Apple. Reported compensation packages ran to nine figures, and OpenAI CEO Sam Altman publicly cited $100m signing bonuses at the time. The hiring drew plenty of skepticism: Meta could pay to attract talent, but whether it could build a frontier lab remained an open question.

Just over a year later, MSL has delivered Wonder. Planning for Wonder, internally codenamed Watermelon, began in August 2025; the Spark line carries the codename Avocado. The team first validated its training recipe, data, and RL environments on a small model, then increased model size by a factor of 4 to 5 with each generation. Spark only began pretraining at the end of 2025, and Wonder Full now scores 56 on the independent index, tied with Sonnet 5.5 and second only to Opus 5.5. In our view, this represents faster iteration than Meta achieved in the Llama era.

We expect Meta to remain in the top tier. The team has proven itself, the Prometheus expansion and the Hyperion build-out will add compute, and Meta’s existing apps provide established distribution channels. We believe progress on the next model depends mainly on when that new capacity comes online.

Specialized models and existing users support personal assistants

Subscribed

Wonder targets different use cases from Astra and Fable. Wang said on a podcast that Meta places greater emphasis on personal and professional agents, whose capabilities standard benchmarks do not fully capture. Active Memory retains a user’s personal and professional context across conversations. Active Focus helps an agent work through obstacles by adjusting its approach, while checking with the user before consequential actions. The demos included filing an insurance claim, reading a lease, managing personal finances, planning a running route, and choosing gifts based on Instagram photos.

Users primarily access Muse through the app, which launched on September 8 and has reportedly reached 6.6 million downloads and 1.8 million daily active users. Other ways to access it include smart glasses, the Muse Charm device due to ship in December, and HomeLink, which connects to Sonos and lighting systems. Public usage reports put the free tier at 100 million tokens per week, with paid plans at $20 and $100 per month.

Startups are already competing in this market. Instinct was founded in 2025 by Noah Shinn, an author of the Reflexion paper. Users interact with it by text message or phone call. Once users grant it access to their email, calendar, screen, and location, it can order a ride, schedule repairs, and make restaurant reservations. According to public reports, it entered private beta in February, raised a $250m Series B at a $2.5b valuation in August, and closed a $1b Series C at a $10b valuation on September 28. Its invite-only user base passed 100,000 in mid-September. The funding reflects investors’ expectations for the personal assistant market; it remains to be seen how well the product retains users and converts them to paid plans.

We believe personal assistance and coding place different demands on the base model: for a personal assistant, memory, tool use and task coordination, and reliability matter more than peak intelligence. OpenAI released its own agent, Dots, three weeks after the launch of the Muse app.

Meta’s advantages are a model trained specifically for personal assistants, an app portfolio reaching more than 3 billion users, and multiple hardware entry points. Muse currently serves users in the US and Canada. Meta’s launch announcement already describes access through WhatsApp; wider distribution there and future Instagram integration offer further growth opportunities. The basic service is free, with revenue from subscriptions and a percentage of transaction value.

Muse’s architecture also increases server-side costs. Meta says Muse provides a dedicated virtual machine for each user. Public user tests have observed configurations of about 2 vCPUs, 8 GB of memory and 100 GB of storage. These observations are not a guaranteed specification for every user, nor do they imply continuously reserved physical resources of the same size. CPU, memory and storage add costs alongside GPU inference; capacity needs depend on active usage, concurrency and resource scheduling.

We expect personal assistants to follow the same path as coding agents: a general-purpose model paired with a task-execution framework (harness) first produces a usable agent, then models trained specifically for the task pull ahead. We view Wonder as the first frontier model trained specifically for this use case. Combined with Meta’s existing distribution, we believe it gives the company the strongest position in this market. We expect startups such as Instinct to face greater competitive pressure over the next two quarters.

Training and inference demand drive further capital spending

On July 1, Bloomberg reported that Meta was preparing “Meta Compute,” a plan to rent surplus AI capacity to external customers, much as CoreWeave does. Infrastructure head Santosh Janardhan, MSL’s Daniel Gross, and Meta President Dina Powell McCormick were leading the effort. On July 17, CNBC and the New York Times reported that Anthropic was in talks to lease compute from Meta in a deal worth $10b over two years.

At the time, Meta’s stock was trailing the S&P 500 for the year, and investors worried that the company had bought more GPUs than it needed. Shares rose 10% on the day of the Bloomberg report as investors saw leasing as a way to put excess capacity to use. At its late-July earnings call, Meta raised the lower end of its 2026 capex guidance from $125–145b to $130–145b.

Three months later, Meta is keeping that capacity for itself. Muse launched on September 8, and the next day Wang said usage had far exceeded projections, with users consuming 10 times as many tokens as internal test groups. Meta subsequently reset token usage counters and raised limits. On October 9, the Wall Street Journal reported that Meta had shelved the plan to lease compute to Anthropic. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei had approached Wang directly earlier in the year, but rising training and inference demand for the Muse models led Meta to retain the capacity. Anthropic’s position as a direct competitor also influenced the decision.

On a podcast in October, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg again described compute as constrained, saying that even an H100 is worth more now than it was a year ago. This also underpins his preference for owning data centers: fully depreciated hardware can remain in use. He estimated that a 1 GW cluster costs $50–60b to build and about $15b a year to operate. Wang said tens of billions of dollars was a reasonable estimate for the cost of training a frontier model. Prometheus is being expanded beyond 1 GW, and Hyperion in Louisiana is planned at 5 GW, with full build-out still several years away.

Anthropic’s compute supply has improved recently. Opus 5.5, released September 22, has been well received and leads the Artificial Analysis index at 58. Anthropic says typical workloads at default settings cost 40% less than on Opus 5. Output is more than 30% faster, and performance matches Fable 5.1 on most tasks at 40% of its token prices. At launch, Anthropic raised the 5-hour usage caps for its Pro, Max, and Team plans and offered a one-time limit reset that users can save for later.

Our channel checks indicate that Anthropic recently secured additional compute from several suppliers, including on the order of 100,000 TPU v7 chips, which we believe helped support the higher usage limits. Based on that channel information, we forecast that its available compute in 26Q4 may temporarily approach or even exceed OpenAI’s. Its release cadence and usage limits over the next quarter or two will help test that view. Capacity under the multi-gigawatt agreement signed with Google and Broadcom in April starts coming online in 2027.

Anthropic has spent the past year seeking additional compute. That need is evident in Amodei’s direct approach to Wang and public reports of Anthropic leasing spare capacity at xAI’s Memphis cluster through SpaceX. The new supply gives Anthropic room to raise limits, but it still has no surplus. Meta, meanwhile, has shelved its leasing plan. Both companies remain constrained by compute supply.

Meta needs more compute for both training and inference. Our channel checks suggest its next model will be several times the size of Wonder. At roughly 3T parameters, Wonder is still smaller than Astra and Fable, leaving room to scale pretraining. RL and agent environments require substantial additional compute. On the inference side, Muse reportedly reached 1.8 million daily active users within a month, and demand should rise as WhatsApp usage grows and Instagram integration develops. Restricting Wonder Full to subscribers, with no API access, is consistent with limited inference capacity, though the access policy alone does not establish a shortage.

Our channel checks indicate that GB300 is currently the scarcest resource and that Meta has placed large orders for Vera Rubin. We expect Vera Rubin shipments to start ramping in 26Q4 and continue to increase through the first half of 2027, in time for the next model’s pretraining. We expect Meta’s 2027 capex guidance to rise again, and GB300 supply to remain tight until Vera Rubin ships at scale.

Compute constraints may widen the open–closed model gap

Meta was once a leading developer of open-weight models; Llama 3.1 405B was the strongest open model of its day. Neither Wonder nor Wonder Full has publicly available weights. Meta did release Muse Glimmer (30B, Apache 2.0) in August and has promised to release the weights for Spark 1.2, but the latter is a small model from six months ago. Zuckerberg’s August essay, “The Future Is for Everyone,” still argues against the concentration of power, yet Meta has shifted to closed models at the frontier.

MiMo, GLM, and Kimi now lead among open-weight models. As of October 12, on the Artificial Analysis index, Xiaomi’s MiMo-V2.6-Pro scores 46, Zhipu’s GLM-5.3 scores 45, and Moonshot AI’s Kimi K3 scores 44. The top tier of closed models scores 53 to 58, a lead of 7 to 12 points over the best open-weight model. Over the past six weeks, Astra, Opus 5.5, and Wonder have been released in quick succession, while the highest open-weight score has remained at 46.

Kimi K3 has 2.8T total parameters, close to Wonder’s roughly 3T, yet scores 44 against Wonder’s 52 and Wonder Full’s 56. Given Kimi K3 and Wonder’s similar parameter counts, we see data, RL and compute investment as likely contributors to the gap. Total parameter count alone cannot isolate differences in architecture, active parameters or training methods. Adoption is not the issue: by May, open-weight models led by Qwen and Kimi accounted for 60% of tokens on OpenRouter. The most capable models, however, remain closed.

As we discussed in our September 28 note on distillation, open-weight models have narrowed the gap mainly through distillation during midtraining, using frontier model outputs as training data to fit the teacher model’s distribution. This requires relatively little compute, allowing open-weight models to trail by just two or three months despite a compute gap of one to two orders of magnitude.

That route is narrowing. The three closed-model developers discussed in that note no longer return full chains of thought in plain text, and training on summaries alone loses most of the benefit. We therefore maintain our view that, as with R1, the competitive challenge posed by open-weight models to the top closed models is likely to prove a one-off. We expect the gap between the leading open and closed models to widen again to roughly 6 to 12 months.

The stages after midtraining all require compute. As distillation data becomes harder to obtain, developers rely primarily on synthetic training data. Generating that data also consumes inference compute. RL requires large-scale rollouts, and agent environments must be run repeatedly. Every new idea has to be tested, and teams with less compute can run fewer experiments. Leading open-weight developers have lower labor costs, but that does not reduce these computing requirements. We believe most of the capital they raise goes toward compute. Public reports indicate that Moonshot AI paused new K3 subscriptions less than three days after launch because demand was approaching its compute capacity.

We expect a divide to emerge among open-weight developers in this round of competition. A handful of companies that can secure compute, including DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Zhipu, and Xiaomi, should be able to keep the gap with the top closed models at 6 to 12 months. The rest will drop out of frontier competition. With Meta’s frontier models now closed, we expect future open-weight frontier models to come only from this group.

Industry implications and what we’re watching

Meta’s decision to shelve its leasing plan further supports our view that capex will keep growing in 2027. All four frontier labs are adding compute for their next models, and inference usage continues to exceed expectations. The hardware implications are the same as in our Astra note: larger pretraining runs require larger scale-up domains and more cross-rack interconnect, while rising inference volumes increase storage demand and the need to reduce per-token costs. Meta is now increasing spending on both training and inference.

We are watching how additional compute translates into product capabilities: whether Meta’s next model training schedule aligns with Vera Rubin deliveries; how Muse’s daily active users and usage limits change as WhatsApp usage grows and Instagram integration develops; and whether Anthropic continues to relax API rate limits and Claude Max caps as new compute comes online.

For open-weight models, we are watching whether the Spark 1.2 weights are released as promised and what leading developers disclose about RL scale and synthetic data in their next models. Those releases will test our view that the gap will widen to 6 to 12 months.
