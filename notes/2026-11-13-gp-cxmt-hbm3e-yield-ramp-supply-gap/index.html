  8. 专家预计即便良率提升，中国HBM供给缺口仍将维持在约20%–30%，因为昇腾、寒武纪等客户需求同样在扩张；2027年月产5.5万片晶圆的预测被认为可实现，新建一座晶圆厂比良率提升10个百分点（约5000片）更有效。
  9. 长科存储（YMTC）凭借NAND混合键合（Xtacking）经验在HBM4上可能更具优势，但尚未产出DRAM晶粒；长鑫在HBM与常规DRAM间的产能分配将兼顾利润与国产算力政策导向，明年更可能向算力倾斜。

SUMMARY

Overview

The expert, drawing on experience in memory process development and industry contacts, discussed CXMT’s HBM3E manufacturing progress, yield improvement, packaging challenges, capacity plans, and the impact on China’s constrained HBM supply. The expert emphasized that reported early yields reflect trial production and that factory-scale execution will determine how quickly production becomes more reliable.

Key Insights

The reported 20%–30% starting yield uses a broadly comparable basis to that used by other manufacturers. The expert views this as evidence of technical feasibility, not proof of mature mass production. A yield near 50% after four to six months was considered reasonable, while matching leading suppliers’ 60%–70% levels was seen as less certain. Process adjustments take time because production runs are long and many steps interact. Bonding and micro-bumps were identified as the most immediate HBM3E yield hurdles, particularly due to interface resistance, equipment precision, and contamination control. Selling a version with somewhat relaxed performance specifications could temporarily ease shortages, since domestic customers may not always need peak speeds. The expert described this as a transitional approach as process uniformity improves. The expert expects China’s HBM supply gap to remain around 20%–30%, even as CXMT improves yields, because customer demand is also rising. Capacity expansion, rather than yield gains alone, is viewed as essential. HBM production may compete with conventional DRAM for resources. The expert expects commercial returns and policy priorities around domestic computing capacity to both influence allocation.

BC: Across CXMT’s entire HBM production process, from front-end etching and deposition to bonding, testing, and packaging in the back end, which module will have the steepest yield learning curve going forward, or which module will present the most severe constraint?

CL: I think the more immediate issues are bonding and micro-bumps. HBM is the first time they have used this bonding process, so it will certainly be the first major hurdle they face. These micro-bumps and bonding processes involve interfacial resistance. Higher interfacial resistance creates what we call RC delay. The R refers to resistance, and RC delay essentially determines speed. Higher resistance slows speed and increases power consumption because the electrical current cannot pass through smoothly. This is also why they need to slightly reduce the JEDEC frequency. I think this is where the issue lies. Bonding also involves equipment precision, vacuum quality, and environmental contamination, all of which affect the cleanliness of the interface between the two surfaces and the resistance encountered when electrons pass through the interface. I think the equipment also needs to be adjusted. They are facing this new technology for the first time, so these will certainly be some of the difficulties they must overcome.

Whenever a new module is introduced, it generally becomes a bottleneck. Samsung previously used a bonding film but encountered difficulties at this stage, so it eventually switched back to the technical route used by SK Hynix. Samsung also suffered significantly in this area and ultimately changed its technical route, which was how SK Hynix surpassed Samsung. Therefore, I estimate that CXMT has also invested substantial effort in this area to avoid similar problems.

BC: Regarding stack height, if CXMT moves to eight-layer stacks and later toward 12-layer stacks, what will the learning path look like?

CL: This is an extension of the same process. Along the same technical path, once overall yield improves, precision increases, and equipment is upgraded, adding more layers becomes relatively less difficult. HBM3E experiences thermal deformation, so controlling these effects and achieving mechanical precision involve a learning curve. They are already dealing with these problems. However, once an eight-layer stack has been successfully produced, moving to a 12-layer stack is a linear extension of the same path. It is mainly a matter of achieving more precise control. Therefore, I think this is only a matter of time. It does not involve fundamental changes to the entire technical path, design, and architecture, as HBM4 does.

Therefore, I think eight-layer and 12-layer stacking are extensions of the same technical path. They should first make the eight-layer stack work and improve its yield. A 12-layer stack is only truly needed when the data requirements are sufficiently large, and not all applications require it. Such a large number of stacked layers is only needed in systems that must move very large blocks of data and handle highly complex parameters. Frankly speaking, for most users in China, an eight-layer stack is sufficient.
