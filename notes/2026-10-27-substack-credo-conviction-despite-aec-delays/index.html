Credo Conviction Strengthens Despite 1.6T AEC Delays and Rising Competition

Timothy Chang, Max Xing

Monday, October 26, 2026

Executive Summary

  • Our conviction in Credo has strengthened despite 1.6T AEC delays and rising competition. We believe the market overstates these risks and underestimates the durability of Credo’s cable franchise and its expansion into optics.
  • SpaceX’s network changes could increase Credo’s revenue per GPU. Additional 800G cables and potential optical-module wins raise our modeled content from $1,400 (all 1.6T AEC case) to $1,468 per GPU, with potential upside to $2,340 from an all-ALC design. Qualification, timing, and supplier share determine that upside.
  • SpaceX’s capacity expansion offers further upward revision potential. Our year-end 2027 forecast of 7.1GW is already ~16% above the 6.1GW Street consensus. The 10GW and 13GW scenarios imply additional upside to Credo’s revenue, although we view these capacity outcomes as ambitious.
  • A larger AEC market can support strong Credo growth even as its share declines. MaxLinear’s investment reinforces our view that the market is still expanding. We are cautious about MaxLinear’s 20-30% share ambition, and Credo is now pursuing Samsung Foundry supply, which is the only reason we like MaxLinear. CPU front-end connectivity adds a ~$2.1B TAM opportunity by 2030, while our conditional CPU scale-up scenarios expand annual CPU AEC TAM to ~$10.5–19B after 2030.
  • ALC extends Credo’s cable franchise beyond copper’s reach. The micro-VCSEL pivot strengthens our confidence in its manufacturing path, while the 50m reach target opens links currently served by conventional optics. ZeroFlap provides a nearer-term optical growth driver, with co-packaged scale-up connectivity offering longer-term upside.
  • Credo’s valuation gives too little credit to these growth opportunities. At the October 23 close, Credo trades at around 10x multiple of our CY2027 earnings estimate. We believe that valuation underprices continued AEC growth and the expanding optical business.

Credo Remains Our Top Pick Amid Recent Market Chatter on Headwinds

Credo remains one of our top picks this year. Our earlier work defended AEC demand and Credo’s moat in qualification, firmware and complete cable assemblies. Our June update also outlined an optimistic case for $2.1B of incremental AEC TAM by 2030 from CPU front-end networks.

We remain confident Credo can reach the $5bn revenue midpoint in its PSU package. That target covers revenue over four consecutive quarters within the performance period through 2031, alongside separate share-price targets.

Our conviction has strengthened despite growing market concerns over delayed 1.6T AEC adoption and increased competition. We believe these concerns underestimate the durability of Credo’s cable franchise and its opportunity to expand beyond copper.

SpaceX’s Network Changes Can Even Raise Credo Revenue

SpaceX should be Credo’s most important customer in CY2027 and drive a sharp increase in AEC revenue. Recent market chatter suggests a longer reach requirement for SpaceX’s VR Oberon racks, raising concerns for Credo’s 1.6T AEC. We called this out in our previous note, and we believe the market is reacting too negatively to the news, given this could even be bullishing Credo.

We expect SpaceX to deploy three architectures in CY2027:

  • GB300: We model two 800G AECs per GPU, one for NIC-to-leaf and one for leaf-to-spine.
  • Vera Rubin (Oberon): We assume two 800G AECs per GPU on shorter links, potentially NIC-to-leaf. We pair these with 51.2T switches and 800G leaf-to-spine pluggable optics. We also see the possibility of an all-1.6T ALC case after 2H27.
  • Vera Rubin (Kessel): A Kyber-like design with a PCB midplane replaces the internal NVLink cable cartridges. Our best guess is that its denser architecture allows it to use two 1.6T ConnectX-9 devices and two OSFP cages per four-GPU tray. We expect SpaceX to deploy Kessel later than standard Oberon; the later date gives suppliers more time to qualify 1.6T products. We therefore retain all-1.6T AECs on Kessel for NIC-to-leaf and leaf-to-spine.

At the center of the market’s debate is VR Oberon. We expect Oberon to retain AECs on short connections, initially at 800G. Reach and product readiness remain the main constraints for 1.6T AECs. Our June update described potential 102.4T switch delays and customers who used two 800G cables with 51.2T switches. The extended use of 800G can increase Credo’s revenue per GPU. At our model ASPs, two 800G AECs generate $1,000 on the NIC stage, compared with $700 for one 1.6T assembly. That is ~43% more cable revenue at this stage.

Credo can also compete for the links that move to optics. Two 800G leaf-to-spine links require four transceivers across their endpoints. At our $585 module ASP, they carry $2,340 of total content per GPU, compared with $700 for the 1.6T AEC they replace. Credo’s existing relationship with SpaceX supports our view that it can win part of this optical business. We assume Credo wins 20% of the leaf-to-spine optical modules and retains 100% of the modeled AEC business.

Lastly, ALC is also an attractive solution: it offers ~50m reach while retaining copper-level reliability. An all-1.6T ALC design would use two complete cables per GPU, one on each modeled hop. We price each ALC at $1,170, equal to our 1.6T transceiver ASP. At 100% Credo share, this gives $2,340 of steady-state content per GPU. However, we expect this to happen no earlier than 2H27.

SpaceX’s network changes could allow Credo to earn more per GPU, even if 1.6T AEC adoption is delayed. Our four scenarios show why fewer 1.6T AECs do not necessarily mean less revenue for Credo. Under our assumptions, the hybrid design raises content from $1,400 to $1,468 per GPU, with further upside to $2,340 in the ALC case.

That upside depends on qualification and supplier share, and an all-optical design at 20% ZeroFlap share would reduce Credo’s content. We nevertheless think the market’s focus on potential AEC cancellations overlooks the revenue Credo could capture through a broader product mix.

SpaceX’s Capacity Offers Potential Upward Revisions

SpaceX’s capacity offers another source of upward revisions. Our Datacenter Model projects 7.1GW at year-end 2027, approximately 16% above the 6.1GW Street consensus. Musk’s previous ambition of approximately 10GW and latest 13GW+ target for year-end 2027 leave further upside, though we believe these targets aretoo ambitious. We are positive on SpaceX’s rapid construction and site conversion support this expansion. Onsite power generation also helps the company add capacity.

AEC Competition Validates Growth as CPU Scale-Up Expands Credo’s TAM

We remain bullish on Credo because we expect the AEC market to expand fast enough for Credo to sustain strong revenue growth even if its market share declines. MaxLinear’s Annapurna targets 1.6T AEC and onboard-retimer applications, with initial revenue expected in 2027 and a stronger ramp in 2028. Its investment in a multiyear-ramp reinforces our view against the Street’s AEC-is-dead thesis. Management outlined a 20–30% share ambition, though we are cautious on its delivery. We like MaxLinear only for its Samsung foundry exposure, and Credo is now also pursuing Samsung Foundry supply. Competition can reduce Credo’s percentage of the market while a larger market still delivers substantially more AEC revenue. We think the Street focuses too heavily on share loss and gives too little credit to market expansion, particularly CPU connectivity.

Agentic AI brings denser CPU racks. Nvidia’s Vera CPU Rack packs 256 CPUs, while Arm’s AGI platform supports configurations with 336 CPUs. Our June Credo update illustrated a ~$2.1B front-end AEC market by 2030, assuming one ~$300 cable for each of seven million CPUs in dense, AEC-cabled clusters. We believe the Street has yet to adequately price this opportunity, even before additional CPU scale-up demand.

PCIe/CXL connectivity could add substantially more cable content per CPU. Arm’s AGI CPU provides 96 PCIe Gen6 lanes with CXL 3.0 Type 3 support, and Credo demonstrated its Toucan Gen6 PCIe AEC for rack-level connectivity at OCP. Using a 96-lane CPU as an illustrative design, reserving 32 lanes for networking and storage leaves eight x8 links. If four to eight become externally cabled AEC connections to switches or memory devices, scale-up adds 4–8× the front-end cable content.

Extending that assumption across our seven-million-CPU population produces a post-2030 annual CPU AEC opportunity of ~$10.5–19B, including front-end connectivity. A 70% Credo share would imply ~$8.8B of incremental revenue. This is a long-term upside scenario conditional on fabric adoption, AEC attach rates, pricing and share. Our near-term forecast does not depend on this outcome, but we believe the potential for CPU connectivity to sustain AEC growth well beyond the current GPU deployment cycle is not reflected in Credo’s valuation.

Active Light Cables Take Credo’s Cable Franchise Past Copper’s Reach

At OCP, Credo unveiled the 1.6T ZeroFlap ALC, a pluggable cable that spreads each 200G electrical lane across many fibers, each driven by its own micro-emitter at a slow non-return-to-zero (NRZ) rate. Credo targets 50m of reach and a 100 million hour mean time between failures (MTBF), and against a comparable AEC claims 30% less power at 12W per cable end and a tenth of the weight. Shipments start in 2027, with first revenue in FY2028, ending April 2028. What we believe is important is the emitter. Credo bought Hyperlume for its microLEDs, and on its September 1 call still described the first product as microLED. At OCP, Bill Brennan said the first product uses micro-VCSELs. We were skeptical that microLED links would work in production. However, the micro-VCSELs pivot is why we now take ALC seriously.

The defensive case comes first. The Street assigns AEC little terminal value because copper runs out. Credo quotes 6.5m for 1.6T AECs, and we expect 448G lanes to shrink further. Beyond copper’s reach the link becomes pluggable optics, where Credo is a new entrant rather than the leader it is in AECs. The ALC keeps the whole link in Credo’s hands from 3m to 50m and makes it the third ZeroFlap pluggable after AECs and optics. This is a large opportunity since 1.6T becomes more than half of AI transceiver spend by 2028.

The offensive case is scale-up. We believe the micro-VCSEL architecture behind ALC could become a leading wide-and-slow CPO solution given its built-in redundancy and scalability. For the current design, each 200G electrical lane fans out into eight ~26G NRZ optical data channels, with another two for error correction, two spare channels, and one for management. Traffic can reroute around failed emitters, while increasing optical lane speeds to 50G offers a path to 3.2T. We see Feynman as the earliest potential entry point, with broader adoption beyond the 400G-per-lane generation. If Credo can carry this architecture from pluggable cables into co-packaged scale-up links, it could substantially expand its addressable market beyond scale-out.

Credo’s Valuation Understates Its Growth Opportunity

We think the market values Credo as if AEC growth will end, and its pivot into optics will be weak. At the October 23 close, Credo trades at no more than a low-teens multiple of our CY2027 earnings estimate. Marvell and Astera Labs trade at ~32.1x and ~39.6x, respectively, on calendarized consensus estimates. Lumentum and MaxLinear trade at ~26.3x and ~14.0x on the same basis.

Credo carries a large valuation discount on our forecasts, and we do not think differences in business mix justify it. We see Credo as the best “copper when we can, optics when we must” investment play. Its AEC franchise serves copper connections, while ZeroFlap and ALC position it to capture spending on links that move to optics.

We expect optical products to become a larger part of Credo’s business. Nvidia has qualified ZeroFlap optics, removing an interoperability barrier. We view Amazon adoption as likely in 2027, given the reliability and operating-cost case.

Credo is likely to reach management’s 50/50 optical-and-copper revenue ambition in CY2027. ZeroFlap drives that transition, with ALC offering further upside and room for AEC revenue to keep growing. The valuation gives too little credit to both the resilience of copper and the growth ahead in optics. Credo remains our top pick.
