In January Elon Musk told Larry Fink at Davos that the thing holding back AI was electricity.

“...we’ll be producing more chips than we can turn on.”

Elon Musk, World Economic Forum, Davos, January 22, 2026

Ten months later the evidence is in El Paso. Meta was told a grid connection for its new AI campus would take about five years, so it’s paying the local utility to build a 366 MW power plant beside the campus due in 15 months.

The industry calls this going behind the meter, because the power plant sits on the customer’s side of the utility meter. In September, Goldman Sachs raised its 2030 forecast for behind-the-meter power at data centers from 40 GW to 67 GW. Morgan Stanley estimates that 38 GW of the 116 GW US data centers need through 2028 has no power source yet.

Then came November 3. Democrats took the House, flipped seven Senate seats and won the governor’s races in Texas, Georgia and Ohio with candidates who want to pause new data centers. The market sold the companies tied to the AI buildout, including the ones that sell behind-the-meter power. Over three sessions Bloom fell 22%, INNIO 27% and ERock 26%.

We read the bills and the utility filings from both parties, and none of them limits how many data centers can be built.

The Hawley-Blumenthal bill asks whether a site above 20 MW gets all of its power from on-site generation. Trump’s pledge asks hyperscalers to “build, bring, or buy” their own. Texas Democrats want proof that households won’t pay more. Each one asks data centers to supply their own electricity and pay for it.

A data center can only meet that requirement with a power plant of its own, which is what Bloom, INNIO and ERock sell. Their shares fell on the fear of a ban in the same week both parties were proposing rules that send them customers.

By the end of this report, you’ll understand what the election slows down, why the air permit decides which power plants get built, and why we own one of these three stocks and are waiting on the other two.

What The Election Actually Slows Down

The election does not reach the data centers already being built. Their permits have been issued, and the Texas proposals apply to new ones.

SemiAnalysis estimates that roughly three quarters of the 33.8 GW due in 2027 across the top 20 states is already being built. The exposure is in 2028, because most of that year’s 62.2 GW has not broken ground and Texas is close to 30% of it.

What’s interesting is the freeze in Texas actually pre-dates the election.

On August 3 Greg Abbott ordered an audit of every data center in the grid queue, which his office put at about 474 GW, more than five times the state’s record peak demand. On September 21 he told the state’s environmental agency to stop issuing data center permits until that audit is done.

“Data centers must pay their own way, protect our grid and water, and complete the ERCOT and TWDB audits.”

Greg Abbott, Governor of Texas, September 21, 2026

Abbott’s September 21 letter directing TCEQ to pause data center permits. Source: Office of the Texas Governor

One detail works against our thesis. The agency says its pause covers any infrastructure directly supporting data centers. SemiAnalysis reads that as including the power plants a data center would build for itself. If that reading holds, the freeze also stops the on-site plants these three companies sell for as long as it lasts.

Gina Hinojosa inherits all of this on January 19.

Texas Governor Elect Gina Hinojosa

She gets Abbott’s tools without Abbott’s people. All three commissioners at the environmental agency are his appointees and her first open seat doesn’t arrive until August 2027. Her picks for the utility commission need two thirds of a state Senate that Republicans hold 19 to 12.

So she can extend the freeze but can’t ban data centers by herself. Anything lasting has to come from the Legislature.

Her campaign published six priorities for that session back in July.

Hinojosa’s “Texans First Data Center Priorities,” July 23, 2026. Source: ginafortexas.com

The six priorities are union labor, a say for local communities, an end to nondisclosure agreements, an end to subsidies, water contracts that can be voided if they threaten local supply, and one condition on cost.

“ensuring data center companies prove households won’t pay more for electricity or water”

Gina Hinojosa, Texans First Data Center Priorities, July 23, 2026

That condition and Abbott’s “pay their own way” are the same demand from opposite parties, and a data center that builds and pays for its own power plant has the simplest way to meet it.

So the election delays the Texas projects that don’t hold permits yet and leaves alone everything being built. When the pause lifts the first projects through the door will be the ones that bring their own power.

Which power plant those projects choose depends on the air permit.

Why The Air Permit Decides Who Wins

Before a data center can switch on its own power plant the state has to sign off on how much pollution that plant will put in the air. It has become the slowest and least certain step in building on-site power.

There are two kinds. A plant that emits less than 250 tons of smog-forming gas a year gets a quick state permit. The limit drops to 100 tons in places that already fail federal smog standards. Anything bigger needs a full federal review that can take 12 to 18 months.

ERock received the quick permit for Meta’s El Paso plant in under 45 days.

Developers have noticed where the line is. The Texas Tribune found a Vantage site in San Antonio permitted at 99.8 tons a year against a 100-ton limit. A CyrusOne site came in at 249.1 against 250.

Environmental groups argue these are really major polluters that skipped the full review.

At least 38 Texas data centers have used the quick permit for on-site power since 2024. That route is now getting squeezed from three sides.

Texas stopped issuing data center permits in September. Local opposition blocked or delayed at least 120 projects worth about $198 billion in the first half of 2026 according to Data Center Watch. And three environmental groups sued Vantage and VoltaGrid in federal court. They argue that 405 MW of gas plants in San Antonio should’ve gone through the full review.

The federal complaint against Vantage and VoltaGrid, Western District of Texas. Source: Environmental Integrity Project

The San Antonio site at the center of the lawsuit. Photo: Environmental Integrity Project

When permits get harder, the plant that emits the least has the advantage, and that’s a fuel cell.

An engine or a turbine burns its fuel and the burning is what creates the smog. A fuel cell turns the same natural gas into electricity with a chemical reaction and no flame. Oracle swapped the turbines and diesel generators planned for its New Mexico campus for up to 2.45 GW of Bloom fuel cells. It says smog-forming emissions at the site fell by about 92%.

Construction at Oracle’s Project Jupiter campus in New Mexico. Photo: Las Cruces Bulletin

“To get an air permit, there is no faster technology available to power the customer on.”

K.R. Sridhar, CEO, Bloom Energy, Q3 earnings call, October 22, 2026

Fuel cells don’t win everywhere though. BloombergNEF puts their power at about $140 per MWh against $103 for gas engines. Where the quick permit stays open an engine is cheaper and still years faster than the grid.

That sorts the three stocks in this report.

Bloom gains the most as permits get stricter. INNIO and ERock need the quick permit to stay open.

1. Bloom Energy ($BE): Five Years vs. 55 Days

Bloom Energy, one year through the November 13 close. Source: Public.com

On November 5, the third day of the selloff, Google announced a $17 billion data center campus in Wyoming’s Laramie County.

Google’s November 5 announcement. Source: blog.google

“We will pay for 100% of the power we use.”

Steve Walter, VP of Global Infrastructure and Energy, Google, November 5, 2026

That sentence is what Abbott, Hinojosa and the Senate bill are all asking data centers to say.

Source: Google, “Investing in Wyoming’s infrastructure, energy and workforce,” November 5, 2026

Most of that power will come from a 702 MW plant built from Bloom fuel cells.

Source: blog.google

AEP is investing about $4 billion in it through a separate company and Google signed on to buy the output for 20 years. The cost gets recovered from that one contract so it never touches a household bill.

AEP’s October 14 release on the 702 MW Bloom fuel cell project. Source: aep.com

Bloom Energy Servers at a customer campus. Photo: Jakub Mosur / Bloom Energy, CC BY 2.0

Bloom’s real product is speed. It had power running at Oracle’s site in 55 days against a plan of 90. A New Jersey planning board approved its fuel cells for a Nebius site within three months of signing.

Fuel cell power costs about $37 more per MWh than a gas engine. On a 100 MW site that’s roughly $29 million a year. SemiAnalysis figures the same 100 MW earns its owner $150 million to $333 million a year in rent.

So one year stuck in permitting costs five to eleven years of the fuel cell premium. A data center owner facing that delay will pay the premium.

Water points the same way. Bloom’s management says a water-cooled gas plant serving a 1 GW data center uses about 25 billion gallons over its life. Bloom uses roughly 1.4 million. Hinojosa’s sixth priority asks data centers to prove households won’t pay more for electricity or water.

Utilities have started buying too. AEP is paying about $4 billion for the Wyoming plant. Ameren Missouri’s 20-year plan calls for 500 MW of gas fuel cells by 2030.

“I expect to see significant wins in our utility related business.”

K.R. Sridhar, CEO, Bloom Energy, Q3 earnings call, October 22, 2026

We bought Bloom around $90 last December and sold at $274 in June for a gain of roughly 204%. We bought it again on the night of November 5 and it’s now 30% of the Asymmetric Bets portfolio.

The business has only gotten stronger since. Third quarter revenue was $1.24 billion against Wall Street’s $1.07 billion. Management started the year guiding to as little as $3.4 billion of revenue and now expects up to $4.6 billion. The stock closed at $263 on October 13 and trades near $192, so the guidance went up while the price fell.

Its adjusted operating margin was 8.9% a year ago and 23.1% last quarter. Much of a fuel cell factory’s cost is fixed, so profit grows faster than sales once the lines fill up. Management told JPMorgan in September that it expects to run 5 GW of output with the same headcount it uses for 1 GW.

The price reflects it. Bloom trades near $192, which is 59 times this year’s guided earnings and about 25 times what Wall Street expects in 2028. Hitting that 2028 number takes roughly 55% earnings growth every year until then.

In July, at a similar market value, Wells Fargo estimated the price implied 10 GW of data center orders over five years. Morgan Stanley expects Bloom to ship those same 10 GW through 2029, and Bloomberg Intelligence expects 15 GW from every fuel cell maker combined through 2032. The price already assumes Bloom wins most of that market, so the upside depends on the new rules making it bigger than those forecasts.

The risks start with the backlog.

“Our backlog is continuing to expand faster than our revenue.”

K.R. Sridhar, CEO, Bloom Energy, Q3 earnings call, October 22, 2026

The $20 billion backlog is softer than it sounds. Mizuho counted only $493 million of it as contractually firm. Bloom has promised its own suppliers $5.8 billion while customers have put down $754 million. Free cash flow was negative $181 million last quarter.

“We already view this as a self funding business.”

Simon Edwards, CFO, Bloom Energy, Q3 earnings call, October 22, 2026

Two customers made up 69% of revenue and one is a related party tied to Brookfield. If either one slows its orders, the quarter misses.

And a fuel cell still needs a gas pipe. Oracle sent a force majeure notice on its New Mexico campus after the pipeline serving it slipped by six months. Pipelines face the same local opposition as data centers.

This is the most expensive stock in the report and the one with the most proof behind it.

2. INNIO ($INIO): The Hundred-Year-Old AI Stock

INNIO since its June IPO. Source: Public.com

INNIO just reported the first billion-dollar quarter in its history. Sales rose 57%, adjusted EBITDA rose 70%, and the backlog more than doubled to $8.0 billion.

The stock trades at about half its IPO price from five months ago.

We can’t find the reason in the business. It makes gas engines and it went public into the hottest part of the AI trade. Then it sat through an October crash and an election in the space of five weeks.

INNIO is based in Munich. It has been refining engines for more than a hundred years under two brands called Jenbacher and Waukesha.

INNIO’s Jenbacher works in Jenbach, Austria. Photo: INNIO

For decades the customers were factories, utilities and gas pipelines.

Then AI data centers needed power sooner than the grid could supply it. Gas engines became power plants that could be ordered and delivered.

A 20-cylinder Jenbacher gas engine. Photo: Benedikt Schneider, CC BY-SA 3.0

INNIO now ships far more on-site power than Bloom does. It delivered 3.4 GW last year while Bloom will ship about 1 GW this year. Data centers were 60% of its equipment revenue last quarter.

The reason is price. Liberty Energy paid about $1.1 million per MW for its INNIO engines. BloombergNEF puts fuel cell equipment at roughly $2.4 million. A data center that has to buy its own power under either party’s rules gets twice the capacity from the same check.

Its prices are rising too. Bank of America calculates that recent orders were priced about 21% higher than the engines shipping today, and that increase reaches the income statement when those orders ship in 2027 and 2028.

“Let me emphasize our projects are progressing as planned with no delays or cancellations.”

Olaf Berlien, CEO, INNIO, Q3 earnings call, October 27, 2026

The engine sale is followed by decades of service revenue, the way a printer is followed by ink. Every engine needs parts and overhauls for as long as it runs. One recent contract covers service “into a second engine lifetime.” Services already brought in $390 million last quarter.

INNIO went public in June at $27 and trades around $14.57.

Down 46% from the $27 IPO price. Source: Public.com

Add its debt and the whole business is valued at about 11 times next year’s expected cash earnings. Bank of America’s peer group averages 22. On a P/E basis it’s roughly 24 times against Bloom’s 59.

Three things explain the discount.

On November 30 the lock-up expires on 646.5 million shares worth about $9.4 billion. Only around $1.5 billion of stock trades freely today. The IPO didn’t raise a dollar for INNIO either. Every share was sold by its existing owner.

The second is the air permit. Engines burn gas and large sites sit close to the line that triggers a full federal review. The San Antonio lawsuit targets a VoltaGrid plant and VoltaGrid builds its fleet on Jenbacher engines. INNIO’s largest customer was 41% of its orders last year, and INNIO has not named it. If it is VoltaGrid, the permit risk and the customer risk are the same risk.

“Our largest customer accounted for approximately 20% of order intake in 2024 and 41% in 2025.”

Olaf Berlien, CEO, INNIO, Q3 earnings call, October 27, 2026

The third is supply. Morgan Stanley estimates the industry is expanding toward 150 GW of annual capacity. Bloomberg Intelligence expects US data centers to need about 5 GW of on-site engines a year. Today’s pricing holds only while engines are scarce.

This is the cheapest way to own a lot of MW. It’s also the one most exposed to a judge in San Antonio.

3. ERock ($EROC): The Power Plant With Two Lives

ERock since its June IPO. Source: Public.com

Beside Meta’s new AI campus in El Paso a Houston company called ERock is lining up 813 natural gas generators in rows. Each one makes about 450 kilowatts. Wire them together and you have a 366 MW power plant.

Rows of ERock natural gas generators at a customer site. Photo: ERock

The whole thing will be running in roughly 15 months. The air permit took less than 45 days.

“We are compressing what would otherwise be an estimated five-year grid interconnection wait into roughly 15 months.”

John Carrington, CEO, ERock, Q3 earnings call, November 12, 2026

The plant cost about $430 million, which is $1.2 million per MW, or roughly 60% of what Bloomberg Intelligence assumes on-site gas power costs.

“EROC is effectively a pure-play speed-to-power platform.”

Morgan Stanley, “AI $1.6T Money Trail,” October 15, 2026

The plant is designed to have a second use.

The plant’s first life is as a bridge. Meta pays for the power while it waits for a real grid connection. Once that connection arrives the plant begins a second life as backup power the local utility can call on. Regulators in El Paso asked for exactly this. The data center pays and every other customer is held harmless.

That is the cost condition on Hinojosa’s list, already written into a Texas regulatory recommendation.

Demand has risen since the vote.

“I would say since the election, we have seen inbound interest picked up.”

John Carrington, CEO, ERock, Q3 earnings call, November 12, 2026

ERock also earns more the longer the grid takes. Customers used to need bridge power for about three years and now ask for five to seven, and ERock collects roughly three times the service revenue during those years.

The numbers are starting to show it. Third quarter revenue was $153 million. That’s almost four times the $40 million of the quarter before. Backlog is about $1.9 billion counting orders signed since September and that’s more than the whole company is worth.

The issue is that ERock barely makes money yet. Its guidance implies about $80 million of annualized cash earnings which puts the stock at roughly 23 times. Count the insider units that convert to shares later and it’s 28.

“The projects we are delivering on in 2026 and 2027 generally have margins in the high teens to low 20s.”

Ian Blakely, CFO, ERock, Q3 earnings call, November 12, 2026

The upside is in the backlog. At those margins it holds $340 million to $400 million of gross profit, in orders that ship over the next sixteen months or so. Anyone buying at $8.41 is paying for that to arrive on schedule.

The $549 million of cash is matched by $588 million that customers have prepaid for work ERock hasn’t delivered. Most of that pile is other people’s money. One counterparty was 68% of revenue last quarter.

The lock-up expires December 7 on 191.5 million shares. Only 27.9 million trade today.

Texas can still hurt it in two ways. The utility commission hasn’t issued its final order on El Paso. ERock’s plants also rely on the quick air permit that Abbott’s freeze and the San Antonio lawsuit both put in question.

This is the smallest and riskiest stock of the three. It’s also the one with the most room to run if El Paso becomes the template for everyone else.
