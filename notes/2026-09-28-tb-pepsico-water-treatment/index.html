[00:08:55] We were trying to learn more about the water treatment industry. What was PepsiCo using water treatment solutions for? What went into the decision-making for when you needed to use it?

Expert [00:09:32]: We were using water treatment solutions in two broad areas. Actually, I would say three, but one and two are kind of similar. One was the incoming water that we received either from the city or from bore wells. In a couple of locations, we had our own bore wells. Out of the 65 facilities I mentioned, for the most part, we received water from the city, a little different from internationally. Internationally, you either take water from a river. Sometimes it's from the city, but oftentimes, it's not directly from the city. You either take it from a river or you have your own bore well. Now the water that the city provides you, even though it meets drinking water standards, according to the WHO, it does not meet the ingredient water standards. I'm differentiating between the two, drinking water and ingredient water, it does not meet ingredient water standards for a beverage company. The reason for that is that the water might contain some chemicals. It might contain, for example, a little bit of iron, a little bit of sodium, a little bit of potassium, some sulphate, some nitrate, which are all reasonably okay for health. They fall within the WHO mandate, within limits, of course, but if they are those contaminants within those regulated limits, they are actually perfectly fine for health. You won't get sick drinking that water.

However, those contaminants react with our formulas. When I'm making Pepsi-Cola, my Pepsi contains certain concentrate. If I have nitrate or sulphate or potassium or magnesium or iron, it affects that concentrate and therefore, affects the taste. The water that comes out of the city to us, even though it is good for drinking water, it is not good for ingredient water. We need to take out whatever those little chemicals are in order for it to perform properly with our concentrate and produce the product that we need to sell. That's one application. The second application comes in process and utility. We have boilers, compressors, cooling towers, stuff like that. Those are mostly for the most part. We are getting away from it now as part of the sustainability programme, but for the most part, those machines are water cooled. Water cooling means you're recirculating water. Obviously, it's not a once-through process. If it's once through, you'll be wasting a lot of water. You're recirculating water. As you're recirculating water, some of the water because of the heat generated by these machines, some of the water is getting evaporated, so the concentration of chemicals that is getting left behind, chemicals don't get evaporated, so whatever is in the water, over time, as you keep recirculating and the water part gets evaporated, the chemicals left behind, they start forming a scale.

When they form a scale and they deposit in your machine and in your pipeline and your cooling towers and so on, the effectiveness of everything drops. You consume more energy for less output. You need to treat the water to prevent the scale formation. The third area is in wastewater treatment. We discharge a lot of wastewater as part of our ongoing operations. Now waste stream water contains sugar because our product is sugary. It contains acids because our product contains some acid. It contains cleaning chemicals because we clean our equipment from time to time. It contains lubricants. We have machinery and then we put oil and grease inside the machinery. Sometimes there's a little sloppy operation, some of that gets in. It contains other contaminants like lubricants for our conveyors, definitely from the facilities, the locker rooms and the kitchens from our factories. Sometimes it contains solid materials like an errant cap, an errant bottle, an errant can, all of that goes in.

All that water with all this stuff mixed in is not acceptable to the local municipal authorities, so we need to treat that water to meet certain standards before we can discharge it to them. That is the second application. The applications are ingredient water, process and utility water and wastewater. I mentioned that one and two are kind of similar. What we are trying to do is instead of having a water stream for ingredient and having a separate water stream for process and utilities, we have been examining and implementing that why can't the ingredient water go into the process and utilities. It's a little bit of an overkill, but there are benefits to doing that because ingredient water is very clean, so there's very little chemicals in that. You can actually do many more turnarounds in the process and utilities before you need to blow down that water, whether it's a boiler or the compressor or something. There are economics around it, but there is definitely a business case where you overtreat the water, bring it to ingredient grade and even then use it for process and utilities. One and two are kind of similar, even though in many places, we do have a separate system for ingredient, separate system for process and then a separate system for wastewater.

[00:24:23] Do you have experience interacting with Gradiant? How does it compare with water treatment vendors such as Veolia, Aquatech and Xylem?

Now over time, the water treatment companies that we were dealing with, the Veolia, the Xylem, the PureChem and so on, these companies started improving their membrane technology. Obviously, it was a function of the quality of water coming in because if your water quality coming in is not the best, obviously, when you're extracting good water, the ingredient water, there is a concentration that's building up in the brine and there's a limit at which you can build up the concentration. That was the sweet spot between number of cleanings that were required, the membrane contamination, which required replacement of membranes, which is not cheap, downtime requirement and the quality of water and sustainability.
