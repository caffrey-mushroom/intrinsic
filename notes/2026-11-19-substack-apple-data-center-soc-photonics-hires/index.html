TL;DR

  • Apple’s careers site currently has four silicon photonics postings that deal with data center connectivity. One is a program manager with “Data Center SoC” in the title, and three look for the same optical packaging work at 3, 10 and 20 years of experience.
  • The annual report from the same month shows Apple’s capital expenditure fell close to 30% from a year earlier. It’s spending of a different order of magnitude from the hyperscalers.
  • Read as written, the postings put this light close to the data center SoC, attached at the package level. Words like mass production, BOM and vendor sit alongside.
  • One thing caught my attention while researching. The packaging posting points to the short distances inside the board as the next place optics will go. I think Apple’s optics are headed toward tying chip to chip more than toward linking rack to rack.

1. A title posted in early November

The posting that went up on Apple’s careers site in early November carries the title Silicon Photonics EPM, Data Center SoC. It’s based in San Diego and Cupertino. The same title has a posting record from September too, so either the seat hasn’t been filled or Apple is adding headcount. EPM stands for Engineering Program Manager, the person who ties together the schedules and deliverables of several design teams and drives them.

I came across this posting because I monitor silicon photonics job postings on a regular basis. I do it to track demand and trends, and Apple’s SiPh packaging postings were really interesting. I knew that Apple does data centers. But seeing a role with Data Center SoC in its title go up together with the packaging postings, I thought the move from copper to optics is now getting serious at Apple too.

The first sentence of the posting caught my eye more than the title did. It says silicon photonics (SiPh, a technology that builds paths for light on a silicon chip so data travels as light instead of electrical signals) and optical I/O are becoming the foundation of next-generation data center SoCs, and that electrical interconnects can no longer keep up on bandwidth, energy efficiency and reach. It’s a sentence I see every day in investor decks from optical component companies, and this time Apple wrote it in its own job posting.

It looked odd because of another number from the same weeks. In the FY2026 annual report Apple filed in early November, capital expenditure, or more exactly cash paid to acquire property, plant and equipment, was $9.0 billion. That’s down close to 30% from $12.7 billion a year earlier. A company with a market cap above $5 trillion (TradingView, close of November 17, 2026) is spending less on facilities while hiring people to attach light to data center chips.

So where does Apple plan to use this light? This piece tries to get closer to an answer by reading the postings line by line. A posting isn’t a product announcement, so I read only as far as the text goes.

2. Four postings

Search Apple’s careers site for photonics and about twenty-five postings come up (retrieved November 17, with multi-location duplicates counted once). Reading all of them as data center roles would be a mistake, though. Close to half belong to other teams. The Silicon Photonics Process Integration Engineer posting, for example, says in its body that it sits in the Biophotonics team. Going by the name, that’s the health sensor side. The laser component engineer postings belong to the camera organization that handles Face ID and LiDAR. There are at least two groups doing silicon photonics inside Apple, and this piece looks at one of them, the data center SoC side.

Filter those out and four remain.

The first is the EPM posting from above. Its scope is wide. It covers SerDes (the transceiver circuits that move data between chips at high speed), the amplifier that turns current from a photodetector into a voltage signal (TIA), the circuits that drive lasers and modulators, and silicon photonics engines and optical I/O subsystems, all of it from early concept through mass production. On the packaging side it lists 2.5D and 3D, plus near-packaged and co-packaged optics. CPO (co-packaged optics) puts the optical parts inside the same package as the chip, and near-packaged puts them on the board right next to the package. The outside companies to manage are foundries, OSATs (companies that do packaging and test on contract), and laser and optical component vendors.

Schematic cross-section of the optical I/O building blocks named in the EPM posting, in near-packaged and co-packaged placements

The other three all carry the title Silicon Photonics & Optical Packaging Eng. The body describing the work is the same and the minimum experience differs. The two posted in late October ask for 3 years or more and 10 years or more, and the one posted in mid November asks for 20 years or more. All three are based in Austin and the San Francisco Bay Area.

Posting date, role number and minimum qualifications from the three “Silicon Photonics & Optical Packaging Eng” postings on Apple’s careers site, stitched together. Source: Apple Careers, retrieved November 18, 2026

This packaging title didn’t first appear this fall. A posting with the same title went up on Apple’s careers site in October last year, and job mirror sites still show posting records from February, April and May this year. So Apple has been looking for optical packaging people for more than a year at least. What’s new this fall is that a program manager role with Data Center SoC in its title went up alongside.

Apple is looking for junior, senior and principal people for the same work all at once. Whether it’s growing an existing team or building a new one can’t be told from the postings. And these three don’t put Data Center SoC in the title. The body talks about data center connectivity and optical I/O, so I read them as the same effort as the EPM posting. The work in this posting is packaging silicon photonics engines and chiplets, designing and developing the process for the optical coupling that attaches single-mode fiber (fiber with a very thin core where light travels in only one mode) to the chip, and deciding the package BOM. It also says the role takes this from concept to high-volume production with optical component vendors, foundries and OSATs.

The words I put weight on here are mass production and BOM. Postings from research groups usually talk about exploration, demonstrations and papers. Setting a bill of materials and going to high-volume production is the vocabulary of a product organization. That’s the vocabulary, of course, and nowhere do the postings say which chip this goes into or when.

Why attaching single-mode fiber to a chip is hard was the subject of an earlier piece, The Silicon Photonics Foundry Layer: Who Can Actually Make a PIC . The point there was that yield is decided less at the wafer step than in the coupling and assembly that come after, and the seats Apple is filling are in that back half of the process.

Summary section of Apple’s “Silicon Photonics EPM, Data Center SoC” job posting. Source: Apple Careers, retrieved November 17, 2026

Beyond photonics, the data center hardware team has about twenty more job titles open. They’re roles like firmware for server management chips, system integration, debug and product design. One of them, the senior systems engineer posting, introduces the team this way. It builds the custom compute platforms that run behind Apple’s services, designs its own circuits and boards, and owns rack integration. It also explains that the systems span multiple physical racks, and that high-speed networking, storage, energy storage and custom compute have to work as one. I come back to this sentence in section 5.

3. What Apple spends on facilities

Before reading further into the postings, it helps to see how much Apple spends on facilities. The short version is that, measured by spending, Apple doesn’t belong next to the hyperscalers.

In its environmental press release this April, Apple put the number of data centers it owns at eight. Maiden, North Carolina is the oldest, and the others are in Oregon, Nevada, Arizona, Iowa, Denmark and elsewhere. In February 2025 it announced it would spend $500 billion in the US over four years and said it would build a server factory in Houston, and in August it raised the total to $600 billion. That number is a commitment that includes component purchases and payroll, so it shouldn’t be read as data center investment. The February announcement also says Apple will keep expanding data center capacity in North Carolina, Iowa, Oregon, Arizona and Nevada.

Actual capital expenditure is as described above. Over the past five years it moved between $9 billion and $13 billion a year, and in FY2026 it came down to the low end of that range. Amazon has guided this year’s capex to about $220 billion, and Meta to $135 billion to $145 billion. Alphabet talked about around $200 billion, and Microsoft about roughly $170 billion on a calendar-year basis. Apple’s number is a result for the fiscal year that ended in September, the other four are full-year outlooks, and each company treats leases differently. Even allowing for that, the gap is an order of magnitude.

Apple’s capital expenditure over five fiscal years next to the 2026 guidance of four hyperscalers

So where does Apple run its AI compute? In two places. One is Private Cloud Compute (PCC), installed in its own data centers. These are the servers that take requests an iPhone can’t handle on the device, and Apple built them on Apple silicon from the same family that goes into the Mac. The other is someone else’s cloud. This June Apple said it was extending PCC to Google Cloud, and it wrote directly that NVIDIA GPUs and Intel CPUs are used there. An architecture that started out on Apple silicon alone has moved outside.

The money flows the same way. On the earnings call last October, CFO Kevan Parekh said he doesn’t see Apple moving away from the hybrid model that uses its own capacity and third-party capacity together. On this November’s call he explained that cloud costs for Siri AI are booked in services cost of sales. In other words, money spent renting someone else’s cloud doesn’t show up in the capex line. So a drop in capex alone doesn’t tell you which way Apple’s AI spending is heading.

How much of its own server fleet is running is hard to see from outside. This March, The Information reported that only about a tenth of PCC server capacity was in use. That means most of the capacity Apple had secured was going unused, and the report also said some servers were still in warehouses and hadn’t been installed yet. Apple hasn’t confirmed that number, and it’s eight months old, so it doesn’t describe the current state either. Apple discloses annual electricity use for its data centers and colocation in its environmental report, but doesn’t break out how much of it is PCC servers. So I can’t put a number on the scale of its AI compute either. That’s a limit of this piece.

That was a lot, so let me sum it up a little. Apple’s capex is far smaller than the hyperscalers’, and it hands part of its heaviest compute to other people’s GPUs. That same company is hiring a team, tiered by experience, to attach light to data center SoCs. So which chip does that light attach to, and where?

4. When the M series becomes a server

To understand Apple’s servers you have to start with how M-series chips are built.

The M series is an SoC where Apple designs the CPU, the GPU and the Neural Engine for AI compute all by itself and puts them on one chip. It’s easy to assume the GPU comes from NVIDIA or AMD, but there’s no outside GPU in the M series. What Apple buys from outside is memory. It buys LPDDR memory, the same family used in smartphones, and mounts it right next to the chip. Counterpoint’s teardown shows LPDDR5X in the M5 Pro.

The heart of this design is that the CPU and GPU share the same memory. Apple calls it unified memory. In an ordinary server with discrete GPUs, the fast memory attached to the GPU and the memory on the CPU side are separate, while in the M series the CPU and GPU use the same physical memory. The M5 Ultra announced this August supports up to 512GB of it. Depending on model size and precision, that means a large language model can sit on a single chip. Apple building PCC servers on Apple silicon probably has something to do with this design. Apple has never said which chips go into PCC, though. This July there was a report that Apple’s internal AI servers run on M2 Ultra and are struggling on performance. That report came a month before the M5 Ultra was announced. In February there was also a 9to5Mac report that the model number of new server hardware using the M5 chip had turned up in a PCC software release, so the server chips may be in the middle of moving to the M5 family. How far that has gone isn’t known.

Things change once you leave the package and try to tie several chips together.

The M5 Ultra joins four dies inside one package. The M5 Max ties two dies together with what Apple calls Fusion Architecture, and the M5 Ultra connects two of those M5 Max units again with UltraFusion. Up to here it’s all inside the package. To leave the package and work with other chips as one body, you need a dedicated link that’s just as fast, but the M series was originally designed as a chip that goes one to a computer. Apple has never disclosed a dedicated link for tying dozens of chips together.

Concept diagram of the four dies and unified memory inside the M5 Ultra package, and the links outside it

When does this become a problem? More requests alone don’t cause one. You put copies of the same model on many servers and split the requests. Chip-to-chip communication starts to matter when a single model is spread across several chips. That happens when the model grows too big for one chip, and sometimes a model that does fit is split anyway to respond faster. A research post Apple published in October is an example. It tied up to four Mac Studios with M5 Ultra together over Thunderbolt 5, and found that even a model that fits on one machine ran faster when split across several. Four computers on a desk are tied together with Thunderbolt cables. What Apple would use to tie dozens of server accelerators together, it has never said. I read Apple putting its heaviest models on NVIDIA GPUs in Google Cloud as a sign that today’s Apple silicon servers can’t comfortably carry that work. Apple has never said so, and that’s my interpretation.

That’s why Apple is said to be making a separate server chip. It’s known by the code name Baltra, and The Information first reported in December 2024 that it was being developed with Broadcom. Since then reports on mass production timing have kept conflicting. In January this year analyst Ming-Chi Kuo talked about mass production in the second half of this year for Apple’s own server chip, and the July report mentioned above also said Baltra’s debut this year had fallen through and that Apple was looking into acquiring chip startups. All of this is reporting, and Apple hasn’t even confirmed the name Baltra.

There’s one more branch. In September a report from The Information said Apple is weighing a plan to sell AI servers built on M8-family chips to outside companies, and that it discussed with NVIDIA using NVIDIA’s NVLink Fusion to connect those chips to each other. According to the report, the reason is that scaling up Apple’s existing way of connecting chips runs into cost and speed problems. The report named 2029 as a possible launch and said the plan could be dropped before then. It isn’t a settled plan.

I gave this report a fair amount of weight. The weak spot I described above, the link that ties chip to chip, comes up as the issue here too. It comes from the same outlet as the July report, though, so it shouldn’t be read as two independent sources confirming each other. It also shows that Apple may not build the answer to that homework itself. NVLink Fusion is a way to borrow NVIDIA’s scale-up connectivity. In its late-August announcement NVIDIA wrote that this connection runs over photonics or electrical interconnects. So using NVLink Fusion alone doesn’t tell you whether the link is electrical or optical.

So whether this round of optics hiring belongs to Baltra, to M-series servers, or to the generation after that can’t be known from the postings alone. The EPM posting lists, as a preferred qualification, experience defining and driving IP and optical interconnect roadmaps across multiple generations. That line describes the background Apple wants in a candidate, so it can’t be used by itself to read Apple’s plan.

5. Where does this light attach

Optics sit in roughly three places in a data center. The oldest is the transceiver plugged into the front of the equipment. Next is switch CPO, where optical parts are attached next to the network switch chip. The last is optical I/O, where optics are attached to the compute chip’s package.

Separate from this split by place there is a split by use. A connection that ties several chips into one body is scale-up, and a connection between those tied-together blocks is scale-out. Place and use don’t map one to one. Transceivers and switch CPO mostly serve scale-out and optical I/O on the compute chip mostly serves scale-up, but there are exceptions. So you have to look at what is being connected to what.

Which one are Apple’s postings talking about? Lay the phrases out one at a time and they lean fairly hard to one side.

First, the thing the optics attach to is a “data center SoC.” None of the in-house chips Apple has disclosed so far is a network switch chip. So it’s natural to read this SoC as a compute chip. The posting doesn’t say what the target chip is or how the package is put together, though.

The packaging posting says silicon photonics is already used for data center connectivity and is now ready to come into the short distances inside the board. That’s a distance inside a board, far shorter than rack to rack. The last line of the EPM posting’s preferred qualifications includes experience applying adjacent optical technologies such as uLED and uVCSEL to SoCs. MicroLED and microVCSEL arrays are light sources being developed for short optical links inside the data center. Short doesn’t automatically mean scale-up, though. The optical cable Credo announced in October targets up to 50 meters with this kind of micro-emitter array, and the uses the company listed were scale-out and front-end networks. So the type of light source alone can’t settle the use. Why microLED optical links are tied to short distances is something I worked through in The Dispersion Problem in microLED Optical Interconnects: Credo’s 30 Meters, MOSAIC’s Data, and the Hyperlume Patent.

There’s one fact from outside the postings too. In January 2025 Apple joined the board of the UALink Consortium. UALink is the group writing an open specification for scale-up connections that tie several AI accelerators into one body.

Phrases from Apple’s postings placed on the three optical interconnect segments of a data center

So I read Apple’s optics as pointing toward scale-up. It isn’t a confirmed fact, though, and there’s a fair amount of room to read it the other way.

The word scale-up doesn’t appear anywhere in the four postings. The UALink membership is a fact separate from the postings, and not every company on that board ships products on the spec. The single-mode fiber in the packaging posting is a part whose use isn’t decided by distance, so it could also be a scale-out link where network functions are merged into the compute chip and sent out as light straight from there. The phrase in the systems engineer posting from section 2 about systems spanning several racks reads both ways too. It could mean several racks tied into one body, or it could just mean the cluster is big.

So I think the line should be drawn like this. The postings do say that optics are integrated at the package level, close to the data center SoC. That this is a scale-up link is my judgment, made by connecting the phrase about short distances inside the board, the term optical I/O, and the consortium membership. The pieces fit together, and that’s different from confirming Apple’s product plan.

Another company drew a similar picture first. The idea of attaching optics to the compute chip package to connect chip to chip and chip to memory is best represented by Photonic Fabric, covered in Marvell Bought Celestial AI. How Far Along Is Photonic Fabric?. As that piece showed, this idea is taking a long time to move from demonstration to volume production. Apple’s packaging posting putting optical coupling design and process, BOM and high-volume production in one seat overlaps with that stretch.

6. The supply chain the postings describe

Who supplies Apple’s optical parts is a question without an answer yet. The public material I checked doesn’t name a supplier for this optics program.

On the non-optical side there are names. TSMC makes the chips, and Broadcom has been reported to take part in server chip design. For server assembly, there are reports that Foxconn runs the Houston factory. Memory is bought from outside, and which company’s memory goes into the servers and how much isn’t public. None of these seats has anything to do with optics. And some optical component companies already work with Apple on other products. Apple’s October announcement of US manufacturing partners shows Coherent supplying lasers for Face ID, Corning supplying iPhone glass, Broadcom supplying wireless components, and Amkor counting Apple among the first customers of the packaging plant it’s building in Arizona. That means a relationship is already open, and it doesn’t mean they’ve won this data center optics work.

The postings name four categories: foundries, OSATs, lasers, and optical components like fiber arrays and connectors. Below are listed companies in each category that can actually do the work. The design partner and micro-emitter link entries at the end of the list aren’t categories from the postings, and I added them. All of these are representative examples picked on public technology and manufacturing capability, and not one of them has been confirmed as doing business with Apple on this data center optics program.

  • Silicon photonics foundry: $TSM, $GFS, $TSEM. TSMC makes Apple’s main chips, the A series and M series, and has its own optical packaging platform, so taking the optics from the same place is the most natural picture. GlobalFoundries and Tower have made photonic chips for outside customers for a long time. No relationship with this optics program has been confirmed for any of the three.
  • OSAT: $ASX, $AMKR. Few places can do fiber-to-chip assembly at volume, so the category itself is narrow. Demonstration-level capability and qualification for high-volume production have to be judged separately, and a relationship with this optics program is unconfirmed.
  • Lasers: $LITE, $COHR. These are the companies usually named as the main suppliers of continuous-wave lasers for CPO. Lumentum is under an acquisition agreement that Marvell announced early this month. Whether that change affects Apple’s choice of parts is unknown.
  • Fiber and connectors: $GLW. It does both optical fiber and connectors for chip coupling. This category has many private companies and Asian suppliers.
  • Design partner: $AVGO. It’s the company reported as the Baltra partner and it has its own CPO products. Apple is looking for in-house people and also wrote joint development with suppliers into the postings. How far Broadcom’s part goes inside that isn’t public.
  • Micro-emitter links: $CRDO, plus ams OSRAM, which isn’t US-listed, and privately held Avicena. In its November earnings release ams OSRAM said its micro-emitters were used in a demonstration of Credo’s cable. Since the EPM posting lists uLED and uVCSEL as a preferred qualification, I’m keeping them in the candidate pool. Credo does the cable system, ams OSRAM the light source, and Avicena the optical link, so they do different jobs. It’s one line of preferred qualifications, so it should be weighed lightly.

Candidate companies by the categories named in the postings, with the stage of confirmation for an Apple relationship

There’s nothing to say on revenue yet. When the optical parts go in, how many, and at what price are all undisclosed, so there’s no basis for working out how much goes to which company. What the postings confirm for now stops at development scope. I still have a reason for giving these postings weight. One more company that designs its own custom chips has started talking about optics on the compute chip in mass production vocabulary, and that company is known as one of TSMC’s largest customers.

Personally, TSMC is the one I end up watching most closely. Apple is a customer that has used TSMC’s leading-edge processes early and at volume. If the same thing happens in optical packaging, volume validation of that platform moves up a step. For now that’s only a possibility.

That said, this isn’t something to end by looking only at TSMC. The place where a foundry relationship with Apple is clearest right now is TSMC, but if you set the optical devices apart and look only at those, I think GlobalFoundries or Tower have a chance too.

7. What to watch

Here’s where I come out. Apple’s capex is far smaller than the hyperscalers’, and there’s no sign yet of that number jumping. Yet the design direction of its server chips is open toward attaching light to the SoC package, and it’s hiring a team to take that work to mass production. I think that light points toward scale-up, the links that tie chip to chip.

I may have read too much into the postings, though. Postings come down, and sometimes people are hired and the project is shelved. A posting coming down doesn’t even tell you whether the seats were filled or the work was dropped. So I plan to watch for spread more than for removal. If the same title spreads into test, reliability and supply chain roles, it’s fair to say the program is really moving. If a year passes with no such postings and no related technical presentations, I’ll set aside my call, which read progress from the mass production vocabulary of the postings alone.

Apple’s next earnings report is expected in early February 2027. If capex rises again there, or data center purchase commitments grow, I have to go back to my starting point that Apple’s spending is small. When a report says Baltra has entered mass production, whether that chip’s I/O is electrical or optical is another thing to check. If light goes in from the first generation, that’s much faster than I expect, and I think the odds of that are low. Even if the M8 server story firms up into an NVLink Fusion adoption, whether that link is electrical or optical has to be checked separately. If it’s confirmed that generation goes electrical only, I have to go back to which generation this optics hiring is aimed at. I’ll also be watching whether a CPO presentation with Apple-affiliated authors, or one that hints at Apple as a customer, shows up at OFC in March, and whether PCC hardware comes up at WWDC in June.
