Abstract

On November 2, Marvell agreed to buy Lumentum, and three days later SemiAnalysis published a benchmark showing Google’s TPU can go head to head with NVIDIA’s GPU on inference token cost. I read the two separately at first and then ended up putting them side by side, and once I did, the question shifted a little. How cheap one chip is matters, but how many chips you can tie together as one body is becoming the thing that sets inference cost. And on whether you build that domain out of copper or out of light, NVIDIA and Google are standing in fairly different places right now. One thing caught on me while I was digging: an optical switch wasn’t the only thing Marvell bought. My view is that in large-model inference, where chip-to-chip communication becomes the bottleneck, whoever has run optically stitched domains first will stand in the better spot in the price fight over the next two years.

1. The Day Marvell Bought Lumentum

On November 2, Marvell announced it would acquire Lumentum in an all-stock deal at an enterprise value of $115 billion. Lumentum shareholders get 4.5 Marvell shares per share, and closing is set for the first half of 2028. The Lumentum assets the press release names as the rationale are high-performance lasers, optical transceivers, OCS (Optical Circuit Switch, a switch that reroutes the path of the light itself without converting it to electricity), and compound semiconductor manufacturing.

Most of the coverage put OCS up front, but my eye went to the lasers first. Lumentum is a company that owns its own InP (indium phosphide, the compound semiconductor that telecom lasers are made from) fabs. And in July this year it signed a six-year capacity reservation agreement with AXT, which makes InP substrates. According to AXT’s filing, AXT sets aside a minimum volume for Lumentum every year, and in return Lumentum pays a deposit of close to $90 million in two installments. It put down a deposit with a substrate company to hold six years’ worth of seats.

Here’s what went through my head when I saw this. Ah, Marvell is trying to go vertically integrated from the chip all the way to the light. And somebody is behind this.

NVIDIA and Google both get named as that somebody. NVIDIA invested $2 billion each in Marvell and Lumentum in March this year, and a Bloomberg report that Jensen Huang, CEO at the time, backed this merger was cited across several outlets. CEO Murphy also said in a CNBC interview on the day of the announcement that the reaction from NVIDIA was very good. Huang then stepped down as CEO on November 12, and CFO Colette Kress is serving as interim CEO. The board said it expects the product roadmap to continue without interruption. On the Google side there is a filing. In August Marvell disclosed a commercial agreement to develop custom chips for Google, covering inference accelerators and various controllers that attach to the TPU ecosystem, and Google received a warrant to buy about 59 million Marvell shares. Most of that warrant vests in pieces each time revenue from those custom products accumulates another $500 million.

In Marvell Buys Lumentum: What If NVIDIA Set Up the Play?, posted the day after the deal, Damnang laid out four scenarios on the assumption that NVIDIA was deeply involved in it. One of them is a picture where Google’s TPU gets a second full-stack supplier besides Broadcom. The author notes that all of it is speculation, and I don’t know who set up the board either. What can be confirmed stops here. NVIDIA put money into both Marvell and Lumentum, and Google has a contract with Marvell for chips around the TPU. Nothing has been confirmed about Google buying Lumentum components or backing this merger.

Why would two companies that compete with each other each have a line into the two companies that are about to merge? I think the answer is in the wiring between the chips, more than in the chips.

2. Why Wiring Became a Problem Only Now

Until a few years ago, wiring wasn’t something people talked about. You plugged eight accelerators into one server, connected them with copper traces on the board, and that was it. Servers were connected to each other over an ordinary network.

But as the number of chips that have to be tied into one body grew, things changed. To connect more chips to each other at the same speed, ports and switches and cables all grow together. Putting 72 chips in one rack and connecting every one to every other takes thousands of cables, and the first thing you run out of is the physical space to route them. Next comes loss. The higher the signal speed, the more copper attenuates the signal even over short distances, so at today’s speeds it’s hard to get out of a single rack. There are more wires, and each one reaches a shorter distance. At the OCP Global Summit this past October, a good share of the talks were hanging on this problem too.

There is a reason on the model side why this problem got more urgent in inference. Today’s large models don’t fit on one chip. Many use the MoE (Mixture of Experts) structure, which keeps several small neural networks that act as experts and picks among them as needed, and the longer an agent works, the bigger the KV cache, which is the equivalent of the conversation record, gets. So the model ends up split across dozens or hundreds of chips, and the split pieces have to exchange data with each other every time a token is produced.

This is where the unit called a domain comes in. A set of chips tied together with dedicated high-speed links and used as one body is what NVIDIA calls by the rack and Google calls a pod. When that communication ends inside the domain, it’s fast and cheap. The moment it leaves the domain and rides the ordinary network, latency gets added and more equipment goes in. So a bigger domain gives you more options for placing a large model and its KV cache. A bigger domain doesn’t make inference cheaper on its own, and the effect shows up in the cases where communication is the bottleneck.

Prices are already coming down fast. On its November 17 earnings call, NVIDIA said token prices have fallen nearly 60% since May. If prices drop at this pace, the side making the tokens has to cut cost by that much to hold on.

The TPU inference benchmark SemiAnalysis put out on November 5 was the starting point for this piece. It’s work that loads the same model on several chips and measures cost per token, and it’s welcome in itself that public measurements like this are piling up. SemiAnalysis compared with the actual serving configuration and software optimization included. What I want to look at next to it is the share of those results that comes from how the chips are physically stitched together, and the benchmark numbers alone can’t pull that share out. Software like TPU Sync, which Google unveiled on November 9 and which moves the KV cache between memory and the network, changes serving performance too. So this piece looks at structure in place of numbers.

3. 72 on Copper, and 1,000 on Copper Mixed with Light

NVIDIA’s current mainstay, NVL72, is just what the name says, a domain of 72 GPUs tied together with copper inside one rack. Copper is cheap, draws almost no power, and is fully proven. The reason NVIDIA worked so hard to cram 72 into one rack was to fit as many as possible within the distance copper can reach.

Google came by a different road. Look at TPU 8i, the inference TPU it announced in April this year: four chips are fully connected to each other on one board, and eight of those boards are tied with copper cables into one group. Up to here Google is on copper too. Then 36 groups are stitched together by optical links that pass through OCS to make one pod. According to Google’s technical write-up, this connects up to 1,152 chips, 1,024 of which are used for actual compute, and any chip reaches any other within seven hops. Google calls this structure Boardfly and said it cut network diameter by more than half. That is the company’s statement, and the number has not been verified externally.

TPU 8t, the training version, is bigger. One superpod is 9,600 chips, and when a link fails, OCS is said to rebuild the configuration without human hands.

On November 9 it announced two more systems. TPU 8i Max, which puts two TPU 8i chips in one package, and TPU 8tx, which handles confidential inference for enterprises and governments. AMD announced the same day that TPU 8tx uses AMD’s 5th Gen EPYC as its host CPU, and most headlines went with AMD getting its CPU into Google’s rack. AMD now competes with NVIDIA with its own racks while supplying the CPU for Google’s TPU racks. But my eye went to a different line in the same announcement. According to Google, TPU 8tx uses the same silicon as the training 8t, and for the way the chips are stitched it brought over Boardfly, which was built for inference. Google said that this brought latency down to as little as half that of 8t. The host CPU and the security features changed along with it, so it’s hard to read this as a number that measures only the wiring’s share. Still, I think it’s a case where Google showed, in a product, that a training system turns into an inference system depending on how you stitch the same chips.

It matters that Google didn’t start this yesterday. In a 2022 paper Google said it had been deploying its own OCS for close to ten years, tens of thousands of units, and it has used OCS in TPU domains for several generations now. I covered this at length in the September piece Is a Slow Mirror Fast Enough? OCS Technology Economics and Market Flow.

Concept diagram of where copper ends and light begins in NVIDIA NVL72 and Google TPU 8i

It would be a mistake to put 72 and 1,000 side by side as they are and read it as Google being more than ten times ahead. In NVL72 the 72 chips reach each other through a single switch hop, and Boardfly goes through up to seven. The performance of one chip differs too. You have to line up model size, response-time target, and the number of chips actually used before the comparison means anything.

Still, there is something to read here. The two companies cross from copper to light at different points. Google has operated OCS-stitched domains since earlier TPU generations, and in TPU 8i it designed the crossover to light to happen between groups of thirty-two chips. General availability of TPU 8i is still ahead. NVIDIA packed one rack full with copper, and is only now about to connect what’s outside it with light.

So when and how does NVIDIA get out of the rack? As it happens, NVIDIA gave part of that answer last month. Which boxes are still empty in the 576-chip domain? What did Marvell buy from Lumentum besides a switch? And when the domain grows, which component groups see their share grow?

4. NVIDIA Said It Is Putting in CPO. What About Rack to Rack?

In the next generation, Vera Rubin Ultra, NVIDIA grows the domain to 576. According to NVIDIA’s technical blog, NVL576 is a structure that ties eight racks of 72 GPUs into one NVLink domain, and the connections use copper together with direct optical links. The blog calls this a two-tier all-to-all topology.

Spelled out, it goes like this. The 72 inside a rack are tied with copper as they are today. Copper doesn’t reach from rack to rack, so that part is connected with light. At the OCP Global Summit in mid-October, NVIDIA reportedly showed the NVLink 7 CPO switch tray that handles this rack-to-rack stretch. CPO (Co-Packaged Optics) is the approach of putting the optical engine, which turns electrical signals into light, right next to the switch chip. Picture fiber coming out of the switch in each rack and crossing over to the other racks. On timing, Ian Buck is reported to have said next year.

So NVIDIA too has nailed down that outside the rack is light. Because the leader of the copper camp has set its direction, I see this announcement as a fairly big one.

But there are a lot of empty boxes. What’s public is about the GPU count, the switch tray, and the timing of next year, and power, optical engine count, and suppliers reportedly weren’t given. The rack form factor also differs from report to report. NVIDIA’s blog wrote it as an eight-rack configuration, but some articles wrote as if it goes into the next-generation Kyber rack. Kyber is the next design that puts 144 in one rack, and in July there was a report that Kyber is slipping to 2028 and that NVL576 could also be late or limited to small volume. It was a report citing SemiAnalysis’s analysis, and NVIDIA is said to have responded that its roadmap is unchanged. The CPO tray shown at OCP in October came after that. Volume production numbers and the final implementation have not been confirmed.

NVIDIA’s CPO itself is already in the field. At SC26 on November 16, Lambda announced it had put into production a cluster that connects more than 10,000 GPUs with NVIDIA’s CPO switches. But these switches are equipment for the outer network that connects domain to domain, that is, the scale-out side. NVL576 is the first time CPO goes into NVLink, the inside of the domain. And on the November 17 earnings call, NVL576 was not mentioned. Interim CEO Kress said scale-out CPO is in volume production and the CPO roadmap is on schedule, and left just one sentence on Vera Rubin Ultra, that it launches next year.

NVIDIA scale-up roadmap, company statements versus press reports by date

Here’s my judgment at this point. NVIDIA has shipped systems before that extended NVLink outside the rack over optical cables, so optical links as such are something it has done. But tying several racks into one domain with CPO switches and shipping that to customers in volume is a first with NVL576. Google has operated OCS-stitched domains for several generations. On the experience of running large optically stitched domains in volume, this is a rare stretch where NVIDIA is following Google, and I think this stretch lasts until NVL576 is released in volume, about one to two years from now. How far this experience gap carries into an inference cost gap hasn’t been measured yet, so that part is my hypothesis. I laid out in the July piece Same Split, Opposite Directions: Where NVIDIA and AMD Cut the Rack Decides Where Optics Gets Paid how the place of optical components changes depending on where NVIDIA and AMD cut the rack.

NVIDIA isn’t sitting still either. On the inference side it separately released the LPX rack, which puts 256 LPUs, brought over from Groq, in each rack. According to NVIDIA’s description, the GPUs keep handling the stage that reads the input and the attention computation, and LPX takes over the latency-sensitive computation in the process of producing tokens. The LPUs are connected to each other by their own chip-to-chip link, different from NVLink, and it scales across racks as well. It’s a design that changes the connection structure along with dividing up the compute, which is also why you shouldn’t measure NVIDIA by domain size alone.

5. Marvell Did Not Buy Only a Switch

Back to Marvell-Lumentum. On the joint call, Marvell CEO Matt Murphy said scale-up, meaning the connections inside the domain, starts to ramp next year, and that this is why the company is moving now. He also said the amount of optical components going inside the domain will be much larger than today’s network outside the domain. Lumentum CEO Michael Hurlston put it on the same call as the copper wall moving. Both are company outlooks.

In this deal, OCS is the half that’s easy to see. CEO Murphy introduced Lumentum as a key supplier of MEMS-based OCS, and in March this year the two companies announced they would show a scale-up demonstration at OFC combining Lumentum OCS with Marvell DSPs. The combined company gets to sell the switch and the signal-processing chip in one go to customers who want to grow their domains with light.

The other half is the laser. Once the domain crosses over to light, a light source goes into every link. OCS is a device that only reroutes the light path, so a network that uses OCS still needs a separate light source on the sending side, and on high-speed links with some distance, like rack to rack, the InP laser is at the center of that light source. Over very short distances other sources such as VCSELs are used too. On the call CEO Hurlston called InP manufacturing one of the scarcest and most strategically valuable manufacturing capabilities, and CEO Murphy also acknowledged that InP is among the components in short supply.

But CEO Murphy said clearly on the same call that tight supply is not the reason for acquiring now. The reason the company puts forward is that it wants to design the chip and the optics together in step with the inside of the domain crossing over to light. I accept the company’s explanation, and I still see the light-source manufacturing that comes along with it as a big effect of this deal.

The AXT agreement from Section 1 comes back here. Once the acquisition closes, the laser fabs, OCS, DSPs, and custom chips sit in one company, and the step before them, the InP substrate, is in a state where the seat is held through the six-year agreement with AXT, an outside supplier. That was a long way around, so to sum it up a little, Marvell is trying to grab in one move the switch and the light-source manufacturing it takes to build a domain out of light. I read NVIDIA putting money into Lumentum and Google signing a contract with Marvell for chips around the TPU in the same context. Whether you’re the one who said you’ll connect outside the rack with light or the one already doing it, you can’t grow the domain without a light source. Before Lumentum, Marvell had also acquired Celestial AI and bought an optical interconnect technology called Photonic Fabric, and at SC26 in November it announced it is jointly developing with Penguin Solutions equipment that lets several racks share memory using this technology. Samples are set for the second half of 2027. How much money Coherent is putting into InP capacity is something I covered in the August piece Coherent FQ4 2026: InP Capacity, CPO/NPO Content, and CapEx Return.

The word TPU did not come up once on the joint call. The names Google and NVIDIA appeared about as far as analyst questions. The company didn’t say whose scale-up optical volume it is that ramps next year, and that it comes first from Google and the custom-chip side is my inference, drawn from the NVIDIA timeline in Section 4.

The market’s first reaction was cold. On the day of the announcement the two companies’ shares reportedly fell by double digits side by side. There are real things to worry about. A company that had no fabs takes on compound semiconductor fabs, and from the point of view of Marvell’s other customers, a component supplier becomes an affiliate of a competitor. The closing target is the first half of 2028, so the long wait is a burden too. In the meantime customers may try to add laser suppliers, and then a company like Coherent picks up the benefit.

Coherent is already going after that spot. On its earnings call on November 4, two days after the acquisition announcement, CEO Jim Anderson was asked about this deal and answered that Coherent is now positioned as an independent optical supplier that can support customers without competitive conflict. On the same call he said the company doubled InP capacity over one year and will more than double it again by the end of 2027, and also that it signed a five-year, $20 billion long-term supply agreement with an unnamed AI data center customer. It covers PhotonLink, a product family for CPO and NPO. Lumentum also said, in the results it released on the same day as the acquisition announcement, that it won a second large NPO and CPO award and that it comes with purchase commitments of more than $5 billion. Contracts and commitments of this size in optical components for CPO and NPO were disclosed one after another within a single week. Neither company said how much of this is the laser’s share.

Holding seats on the substrate side is no longer just a Lumentum thing either. China’s Guangzhi Technology (光智科技) said in a November 6 filing that its subsidiary signed a letter of intent to supply 6-inch InP substrates with Coherent. It covers supplying substrates to Coherent’s plants in Switzerland and Sweden for three years starting in 2027, and the expected amount is $600 million or more. It’s at the letter-of-intent stage, so the final contract is still ahead, and the filing states that it can be cancelled if China’s export license is not obtained.

6. The Bigger the Domain, the Stronger OCS Gets

When the domain goes from hundreds to thousands, the way of connecting changes too. You can connect 72 with a single layer of switches, but connecting thousands with electrical switches means stacking layers, and if you connect the layers with optical links, the light gets turned into electricity and back into light every time it passes a switch. OCS only reroutes the light path, without that conversion. So I expect OCS’s share to grow by as much as designs that stitch the domain with OCS increase. This is the road Google took first, and what Marvell and Lumentum said they would show at OFC points the same way. OCS doesn’t automatically follow just because optical links increase. There is also the road of electrical switches with CPO attached, like NVIDIA’s.

Concept diagram of a light path through stacked electrical switches versus an optical circuit switch

What OCS does doesn’t end at saving the conversion either. In its November 9 announcement Google made a feature called GKE dynamic slicing generally available, and it’s a feature that joins and splits TPU domains in software using OCS. When a big job comes in, it joins several domains together, and when many small jobs come in, it splits them and runs them separately. Google said that with this, the time to start a job dropped to as little as one fifth and failure recovery got several times faster. Those numbers measure job placement and recovery, and in the current documentation the supported target is Ironwood, the previous generation. Inference is work where request volume swings by time of day, so how little you let the installed chips sit idle goes straight into cost. Being able to rebuild the domain is a means of pulling that utilization up.

Numbers on the demand side have started to come out too. Lumentum said in this quarter’s results that visibility into OCS demand has widened considerably in recent months and that it has reached a position where OCS could become a multi-billion-dollar annual revenue stream. Coherent said on its call that it sees the OCS market at a minimum of $4 billion in 2030, and it’s ramping production at two plants at the same time. Both are company outlooks.

This market has established players like Lumentum and Coherent, and it has companies coming up new. The one I personally keep watching is nEye. It’s a company that came out of Professor Ming Wu’s lab at Berkeley, and it puts MEMS actuators on top of a silicon photonics chip to make an OCS on a single chip. iPronics took an investment in September that NVIDIA participated in. I’ve laid out the technical differences and loss numbers for these companies in the OCS piece linked earlier, so if you’re curious, that’s the place to look.

Lately even quantum photonics companies are coming near this market. On November 10, Xanadu announced that together with HyperLight it lowered the insertion loss of an optical switch to 7mdB. That is about one third of the previous record, and the result is said to come from building thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) chips on a volume wafer line. This switch is for photonic quantum computers and is not a product aimed at data centers. But Xanadu’s CEO said this low-loss technology could bring value to the wider data center ecosystem as well. It’s a mention of possibility, so take it for that much. Whether technology built to cut loss on the quantum side can cross over into data center interconnect is worth watching.

Split by component group, it looks like this.

  • The OCS itself: Its share grows most directly when the domain grows to several racks. Right now Google-style large domains are the center of demand, and NVIDIA’s 576-chip structure is a design that goes with CPO on the switch side, so whether OCS goes in is not knowable yet.
  • Lasers and InP substrates: Whether it’s CPO or a transceiver, an optical link takes a light source, and a domain stitched with OCS also needs light sources at both ends of the link. It’s a component needed whichever camp gets ahead, and as the AXT agreement and this acquisition show, the race to hold seats is already on.
  • Copper extension: Inside the rack stays copper on NVIDIA’s roadmap too. The later the optical transition comes, the longer the life of the components that carry copper one more generation.
  • CPO for NVLink scale-up and what surrounds it: CPO on the scale-out side has already started going in, and the additional volume hangs on when NVIDIA’s rack-to-rack stretch opens. Whether the word next year holds is the key.

Google-style domains also go outside Google’s data centers next year. On October 27 Oracle and Google announced they will offer TPUs on Oracle’s cloud. It covers the eighth-generation TPUs, and volume grows over several years starting in 2027. Power, cooling, and the data center network are stated to be Oracle’s part, but the announcement doesn’t say whether the OCS wiring inside the pod is carried over and installed as is in Oracle’s data centers. If it is carried over as is, this would be a case of the structure spreading outside Google, and who procures the OCS has not been made public yet.

Here’s my sense of the odds. I put the case where NVIDIA’s 576-chip optical domain fails to run in meaningful volume in customer data centers by the end of 2027 at about 60% (a qualitative estimate). In that case only Google and the custom-chip camp actually operate large optically stitched domains, and NVIDIA responds with rack-level performance and price, and with LPX. The case where volume comes out on time is the remaining 40%. Either way, demand for optical links and the light sources that go into them grows. OCS’s share depends on how far customers adopt the approach of rebuilding the light path.

7. Dates to Watch

The nearest date, NVIDIA’s earnings on November 17, has passed. As written in Section 4, NVL576 and optical links inside the domain didn’t come up on the call, and there was no volume evidence either. So I’m leaving the 60% where it is. It was a call held just five days after the CEO changed. I won’t read the lack of a mention alone as the schedule slipping.

Next is Marvell’s earnings on December 1. It’s the first call since the acquisition announcement, so questions about the customer mix of scale-up optical volume will come. On Broadcom’s earnings on December 9, I’ll be looking at TPU volume. Google said in April that TPU 8i would be generally available within the year, but as of November 19 the product page lists all four eighth-generation systems as coming soon. On the acquisition side, shareholder approval and regulatory reviews in each country remain, and for the specific schedule we have to wait for further filings.

If NVIDIA, at its next public appearance, nails down the optical stretch of NVL576 together with volume evidence for next year, the judgment in this piece, which rests on the 60% figure and a time gap of one to two years, gets a lot weaker. Conversely, if the TPU 8i launch slides into next year, or if a measurement comes out showing that the advantage of domain size doesn’t carry into actual inference prices, I’d have to rewrite it then too. If regulatory review drags on and the acquisition drifts, the vertical integration picture in Section 5 stays just a picture for a while.

After listening to the two December calls, if anything has changed I’ll write a short follow-up.
