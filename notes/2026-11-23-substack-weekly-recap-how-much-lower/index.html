How we got here

Remember a couple weeks ago when tech was getting hammered and five sectors still finished green?

This week all eleven were down.

Semis lost 17.1%, and Bespoke had Nvidia alone accounting for about a fifth of Friday’s S&P point drop. That explains a lot of what you see on the index.

But give every S&P stock the same weight and you were still down 6.2% for the week. The index itself lost 8.6%.

Go down this chart and you can see how the typical stock in each sector did on Friday. Even in utilities and staples it was down.

Here’s something I found interesting. People took about one hike out of the path and stocks still sold off that hard.

Edward Jones had markets pricing about two to three more quarter-point hikes by the end of the week, down from three to four a week earlier. December alone was still around 90% for a quarter point on Friday afternoon.

See how close December still is to one full hike? Farther out, the bars are well below the November 13 line. So people gave up on some of the hikes later on while still paying for one pretty much right away.

In the minutes Wednesday, nearly everyone at the Fed thought rates would need to go higher if the economy played out the way they expected. They also spent time on what happens if all the money going into AI earns less than people have counted on. If you’re lending to a data center, rates are already high and the thing that’s supposed to pay back your loan just got a little harder to be sure of.

Housing is really feeling it. Pending sales were down 4.7% in October, in all four regions. Freddie Mac had the average 30-year mortgage back at 7.28% on Thursday. I bring that up because we need to think about what a builder can do with that. They can sell fewer houses, cut prices or help the buyer get a cheaper mortgage. Any of those can cost them money.

So when people talk about rotating out of tech, I’d like to know what’s happening to earnings in the place they’re going. Housing is already weak and people still expect the Fed to hike in December.

Now look at this. By Friday the VIX had gone from 19.30 to 35.89 in a week.

Look at the bottom half of that chart. For those of you that are unfamiliar, the VIX comes from what people pay for S&P options and reflects how much movement they’re expecting over the next 30 days. At 35.89 they’re paying for a lot more movement than they were a week ago.

And then look at some of those spikes. When options are pricing swings that big, a bounce can be huge, and so can the next move down. Some people tend to see thee spikes as attractive entry points as it could make peak fear or capitulation. Or the spike and then the subsequent decline below 30. Definitely not a guarantee, but those are some strategies that have worked at certain points in the past.

Bespoke had the S&P at its most oversold since August 2015 on its 50-day measure. And we went from about seven in ten stocks in the index above their own 50-day averages to closer to four in ten.

Lots of factors playing in here.

Where’d all the money go?

See the little chart in the top right? The 10-year went from 5.15% to 4.92% over the week. Good if you bought Treasuries. What if you’re the one that needs to borrow?

On a corporate bond you start with the Treasury yield for a similar maturity, then add whatever it takes to get someone to lend to that business. Compare the same days through Thursday. The 10-year was down 11 basis points and the extra yield people wanted on high-yield debt was up 29. The effective yield on those high-yield bonds actually went from 8.20% to 8.35%.

For a developer, a higher rate means the lender gets more of the cash the site is going to make. That can mean putting up more equity, accepting a worse return or holding off on the build. The bond figures can give us insight into which way the market is leaning.

Look at XLK all the way at the bottom. Down 12.49% Friday to Friday. At the top you’ve got the stuff that went down the least.

On staples, I think people are already paying a lot for the stability.

The current staples bar is well above both its five- and ten-year averages. You’re paying 23.3 times forward earnings versus 17.1 for the S&P, and FactSet had estimates for the sector’s fourth quarter down 4% from the end of September to October 29.

Put the multiple back at about 20, near its long-run average, and earnings have to grow roughly 16% just for the price to stay where it is before dividends. That’s a lot to pay for a sector people are buying to feel safer.

Healthcare’s a little different. Both funds gained about 3.5% in twelve weeks and estimates went up with them.

What changed for the AI trade

Nvidia’s quarterly revenue grew 103% year over year. Its stock still lost about 23% on the week.

On Friday, Reuters reported that the SEC’s inquiry is looking at potential accounting issues at Nvidia. Reuters couldn’t establish which accounting practices the SEC was examining.

For the AI buildout, the bigger question is how some of Nvidia’s customers get their next projects financed if Nvidia pulls back. Take SB Energy’s Ohio campus.

SB Energy says Nvidia is guaranteeing a minimum value for the first nine data center buildings, with a $105 billion cap across all nine. The protection kicks in only after the building meets its ready-for-service requirements and if the OpenAI tenant becomes insolvent or misses a payment and doesn’t make it right.

That gives lenders more confidence they can recover their money if the tenant defaults, which helps SB Energy finance construction.

Nvidia gets to decide whether to extend that protection to the remaining 3.78 gigawatts of computing capacity. If it doesn’t by the deadline, OpenAI has to find another backer rated at least A− by S&P or the equivalent by Moody’s.

OpenAI can still want all that capacity. If finding someone to back the lease takes longer, construction can start later and chip and optics suppliers can end up shipping later.

A cloud giant with cash coming in from its other businesses has more room to keep building through a tougher credit market.

For a smaller developer that needs to raise money for the next site, the terms it gets can decide when the project goes ahead.

A funded project with a delivery date gives suppliers a clearer idea of which quarter those sales can land in.

On Friday, Trump threatened to tax American companies that build AI data centers overseas. I feel like he’s trolling us at this point.

He framed it as a way to keep the investment here and compete with China.

Bessent said he hadn’t discussed the tax with Trump, so for now we’re working from the remarks.

But…let me get this straight

1 - ‘We’ want to win the ‘AI Race’

2 - We’re going to make it harder to build more data centers here

3 - We’re going to make it harder to build more data centers every where else

got it.

Getting companies to pick a US site is only part of it. Aside from all the political/community stuff, the site has to be able to draw enough power to run the equipment.

Google said it picked locations in Finland in part because there was already grid infrastructure nearby.

Here, PJM, the grid operator across much of the Mid-Atlantic and parts of the Midwest, is proposing to cut certain studies from two years to one. Those studies tell you what upgrades a project needs before it can connect.

The right side of the IEA chart puts transmission projects in advanced economies at around four to eight years. That’s what makes the tax idea tricky.

If a company drops an overseas site and its US alternative needs new power lines, it can still be years before the equipment goes in.

Sites with power already lined up can take delivery sooner.

That’s where suppliers have a better chance of booking sales on schedule.

But I just don’t get this.
