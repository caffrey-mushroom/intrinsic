SemiAnalysis Datacenter Leading Indicators: how we see the buildout before everyone else

Today we want to dive into two crucial indicators that give us strong confidence on our 2027 forecast. The first one is quarterly self-build construction starts. To accurately forecast US hyperscalers, we track the exact construction evolution of every single building. And the conclusion is simple: US hyperscalers have never launched more construction of datacenters. Each quarter, we track how many MWs start construction, and that number is going parabolic, driven in particular by two hyperscalers. The second indicator is Datacenter Leasing. We’ve talked about this a lot in prior reports, but as a quick recap: given the capital-intensity of building multi hundred MW datacenters, developers typically only spend 5-10% of project capital without a customer. They need a “pre-lease” to fund and develop the whole project. That’s typically a 15-20 year contract, with a TCV of $30-50B per GW. Check out our HPC Lease Tracker as part of the Datacenter Model to dive into actual contract economics.

As such, tracking these lease contracts is a key leading indicator, as it leads debt raises, which itself leads the actual buildout. A big reason why 2026 datacenter growth isn’t “that” high relative to 2025, is that quarterly datacenter leasing remained relatively stable between H2 2023 and H1 2025. Of course, the key reason was the Microsoft Datacenter Freeze, which we called out before anyone else in December 2024 for Datacenter model subscribers: the Redmond giant single-handedly drove the leasing market in 2023 and 2024, then paused, and others could only pick up what was left behind.

Since H2 2025, things have dramatically changed. Oracle kicked off the era of gigantic leases, after closing on ~4.5GW of datacenter IT capacity across three sites between August and October 2025. Everyone else followed, and the pool of buyers has never been broader, with Meta, AWS, Microsoft, Google, Nvidia, AMD, Anthropic and OpenAI all being in the market for GW-scale leases. There’s also a long tail of Neoclouds signing hundred-MW-scale deals.

In USD terms, this means an unprecedented amount of lease value that builders can use to raise debt. Just in 2026 year-to-date, we’ve identified over a trillion USD of lease value that was signed and closed. That trillion is supporting a gigantic datacenter buildout in 2027-28. Our new Compute, Capital & Markets Model is now tracking all the debt raises and can confirm that most of this debt has been raised.

The shape of the buildout: how to build 50GW in a year

Tracking all of these leading indicators has enabled us to forecast, before anyone else, the upcoming surge in orders for many industrial suppliers like Vertiv, ABB, Sterling Infrastructure. Check out our Industrials Model for in-depth coverage. However, the challenge is now going from orders to delivery. And resources have obviously become constrained, as power, labor, and sheer regulatory overhead are enormous burdens for developers. But the industry has promptly reacted with innovation.

The first step is to simplify datacenter designs. We show below how Google has modularized their electrical architecture, removing a significant amount of outdoors cabling that requires onsite assembly, replacing them with prefabricated electrical modules. We track all the equipment changes (including suppliers) for datacenter designs and forecast orders in our Industrials Model

Source: SemiAnalysis Industrials Model

Others have opted for more radical design changes. One example is Meta with its tent design, that we exclusively unveiled over a year ago. Another one is AWS which has materially simplified their shell design and launched a new rapid design dubbed “SAMDC”. Data halls are also increasingly prefabricated, an effort called Project Houdini at AWS. Construction timelines are now under 10 months. Hence our “LEGO Datacenters” title for the modular movement, you literally slot them in and go, and doing so allows for MEP labor (7-11 months of work) to drop up to 63%! We will have a full deep dive on the labor of the 2027 buildout coming soon.

Source: SemiAnalysis Industrials Model

In addition to hyperscale selfbuild, datacenter developers promoting prefabricated designs have also been remarkably successful. DayOne is likely the best example. With a total of ~3GW leased since their first deal in early 2023, the Singaporean company has been a rocket ship in terms of construction speed. While traditional developers have >18 months build timelines, DayOne cut this to ~9 months, which was key to signing huge deals with Firmus, OpenAI and Nvidia.

Another core issue is power, and again we think the issue is largely solved. We’ve been the first to talk about all issues on power, since March 2024 and subsequently given the grid headroom dips negative at the end of 2027. This ended up driving AI Labs and hyperscalers to build their own power plants, better known as Behind-the-meter generation, bypassing 5-10year grid interconnection timelines. And while turbine incumbents like GEV quickly faced production bottlenecks, the industry quickly reacted and we’ve since seen a surge in new OEMs and new solutions supplying the market. From fuel cells to truck engines to refurbished turbines, the power generation problem has broadly been solved.
