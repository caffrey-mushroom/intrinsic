OpenAI

One question behind all the spending concerns is whether the customers can fund the infrastructure they have agreed to use.

OpenAI is one of the biggest.

Suppliers are committing money to capacity today, expecting OpenAI’s spending to support years of business.

After the close on Fri, Reuters reported that OpenAI had reduced its latest fundraising target from $30 billion to about $20 billion.

The valuation being discussed fell from $1.4 trillion to $1.25 trillion, with the WSJ reporting roughly $11 billion of commitments so far. This private round is still being arranged.

March’s financing brought $122 billion of commitments at an $852 billion valuation, so the proposed valuation is still considerably higher.

If OpenAI raises less than planned, the question is how much its own business can cover and whether that changes its spending schedule.

Reuters reported that the company was telling investors growth accelerated after GPT-6 Astra.

September revenue was approaching a $50 billion annualized pace, compared with OpenAI’s reported pace above $20 billion exiting 2025. It was targeting $70 billion by year-end.

For the actual first two quarters, the WSJ reported revenue of $5.7 billion and $6.7 billion, alongside deeper Q2 operating losses.

We still need newer cost figures to judge how much of its expansion that growth can fund.

Look at what happened on OpenRouter after Astra arrived.

OpenAI passed 50% of combined OpenAI and Anthropic spending in the week of September 7, its first time above that level since February 2024.

Astra alone took roughly a third the following week.

That is a substantial change from OpenAI’s share of around a quarter earlier in the year, among customers using those two labs through OpenRouter.

Weel’s October 22 study gives us a look at actual switching among 4,000-plus Australian and New Zealand businesses.

In September, roughly three moved from Anthropic to OpenAI for every two going the other way.

Plenty were keeping both with 34.8% of businesses paying either lab paid both.

The more recent numbers are stronger for Anthropic though.

Village’s October 15 analysis put its share of the two labs’ OpenRouter spending at 65–71% since October 7, following Opus 5.5.

Ramp’s separate Token Spend Management sample had Anthropic at 60.5% and OpenAI at 35.1% for the week ended October 23.

A month earlier, they were at 51.3% and 44.2%.

Total spending in that Ramp sample grew 8.9% over the same period.

Ramp’s October 14 chart, covering prices through October 11, shows businesses paying less per million tokens on average. Model choice and discounts affect that average.

Customers can use more AI while paying less for each unit, so the revenue going back to the labs can grow more slowly than usage.

Amazon’s AWS expansion covers $100 billion over eight years, on top of its existing $38 billion agreement. Amazon also funded a separate $50 billion investment in OpenAI by September 30. It benefits from more AWS spending and has money invested in the customer doing that spending.

Microsoft recorded $10 billion of commercial revenue from OpenAI in the September quarter, including revenue sharing. That works out to about 11% of Microsoft’s total revenue.

OpenAI receivables rose to $7.2 billion from $6 billion at June-end. How that balance converts into cash will be worth following as the relationship grows.

NVIDIA has agreed to up to $105 billion of conditional guarantees for limited portions of PORTS lease and power obligations if specified OpenAI defaults occur. Those obligations activate in phases as leases begin and service conditions are met, with first service expected in NVIDIA fiscal 2029. The arrangement helps secure future demand for NVIDIA infrastructure while creating potential costs if OpenAI cannot meet those obligations.

If financing delays push deployments back, cloud revenue could arrive later and equipment suppliers could miss shipment forecasts while carrying the cost of capacity already built.

We may lower earnings estimates or pay a smaller multiple for them before that happens. A completed funding round and capacity arriving on schedule could ease those concerns.

When does hyperscaler spending start to slow?

I want to continually monitor this because a slowdown in spending will change where I want to be in the AI trade.

Google or Microsoft could start keeping more of the cash their businesses generate. Their equipment suppliers would have less growth coming from ever-larger budgets. The timing of that shift is worth understanding if we own both.

The latest calls still point to another big year of investment in 2027.

  • Google expects $195–205 billion of capex in 2026 and a significant increase next year.- Amazon expects about $220 billion of cash capex this year and a meaningful increase in 2027.- Microsoft expects around $170 billion for calendar 2026, including more than $50 billion in the December quarter. Spending should accelerate in January through June 2027.- Meta expects $135–145 billion this year, including finance-lease principal payments, and continued aggressive investment next year. Its numerical 2027 budget is still to come.

The accounting differs. Microsoft includes new finance leases in its capex number, while Amazon reports cash spending. Microsoft also lowered its calendar-year estimate because some third-party capacity is arriving later. That spending moved into 2027. Management still says demand exceeds supply.

Further out, the forecasts start to flatten. Goldman Sachs’s September estimates were $800 billion of hyperscaler capex in 2026, $1.2 trillion in 2027 and $1.4 trillion in 2028. Growth drops from 50% to about 17%, even though spending rises another $200 billion. A supplier can have a huge market to sell into and still face much slower growth.

The Vanguard chart below uses Bloomberg estimates from July 31, before these earnings calls. It shows $1.08 trillion in 2027, $1.19 trillion in 2028 and $1.15 trillion in 2029. That forecast has spending flattening after 2028.

Meta gives us one reason to pay attention to 2028. It wants to maximize capacity through 2026–2027, then has more flexibility to adjust server purchases to how much people use its AI products. It could slow spending if adoption disappoints, or keep investing if demand justifies it. Management has left itself that choice.

Oracle has put dates around its recovery. At its October 28 investor day, it said capex should peak in fiscal 2027–2028 and step down in fiscal 2029. It expects positive free cash flow during December 2027 through May 2028, the second half of fiscal 2028. It then targets at least $30 billion of free cash flow in fiscal 2029, which runs from June 2028 through May 2029.

Oracle expects to fund its own growth from then on. That would reduce its need to keep raising capital, which is a useful change for shareholders. The plan also uses vendor financing, customers supplying hardware and customer prepayments to reduce what Oracle has to pay upfront. Equipment suppliers could still be selling into those projects even as Oracle’s own spending falls.

The other companies are starting from different places. Microsoft already generated $18.6 billion of free cash flow in its September quarter and expects to stay positive for fiscal 2027, ending June 2027. Google had negative $9.1 billion in Q3 but was still positive $19.7 billion over the trailing year. A $5.2 billion fine payment and higher TPU inventory also weighed on that quarter. Google has not given a date for quarterly free cash flow to recover.

Amazon had negative $42.4 billion of trailing-year free cash flow. Andy Jassy has said much of its 2026 AWS investment should start being monetized in 2027–2028. That is when more of the new capacity should bring in revenue, though he has not promised free-cash-flow breakeven in either year.

Meta had negative $2.1 billion in Q3 and has not given a recovery date.

For the hyperscaler stocks, the encouraging outcome would be revenue and cash generation catching up with the buildout. Capex can keep rising and free cash flow can still improve, provided operating cash flow increases by more dollars than capex does. The stocks could respond before cash flow fully recovers if each quarter makes that outcome look more likely. How much upside that creates depends on how much recovery investors already expect. If capex is cut because customers are buying less, the revenue outlook weakens too.

Lower investment also helps cash flow sooner than earnings. The servers already bought keep being depreciated, and the data centers still cost money to run. We could see cash flow recover while those costs continue weighing on reported profit.

For our AI infra naes, the risk is investors paying for growth that the next round of budgets cannot support. A supplier can beat last year’s revenue and still disappoint the market. Faster connections, more optics per system and winning customers can help individual businesses grow through a spending plateau.

The stock reaction will depend on how those results compare with the growth the market already expects.

What could the midterms bring?

I want to think through the possible stock reactions before Tuesday. At the time of this writing Kalshi had Democrats at about 93% to control the House and 70% for the Senate.

Democrats are favored in both chambers.

A stronger Republican result would be the bigger surprise.

Getting the expected result may do less for a stock if investors have already bought in anticipation of it.

Healthcare costs are always a major election issue.

In KFF’s September 16–21 poll, 47% of registered voters called healthcare costs an extremely important campaign issue and another 35% called them very important.

For OSCR, stronger Democratic control could lift hopes for more help paying insurance premiums. That could support enrollment and the stock. A Republican surprise could pressure it if investors expect less support. Subsidies from CMS already accounted for about 90% of Oscar’s direct policy premiums in Q2. They also raised its 2026 operating-earnings guidance to $600–800 million in September. Its November 5 earnings call could quickly change how investors see the business.

CNC, MOH, HCA and THC also depend on how many people have health coverage. CNC and MOH have more Medicaid exposure, so enrollment and payments from states are important. HCA and THC benefit when patients have insurance to pay hospital bills. A stronger Democratic result could help expectations for coverage, while a Republican surprise could weigh on them. Higher medical costs or hospital wages could offset the benefit of more insured patients. New Medicaid work requirements are due to begin January 1, 2027, before the new Congress takes office. Reversing those rules would still require a change in the law.

ENPH could get a lift from a stronger Democratic result if investors expect more support for household solar. Helping homeowners afford systems would help demand for its equipment. A Republican surprise could push expectations the other way. High financing costs also make those systems harder to sell.

FSLR makes panels in the U.S., and announced tariffs on competing imports could help it compete on price. It could hold up better than ENPH on a Republican surprise if investors focus on that protection. Democratic support for more solar installations could help both.

NEE could benefit from stronger support for renewables too, but its regulated utility and gas and nuclear exposure make it less dependent on that outcome.

A Democratic Congress could help TLT if investors expect it to block additional deficit spending, bringing long-term Treasury yields down. A Republican surprise could hurt if investors expect bigger deficits or more inflation and yields rise. TLT rises when long-term Treasury yields fall and falls when they rise. Lower rates would also help small companies refinance, although a slowing economy could hurt their sales.

State and local decisions on permits and power connections can change the timing of the AI buildout. Opposition to higher bills, water use and local development comes from both parties. Texas and New York already have permitting pauses with different scopes.

Asking a developer to fund a grid upgrade or change its cooling design adds costs and can delay construction. Freezing a permit it still needs can leave the project waiting. A campus without power also changes when its customer needs equipment. NVIDIA flags this risk and shipments for suppliers such as MRVL, LITE, COHR and AAOI could move into later quarters.

Some equipment may still arrive ahead of opening under its contract. For example AAOI said in August it expected 800G and 1.6T demand to exceed capacity through mid-2027, so other customers might take available supply if one project slips.

SemiAnalysis’s model from today estimates roughly 75% of the 33.8 GW scheduled for 2027 across its top 20 U.S. states is under construction.

Its broader U.S. forecast for 2028 is 62.2 GW of net additions, mostly not yet started.

The later projects have more approvals ahead, and even existing construction may still need permission for its next power phase.

Texas accounts for close to 30% of its modeled U.S. additions in 2028. A prolonged freeze there could move the national schedule. ERCOT targets a report by December 10.

For the AI stocks, candidates who keep projects moving could support the growth outlook, whichever party they belong to.

Longer delays could push equipment and cloud revenue back and bring earnings estimates down. Higher grid costs or fewer tax breaks could reduce returns too.

A bipartisan Senate bill introduced September 30 aims to speed federal reviews while making data centers pay their associated transmission costs.

If it becomes law, that could help projects move forward.

Changes in delivery guidance would show whether those decisions are starting to affect revenue timing.

Let’s see how it all unfolds.

The healthcare companies I’m researching

Healthcare is also where I’ve been looking at AI being used for work beyond building the infrastructure.

A researcher might use it to help design a drug or a doctor might use a test to learn more about a patient’s cancer.

Another company supplies the DNA or sequencing tools needed to do the research. The companies get paid in quite different ways.

I am still early in all of this but if I end up taking a position I want to point back to something so it doesn’t feel so random.

These are very quick takes and I will include their charts in the charting section later in the article.

Recursion (RXRX) is trying to make drug development more systematic. It runs large numbers of experiments on cells, uses the results to train its models, then tests the drug ideas those models produce. It develops its own medicines and gets paid to work with larger drugmakers.

Its own drugs are still in clinical development, so the value depends heavily on what happens when they are tested in people.

Q2 revenue was $7.7 million, and partner payments can make that number jump around.

Its $200 million October share offering extended management’s projected funding runway into 2029, while diluting existing shareholders.

November 2 brings additional results from REC-4881, a drug being studied for an inherited condition that causes large numbers of intestinal polyps.

They already shared results in October. The additional detail will help investors assess the drug ahead of a larger trial targeted for the second half of 2027, subject to discussions with the FDA.

Absci (ABSI) uses AI to design antibodies, proteins that can attach to a particular target in the body. It then makes and tests those designs in the lab.

The goal is to produce medicines with the properties a drugmaker wants, with fewer rounds of trial and error. It works with partners and is also developing its own drugs, including a treatment for hair loss.

This is still an early drug-development business. Q2 revenue was only about $0.3 million against a $33.2 million net loss. After raising money in June, it had $201.1 million in cash and securities, which management expected to fund operations into the second half of 2028.

The next milestones are Q3 results on November 12 and interim hair-growth data expected in the second half of 2026. That clinical evidence should help show whether its designs can produce a treatment that works.

Schrödinger (SDGR) sells software that helps researchers predict how molecules will behave before they spend time making and testing them.

Drugmakers pay to use the software, and Schrödinger also uses it to develop its own medicines. That gives it an established software business alongside a more uncertain drug pipeline.

Q2 revenue rose about 8% to $58.9 million, but operations lost $41.5 million. Software revenue fell during a change in how customers access and pay for it, while the value of annual software contracts increased 27%. It had $418.8 million in cash and securities, including restricted cash.

November 4 results should help show how that transition is going. Software growth can help fund development, while clinical results could add value to its own drug pipeline.

Hinge Health (HNGE) helps people treat back, joint and muscle pain at home. Members use an app for guided exercises and can work with clinicians remotely.

Employers and health plans pay for access because effective treatment could reduce the need for more expensive care. AI helps with things such as measuring movement through a phone camera and supporting the care team.

Hinge is already a substantial, profitable business. Q2 revenue grew 53% to $212.8 million, with $40.4 million of GAAP operating profit. That puts it much further along financially than the drug developers in this list.

It reports November 2, giving us an update on member growth and whether the strong margins are holding up as it expands. Employers renewing coverage and more eligible members choosing treatment can help it keep growing.

Natera (NTRA) sells genetic tests used in pregnancy, cancer care and organ transplants. Its cancer-monitoring test looks for tiny traces of tumor DNA in a blood sample after treatment.

Doctors can order repeat tests to help see whether cancer may still be present or return. That makes ongoing monitoring an important part of the opportunity.

Q2 revenue grew 37.7% to $752.8 million, and its net loss narrowed to $67 million from $100.9 million. It had about $1.09 billion in cash, including restricted cash, and management expected positive cash flow for 2026.

So the business is growing quickly and moving closer to funding itself. November 5 results are the next checkpoint.

Cancer-test adoption and insurance coverage are key drivers, because more repeat orders only become a good business if Natera gets paid enough to cover the testing costs.

BillionToOne (BLLN) measures very small differences in DNA from a blood sample. Its prenatal tests help assess the risk of certain conditions during pregnancy.

Its cancer tests help doctors choose treatment and monitor how it is working. Screening gives doctors more information, and patients may still need separate testing to confirm a diagnosis.

Q2 revenue rose 64% to $109.4 million, and operating profit reached $5.5 million. The business has already moved into operating profitability, with prenatal testing providing $94.2 million of that revenue. Cancer testing is the smaller growth opportunity.

Results on November 4 should show whether the rapid growth and early profits are holding up. More doctors ordering tests and better insurance reimbursement would both help it scale.

Veracyte (VCYT) sells tests that help doctors decide what to do next. For example, its thyroid test can help clarify whether a suspicious lump is likely to be cancer, while its prostate-cancer test helps assess how aggressive the disease may be.

It uses patterns in genetic data to give doctors information that a standard examination may not provide.

Q2 revenue increased 15% to $150.3 million, with about $23 million of GAAP operating profit. Established tests already support a profitable business. Newer breast-cancer testing and a blood-based cancer-monitoring program give it more places to grow.

ER on Nov 4th. After that, new clinical evidence, product launches and insurance coverage will help determine how much those newer tests contribute.

Caris Life Sciences (CAI) analyzes tumor samples to help doctors choose treatments. It looks at the tumor’s molecular information and uses that data to identify potentially useful medicines or clinical trials.

One of its AI tools can also help identify where a cancer started when the diagnosis is unclear. It earns most of its revenue from testing, with additional opportunities to work with drug developers using its data.

Q2 revenue rose 45% to $263.7 million, while clinical cases increased 18%. Better payments per case and revenue recognized from earlier work helped produce that gap. Caris was close to net break-even, losing $0.6 million, and generated $6.4 million in free cash flow.

November 4 results should show whether that improvement is continuing. Its newly launched cancer-detection blood test adds another growth opportunity, with adoption and payment coverage still important to establish.

Butterfly Network (BFLY) makes a handheld ultrasound probe that connects to a mobile device. A clinician can use it to see inside the body without bringing in a large ultrasound cart.

Butterfly also sells software and licenses its chip technology. AI can help users capture a usable image and make measurements, which could expand where ultrasound is practical.

It has already reported Q3. Revenue grew about 52% to $32.6 million, but device revenue grew about 7%, with licensing and development work contributing much of the difference.

It lost $12.8 million and ended the quarter with $122.3 million in cash. Management expects first revenue from its home and community initiative in November and a product using its next-generation chip in early 2027.

Those launches could broaden its reach, while repeat device and software sales would make the growth easier to sustain.

Twist Bioscience (TWST) manufactures synthetic DNA for researchers. A scientist can design a DNA sequence on a computer, then pay Twist to make the physical material needed for an experiment.

It also supplies materials used in genetic sequencing and helps drugmakers find and test antibodies. More computer-designed experiments can create more work for the factory.

Revenue for the June quarter rose 23% to $118.4 million, with a $36.3 million GAAP operating loss. An August share offering raised $300 million before expenses, giving it more funding while diluting shareholders. Management targeted adjusted EBITDA break-even for the September quarter, and November 12 results will show whether it got there.

That would be a step toward profitability, though it would still leave expenses such as stock compensation and depreciation outside the measure.

Pacific Biosciences (PACB) sells machines that read DNA, along with the supplies laboratories need each time they run samples.

Its technology can read long stretches of DNA, helping researchers find changes that are harder to see with shorter reads.

AI researchers can use that data, but PacBio gets paid when customers buy machines and keep using them.

Q2 revenue was $39 million, down 1.9%, with a $44.7 million net loss. Cash and investments were about $237 million, against approximately $644 million of convertible debt on the balance sheet.

This is a turnaround with a funding challenge. November 4 results should show whether instrument sales and recurring supplies revenue are improving, and whether cash burn is falling.

New sequencing chemistry is designed to lower the cost of running samples, which could encourage customers to use their machines more.

Some risks reach several of these businesses. If drug developers struggle to raise money, their tools suppliers can feel it.

A reimbursement change can affect several testing businesses at once. Owning different names within the theme can still leave you exposed to the same problem.

AAOI - $69.05, 17.9× adjusted earnings

This is a big quarter for AAOI because there is very little time left to deliver the ramp.

Management’s latest target was about $1.1 billion of revenue for 2026. The first half produced $343 million. If Q3 lands at the midpoint of its $255–290 million guide, my calculation puts the Q4 revenue needed at roughly $484 million, up 78% sequentially.

Management has already described a business capable of that kind of jump. On the last call, the CEO said capacity could support more than $500 million in Q4 revenue. But he also said materials were limiting the 1.6T shipments AAOI could commit to. Building the lines and getting everything needed to ship from them are different hurdles.

So a decent Q3 followed by a weak Q4 guide would be a serious problem. The earnings behind that 17.9× multiple assume a much bigger, more profitable company. If the ramp slips again, we have to revisit those earnings.

This also connects to what I wrote about its funding. Raising equity to build capacity can work if the revenue follows. Shareholders get the dilution either way. A delayed ramp means they could be left with more shares and less earnings than expected.

Remember too that my base case is another ~$600 ATM this year. If they can tell us on the call that this isn’t going to happen and they can tap into something more shareholder friendly, that would be quite bullish.

Anything that can show the market that their demand is durable would be great as well aside from just verbal demand.

COHR - $210.32, 19.4×

The last earnings call gave us a path to a $3 billion-plus quarter by the end of June 2027. This week needs to keep that path believable, including how much profit comes with the growth.

In my last earnings write-up, the 6-inch wafer expansion was a big part of the profit case. Larger wafers should lower the cost per chip once production is running well. That gives Coherent a way to improve earnings beyond selling more units into a shortage. But since then I have been exposed to concerns about the amount of 6-in substrates that they have access to and the yields that they can get out of them. Yes management says their yields are good and everything, but there are reasons to believe that there is more to the story. We need margin expansion for proof.

The other part is how much of the finished optical assembly it supplies. As I covered after ECOC, providing more of the tested assembly can add revenue per system and bring Coherent further into the customer’s design. That could help it retain business as the architecture changes.

A stronger revenue outlook should come with evidence that those manufacturing improvements are helping margins. Otherwise, Coherent could sell a lot more product without delivering the profit expansion we are paying for.

FN - $327.29, 16.9×

We already discussed Fabrinet losing some NVIDIA share. The response was new programs taking over more of the growth, and this is where the timing starts to count.

Management put a hyperscaler-direct ramp in the September quarter, a merchant transceiver ramp in December, and further programs in early 2027. The report should tell us whether those first programs are arriving and whether the next ones are still on schedule.

That is a more useful test than demanding that every existing customer grow every quarter. Fabrinet needs to show that it can replace business that moves elsewhere and win the next manufacturing program.

There is also a potentially interesting margin change as optics move closer to the chip. In the Rosenblatt conference write-up, I discussed how some of that work could carry lower selling prices but better percentage margins. Revenue alone would not capture the whole benefit.

TSEM - $166.48, 27.0×

Tower’s customers have committed $1.3 billion for silicon photonics in 2027 and already paid $290 million upfront. The risk now is getting the capacity ready in time to serve it.

Management expects the full revenue benefit of the capacity added in Q4 to come through in Q2 2027. A delay would push revenue and earnings further out, which would be painful at 27×. We need the expansion and customer qualifications to stay on schedule.

What gives this some staying power is how closely Tower works with customers developing their next products.

Some of those relationships include exclusivity or supplying the entire program. Once a customer has developed and qualified a product with Tower, moving it elsewhere takes work.

Carrying those relationships into the next generation could keep the new capacity busy well beyond this ramp.

LITE - $840.70, 32.0×

Lumentum has already given us a big jump in profitability. Adjusted gross margin went from 47.9% to 50.4% last quarter. At 32×, there is a lot riding on how much further earnings can grow from here.

OCS is a big part of that. Management was tracking toward $400 million of revenue in the second half of 2026, then described a further increase in orders from its largest switch customer.

The next guide needs to show how quickly those orders turn into shipments. A strong switch ramp into 2027 would help extend the earnings growth we have already seen in lasers.

As customers move toward SiPh, Lumentum is allocating more production to the CW lasers used in those systems. Management said smaller chips and better customer yields were helping close the profitability gap with its existing products. If that continues, the next generation could bring both more revenue and attractive margins.

The reported Marvell talks could change this quickly. If an all-stock deal is announced, what LITE shareholders receive will depend on the exchange ratio and Marvell’s share price. We would have to reassess the valuation around those terms. Will be interesting to see what comes of it. I meaningfully trimmed back my LITE position in the $1040 area as I didn’t see that much upside from there on a deal. I will just watch with my small allocation left.

MTSI - $234.39, 27.6×

Holding data-center revenue at its Q4 guided level would produce roughly 50% growth next year. Management described that as its base case from annualizing the run rate. This call should give us the full FY2027 outlook.

The growth is coming from 200G-per-lane components used in 800G and 1.6T modules, with 200G photodetectors also entering volume production. Higher utilization and better yields in MACOM’s own fabs can improve margins as production grows. Management expects adjusted operating margin around 37% in Q4 and wants to cross 40% during FY2027.

At 27.6×, a weaker revenue outlook or slower margin improvement would put pressure on the earnings we are paying for.

Customers developing NPO still need drivers and TIAs, redesigned for smaller spaces and lower power. MACOM had 10–20 development programs underway, with opportunities to contribute around 2028. CW lasers could begin production in late 2027, depending on qualification.

GFS - $41.90, 16.7×

GFS has a smaller multiple, but it also has a lot of business outside the areas growing quickly. Communications and data centers were 16% of Q2 sales. Mobile was 36% and struggling.

Management expects communications and data-center revenue to grow 50–60% in 2026 and now sees roughly 30% adjusted gross margin for the full year.

Filling more of the factories spreads their costs over more sales, so earnings could recover faster than revenue.

That is where this gets interesting at 16.7×.

Continued weakness in mobile would slow that recovery, so we need to see the stronger business becoming large enough to lift overall profits.

I am hoping for some meaningful progress / customer announcements that would have the market start seeing GFS as a legitimate player in this market and be willing to value them accordingly.

VIAV - $29.92, 17.2×

VIAVI has a useful reason for customers to keep spending as optical systems get more complicated. With CPO a faulty component can become part of an expensive assembly. Testing it earlier helps avoid throwing away the work and components that went into that assembly.

Management expected revenue from this testing to accelerate in December. The guide should tell us how much is starting to come through.

A 1.6T tester can handle twice as many 800G devices. Customers can increase production with fewer additional machines.

More testing steps and more customers expanding production have to generate enough orders to keep VIAVI growing as its equipment becomes more productive.

I want to hear more about their longer term EPS growth if they have any visibility into 2028.

ADTN - $6.53, 12.5×

A lot of the appeal in ADTRAN depends on getting margins back up. The estimates behind this valuation roughly double earnings from 2026 to 2027 on only 8% revenue growth. That is a big improvement in profitability to deliver.

Last quarter went the other way. Optical revenue grew 22% YoY, while adjusted operating margin fell from 6.9% to 3.8% QoQ. Lower-margin pluggable products were selling well. Missing components were holding back the higher-margin systems.

Getting those systems shipped would help. Management said reaching a 10% operating margin requires $310–320 million of quarterly revenue and 42–43% gross margin. The Q3 revenue guide was $275–295 million. We still need both more revenue and a better mix of products to get there.

The guide also assumed the delayed customer’s recovery would come later, so an earlier return of those projects could help. But if the higher-margin business keeps slipping, the earnings recovery gets pushed out with it. At 12.5×, how much of that recovery ADTRAN can deliver is what makes the stock cheap or expensive.

AEHR - $54.89, 56.3× · Watchlist

Aehr is on my watchlist, and 56.3× puts a lot of pressure on growth continuing after this equipment rollout.

Management’s $130–150 million FY2027 revenue outlook was largely supported by existing customers.

Shipments are concentrated in fiscal Q2, so revenue will move around from quarter to quarter.

Getting to another year of growth depends on customers ordering equipment for more production programs, buying the consumables used with it, and new customers moving into production.

A big year followed by flat earnings would be a difficult outcome for a stock priced at 56.3×.
