Agenda

  • Panasonic's (TYO: 6752) Energy segment BBU (battery backup unit) product lineup and technical limitations
  • Market demand trends for in-rack BBU
  • Competitive landscape vs CATL (SHE: 300750), BYD (HKG: 1211), Samsung SDI (KRX: 006400) and LG Energy Solution (KRX: 373220)

Key Insights

  • Regarding the 800 VDC transition, c10-15% of new AI data centre deployments could adopt 800 VDC architecture by 2028, scaling to one-third by 2030 and two-thirds by 2031-32
  • Expert evaluates Panasonic Energy's guidance of tripling data centre battery net sales by FY29 as realistic. The growth strategy relies on converting unutilised manufacturing capacity at the Kansas facility from EVs to data centre batteries
  • Panasonic's claimed 80% market share in data centre power systems likely refers to facility-level UPS systems, not in-rack BBU architecture. While it will maintain a short-to-mid-term competitive advantage in US through 2028-29 due to its localised footprint, Inflation Reduction Act 45X tax credits and absence of direct Chinese competition in North America, 80% market share is unsustainable in the long term. Panasonic's market share is projected to erode to 30-40% over five years as Taiwanese and South Korean competitors such as LG Energy Solution and Samsung SDI scale up global data centre offerings

00:02:03] Could you explain the current architecture of power backup in data centres, including facility-level UPS [uninterruptible power supply], BBUs [battery backup unit], power shelves and capacitor backup units?

RS [00:02:30]: As this is growing very fast, the technology is evolving very quickly, from grid power, then you come to UPS with a generator capacity already installed. Then from UPS, you go to BBU racks, power shelves and then there are some capacitors also involved. This whole architecture is principally based on how much latency or how much delay you have in terms of powering the GPUs, where actually you need a constant power and also have to manage the surges. The UPS role is, short-to-mid-term, that if the grid failure happens, it can sustain the power supply for some good 10-15 minutes, which is time enough for the generator backup to come up in place of the grid. From that UPS onwards, the BBU racks and power shelves… Power shelves are principally which are distributing the power to the GPUs or the processors. The BBU rack is there principally which is used to be in different rooms in the data centres, which is now transferring right by the side of the GPUs as the battery safety goes on and also, since the data centres are moving from 48V architecture to 800V, which actually brings a different kind of issue.

As we go forward, the requirement is to have safer battery systems and higher voltage to reduce the amount of current flowing through the wiring and then the copper losses because cost also plays a big role here and lessening the heat generation because the cooling cost of data centres is also very high. That's how principally UPS, BBUs and the capacitors, they work. Pretty much everything is moving towards the rack these days in my understanding.

00:05:00] How do you expect AI data centre and power architectures to evolve from the current systems towards 800 VDC [800-volt direct current]? Where will the battery physically sit in the architecture?

RS [00:05:28]: There is a very high need of putting batteries right adjacent to the GPUs. It's a multimillion-dollar set-up. The key there is how to get the safest possible battery to avoid any thermal runaway. Solid-state batteries or something close to that is actually the trend. Yes, there have been a lot of claims in the market, but there are manufacturers who are coming up with solutions. In my understanding, especially Chinese are pretty close and so are a couple of manufacturers in US. The whole idea is since you are bringing these things closer to the processors, the compactness of the system and the operational bandwidth at which these batteries have to work, that has to go to the higher temperature levels. That's where non-liquid platforms or solid-state platforms become very, very helpful and even the format of the battery. Today, cylindrical batteries, which are very prevalent, are available, but tomorrow, when you bring to compactness because the space near the GPUs is obviously limited, and since the whole purpose of the system is the compute, you want to have as much space available for compute than batteries. Obviously, to support that big compute for a couple of minutes in case of a power failure or fluctuation, using these batteries or power capacitors, you need to have compactness and safety as prime factors.

With this 800V architecture, the whole idea is to reduce the wiring, which obviously takes a lot of space, and then the heat generation. The higher the voltage, you will use lesser current to draw the same amount of power, and since data centres are very prone to fluctuations, the surges actually generate a lot of heat. An 800V architecture would be very helpful because the current [sic] for power is I square R. The rate at which if you draw current, the amount of heat generation is exponential. Reducing the amount of current for the same requirement of power with the higher voltage is the trend. My understanding is that the data centre companies have figured that out rather well.
