SUMMARY

Overview

The expert, a former digital-infrastructure executive, discussed how corporate credit stress affects data center financing and project economics, alongside permitting, community opposition, power availability, cooling choices, and construction bottlenecks. The discussion highlighted the difference between having financing and being able to secure power and deliver a project on schedule.

Key Insights

Credit stress is more likely to reduce returns and constrain developers’ flexibility than to prevent projects from being financed. Suppliers and customers may demand larger deposits, guarantees, and stronger completion protections. Customer-backed contracts can help secure financing, but may give customers greater negotiating leverage. The expert described CoreWeave’s trade-off between lower-priced, longer-term contracts that support financing certainty and higher- priced, shorter-term business with less predictable revenue. A committed hyperscaler customer reduces financing and cancellation risk but does not resolve permitting, grid, or energization challenges. Financing announcements alone do not prove a project can receive power on time. Power infrastructure and schedule uncertainty are generally more consequential than noise or water-mitigation costs. For very large campuses, power availability is a central financing consideration. More scrutiny may eventually make costs and responsibilities clearer, but current processes are adding near-term uncertainty and delays.

Quantitative Insights

Credit and project economics

Corporate bond yields above roughly 12–14% may signal heightened risk. Lower loan-to-value ratios, higher interest costs, added equity, and reduced project returns may follow.

Contract pricing

CoreWeave’s longer-term contracts were described at about $20 million per megawatt, versus roughly $40–45 million in current market pricing; the expert emphasized differing contract duration and risk.

Costs for a 100 MW project

Localized noise controls: $1–5 million; suburban noise mitigation: $5–15 million; extensive urban-adjacent measures: $15–30 million. Water infrastructure: $5–10 million; water-efficient cooling: an additional $10–15 million.

Power infrastructure

Interconnection and substation work may cost $20–50 million; major transmission upgrades could reach $100–150 million. Behind-the-meter generation and batteries may add $100–200 million.

Cooling and capacity

Cooling loads were estimated at 3–6 MW for evaporative systems, 4–8 MW for hybrid adiabatic systems, and 6–12 MW for dry cooling in hot climates. A 0.05–0.10 PUE increase could reduce usable IT capacity by about 5–7 MW in a 100 MW facility.

Schedule and tax exposure

The expert estimated 3–6 months of incremental permitting risk for many projects planned for 2029, with 6–12 months possible in higher-risk cases. Losing a 6.25% equipment tax exemption on $500 million of investment could cost $30 million.
