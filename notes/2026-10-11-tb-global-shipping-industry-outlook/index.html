Expert [00:06:14]: First of all, let's take a look at the current port congestion situation. Around 5% of the global fleet's capacity is affected by port congestion or schedule delays and this is not a static figure. The major economies of many countries in the world are distributed in the northern hemisphere. Therefore, when factors such as monsoons and especially typhoons have a strong impact on the northern hemisphere, especially in July and August this year, the industry data shows that it has reached 12.6% at most. It doesn’t stay at this high level all the time; it is a peak.

In addition, there is another factor to consider. Some of them are not congestion or planned delays. They may deviate from the route. This is what we have talked about since the outbreak of the Red Sea Crisis. If the detour is converted into the total global transport capacity, more than 10% of the transport capacity may be over-deployed or not effectively circulated in the market or the turnover cycle is prolonged due to the detour. Therefore, if there is a fleet capacity of 34 million TEUs in the world now, only about 90% of them are actually working. Other ships have been consumed due to port congestion, delays and even detours in the Red Sea.

In addition, port congestion is sometimes not a well-defined matter. At present, there is no international figure specifying how many ships are in a port or how long a ship stays before it is called congestion, so the statistical definition of congestion itself is highly questionable. Currently, there are several definitions. The most common one is to draw a geofence around the port. For example, in 2021, many people were aware that port congestion in Los Angeles was serious. Then we might draw a geofence on the map to mark the boundaries of the Port of Los Angeles.

Based on AIS, I can see when the ship reaches the boundary, when it operates within the boundary and when it leaves, so I can calculate how long it stays in the port. This is a common industry method for measuring a ship's turnaround time in port or its waiting time before berthing. However, this method has a major drawback: the period before the ship enters the electronic fence is not counted. However, when port congestion becomes severe enough, the ship often slows down before entering the electronic fence or drops anchor somewhere outside the port boundary. The traditional statistical approach is often unable to accurately capture the situation I described later. It can only count the number of ships stranded in the area marked by the electronic fence or the length of their waiting time.

Second, some third-party institutions published figures. For example, a Danish company called Sea-Intelligence uses the following calibre when calculating such delays: it considers that global liner schedule reliability is about 56.4%, and that delayed ships arrive on average 6.06 days late. If calculated using this calibre, multiplying the average number of days these ships are late by the loading capacity of the ships yields a global figure of 1.7 million TEU. This is also where the 5% calibre mentioned just now comes from, meaning that 5% of global transport capacity is occupied due to port congestion. Obviously, it does not look at the electronic fence from a spatial dimension. It looks at the estimated arrival time according to plan, but actually the delay time. The more days delayed, the more it is multiplied by the loading capacity of the ship itself. This method of conversion is different from the calibre I mentioned just now.

A Singaporean company called Linerlytica published many analysis reports in July and August this year regarding port congestion in Asia. It calculates how many ships are queuing up to berth at global anchorages at a given moment. It does not accurately mark anchorages one by one, but often outlines a large area. For example, for the whole Northeast Asia, it outlines a large area. In this area, all ships that appear not to be sailing and are nearly anchored, but have not reached the productive berth of the wharf, are considered as waiting for berth. It used this method to calculate the figure, which was as high as 4.31 million TEU at the time. It said that ports across Northeast Asia were severely congested, accounting for 12.6% of the total capacity of the global fleet, which is the peak value I just mentioned calculated using this calibre.

The tricky thing now is that almost every institution in the industry publishes figures that are not compiled on the same calibre. Therefore, when you see different reports, you may feel that the trends they show are consistent, but the numbers do not match. This may be a rather big problem for the whole industry at present. Strictly speaking, whether using spatial scope statistics, precise port scope statistics, the number of ships waiting to berth in a certain area or days of delay, ultimately, if these ships really cause delays, theoretically most of them should still be counted as the same group of ships. It is just that the aggregated amounts and calibres are somewhat different, so it feels as if the calibre is a bit confusing.

Expert [00:14:40]: First of all, let us see whether this congestion will worsen or improve in the future. There is a positive factor everyone is looking forward to: the hope that the Red Sea crisis can be effectively eased. An effective easing of the Red Sea crisis means that most ships no longer need to sail around the Cape of Good Hope and can instead use the original route from the Bab el-Mandeb Strait to the Suez Canal. This route from Asia to Europe takes up a great deal of capacity. From a global perspective, more than 10% of global capacity may be consumed by the detours just mentioned. At present, at least, there may be detours involved. If normal routing is restored in the future, 6.5% of global capacity can be freed up, reducing the time for each round trip.

On the Asia-Europe route, if a detour is taken, each voyage will be 10-14 days longer. If it takes 10-14 days longer, it means that for a round trip from Asia to Europe and then back to Asia, a 10-14 day difference on each one-way leg amounts to a gap of nearly one month. However, if liner service is to be maintained, for example, if I am a shipowner and want to run a weekly liner service from Asia to Europe, then the one-month detour gap means I would have to deploy at least four additional ships on this route. For the same route, to ensure a sailing every week, I would have to deploy four additional ships. Therefore, from this perspective, if this capacity is released in the future, it could be the biggest positive factor. In the future market, over the next one to two years, if the Red Sea crisis in the Bab el-Mandeb Strait is alleviated, I think this could be the biggest positive factor.

At present, compared with the first outbreak of the Red Sea Crisis, by September 2026, we had 282 ships sailing on the route from Asia to the European Mediterranean, of which about 142 were sailing around the Cape of Good Hope and about 140 had returned to their original route through the Suez Canal. The proportion of detours accounts for about half of them, which means that it is still possible to release more than 3% of transport capacity in the future. It depends on whether people have full confidence in this route in the future. At present, Houthi armed forces have reported fewer attacks on merchant ships in the past two years. However, we must also note that even if this positive development does materialise, it does not mean that once the Red Sea crisis is resolved and all ships return to the Suez Canal, shipping capacity will be released immediately and prices will fall right away. Instead, there may be a shock before things return to normal, because if all ships use the Suez Canal, ports in Europe and Asia will see a sharp increase in the number of departures for several consecutive weeks, with more ships waiting outside the ports to unload, which will create a wave of pulse-like shocks.

We can all imagine this. For example, when a water pipe has been tightly constricted and is suddenly released or loosened, there may be a large surge of flow before it gradually settles down. In the longer term, once the Asia-Europe routes eventually reach a stable state, freight rates will fall by at least 20-40% and fuel costs are expected to fall by around 30-40%. This is the first variable that could materialise over the next 1-2 years. Second, as you all know, liner companies were rushing to place newbuilding orders over the past few years, so the next few years will see considerable pressure from the delivery of a large number of newbuildings. This pressure will be reflected in the delivery of approximately 1.7 million TEU of newbuilding capacity in 2026. By 2027, it will climb even higher, with potentially 2.8 million TEU of newbuilding capacity to be delivered. By 2028, it will reach its peak, with approximately 3.5 million TEU of newbuilding capacity to be delivered.

Global newbuilding deliveries will reach a peak. This factor is also why, going forward, people will probably think that capacity will be oversupplied in the long run and almost everyone already holds this view. Even if so much capacity is actually deployed, it does not mean that it will necessarily operate effectively. With so many ships, I doubt whether ports can accommodate them all, whether their production efficiency can keep up and whether supporting transport from ports to the hinterland can be developed in tandem. In the next few years, the improvement of port and terminal production or operational efficiency, including the commissioning of new berths, may not keep pace with the speed of vessel and capacity delivery.

Moreover, the more such large vessels are delivered, the higher the requirements for hub ports will inevitably become. Because once large vessels are delivered and there are more of them in the market, the role of pivotal hub ports on trunk routes will be magnified. When more vessels of nearly 20,000 TEU operate on these trunk lines, higher requirements will be placed on the production efficiency of these key ports. Because these major ports connect to other places, there may be more feeder vessels providing connections and we expect more cargo to be carried on trunk lines. This will objectively place higher demands on the production efficiency, including operational capability, of some key hub ports.

What we are very uncertain about now is whether ports will be able to match the pace of shipping capacity delivery in the future and whether port service capability can keep up rapidly. For shipping, this is the barrel principle. What usually causes port congestion? In fact, it is the barrel principle: the shortest board determines the bottleneck of overall system efficiency. Congestion may be caused by ships waiting outside the port, but it may also be due to insufficient berths at the port. Another possibility is that too much cargo is stacked in the port's storage yard, reducing handling efficiency and causing congestion, because when a ship arrives, the yard is still full and the ship cannot unload. It may also be that the operating efficiency of port machinery such as quay cranes is poor or even that the machinery malfunctions. It may also be the trucks in the port hinterland, especially at some ports in Western Europe and North America. In many cases, strikes by truck drivers can also cause port congestion.

Finally, another possibility is a shortage of warehouse space outside the port and a shortage in the supply of empty containers, which can also lead to port congestion. Thus congestion sometimes appears as ships waiting, but the cause is not necessarily the ships themselves. We often encounter port congestion. It may be because the surrounding warehouses are full or there are not enough empty containers, either of which can also cause port congestion. Perhaps the most visible sign is ship delays. The other links we just mentioned are not directly perceived, because much of the data is hidden in the operators' own information systems. As an industry insider, you cannot see it. What you can see is ships stuck there, but there are many reasons behind it.

Let us talk about several possible reasons. For example, every port terminal has a storage yard for goods. When the stacking density exceeds 70%, the operational efficiency of the port often drops significantly. For example, in the yard, if containers to be loaded first and containers to be loaded later are stacked in the wrong order, with the latter on top of the former, this is called overstowage. Overstowage means that when carrying out yard operations, we have to perform rehandling and lift out the containers underneath. The container rehandling rate rises exponentially with the stacking density of cargo in the yard, rather than increasing linearly.

Taking August 2026 as an example, the yard utilisation rate in Ningbo is close to 92-95%, already at a very high level. At that point, the terminal's efficiency and bottlenecks will become apparent. Therefore, especially for a port such as Singapore, once a shipping schedule is delayed, its operations will be under great pressure, because large quantities of cargo are not shipped out or need to be consumed by the hinterland. In Singapore, large quantities of cargo are unloaded from one vessel and loaded onto another, making it a typical transhipment port. When its yard occupancy rate is high, it is a disaster and you will clearly feel the operational efficiency of the Port of Singapore decline. This is also why, in 2024-25, when the Red Sea crisis caused widespread delays in ships returning from Europe to Asia, the Port of Singapore found it difficult to schedule each ship's arrival time and operation plan. The scheduling of the whole container yard became very complex, leading to some congestion at that time.

Another one is the shortage of empty containers. What kind of ports are prone to empty container shortages? That is, it has a large manufacturing sector behind it and it is a country mainly engaged in production-oriented manufacturing and exports. This is because imported laden containers are emptied after being unloaded. Only when the goods inside are taken out are there empty containers, which can then be loaded with goods and shipped out again. However, for a manufacturing country like China, if global shipping is very busy in the short term and all containerised cargo has either arrived in the US or is still stuck with truck drivers somewhere, there will be no return containers shipped back to China. Since China is mainly shipping goods outwards, it is very likely to face a shortage of empty containers. In 2022-23, China experienced a shortage of empty containers, which led to a frantic bout of empty container production at that time. However, China is also the fastest country in building new empty containers, because more than 95% of the world's container manufacturing capacity is in China, so this problem can at least be alleviated. That is to say, when there is a shortage of empty containers, China can fill the gap by frantically building containers.

Then there are trucks and chassis. This is the most uncertain factor in the US, because American truck drivers and unions are too powerful. In addition, the FMC will investigate shipping lines, looking into anti-competitive behaviour in which shipping lines use service contracts to force truck drivers and cargo owners to use their designated chassis suppliers. This is intended to maintain industry order. Objectively, however, the shortcomings of the US port chain are largely constrained by the market structure of chassis supply, namely the capacity of trailers and trucks. The railways are also under strain, as the major east-west rail corridors in the US are heavily relied upon to move cargo from the coast to the interior. Therefore, once the dwell time for rail containers in Los Angeles and Long Beach worsens, logistics across the whole of North America will grind to a halt. This is also what we consider the main bottleneck in the US.

In my view, the most influential factor is the number of concentrated vessel arrivals and berth capacity. This is the fundamental port factor and ranks first. If the port's own capacity cannot keep up and a large number of ships arrive in a concentrated manner, this is often the primary factor causing congestion. At the same time, the other factors I just mentioned are also very important. The second factor, in my view, is the utilisation of the storage yard at the port terminal itself. The third factor is whether inland collection and distribution itself is smooth. For example, whether the turnaround of barges, rail, trucks and chassis is smooth also affects whether a port is congested. The fourth factor, also commonly encountered in summer, especially in the northern hemisphere summer, is the disturbance caused by weather factors such as adverse weather. The fifth is the labour factor, which is often caused by strikes or other factors. The sixth is the production efficiency and equipment of quay cranes.
