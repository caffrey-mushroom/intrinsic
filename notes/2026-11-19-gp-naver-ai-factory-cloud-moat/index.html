SUMMARY

Overview

The expert views Naver’s AI factory as an infrastructure partnership: Brookfield is expected to finance and own project assets through a separate vehicle, while Naver Cloud operates the capacity and earns revenue from customer usage. NVIDIA is positioned as a technology and GPU supplier, not as an owner of the project vehicle.

The discussion covered financing, GPU supply, construction and commissioning, customer demand, international expansion, competition, and the outlook for Naver’s traditional cloud and AI businesses.

Key Insights

Funding is expected to be deployed in stages as project milestones are met, rather than paid to Naver all at once. The structure may reduce upfront investment pressure, but Naver’s cash-flow benefit depends on facilities becoming operational and attracting customers. NVIDIA’s leadership transition is not seen as a major near-term supply risk. The greater concerns are industry-wide GPU shortages, export controls, next-generation product delays, and the absence of explicit delivery commitments. Cluster commissioning is a major execution risk: GPUs, networking, power, and cooling must work together and undergo extensive testing. South Korea may support the initial build-out, but land, power, costs, and permitting make a full 1-gigawatt domestic expansion difficult. Overseas partnerships are likely to be important. The expert expects inference demand to be more consistent than training demand, supporting steadier infrastructure revenue. Naver’s long-term competitiveness will depend on total computing cost, service quality, and reliability—not GPU access alone. Sovereign AI can command higher prices, but bespoke delivery raises costs. Reusable platforms and software are important to protect margins. Naver’s world-model work remains early-stage and pilot-oriented; converting pilots into recurring commercial contracts is still unproven.
