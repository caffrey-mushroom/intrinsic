Bull vs. Bear Debate

Bulls see PLTR as the default “diffusion layer” for enterprise and government AI. That is the place where model intelligence gets wired into real workflows, data, permissions and actions through the Ontology, and where its forward-deployed engineers (FDEs) turn that into measurable outcomes. In that framing, the more models commoditize, the more valuable PLTR becomes. Sovereign AI makes the argument explicit: if task-specific, fine-tuned open-weight models beat frontier models at a fraction of the cost (L3Harris, McCarthy, NVIDIA’s own supply chain, the PACOM exercise), the scarce asset is the ontology-fed pipeline that keeps retraining them, not the model. Bulls point to four durable advantages:

  • two decades of deployment know-how built up inside customers
  • an Ontology that becomes the system customers act through and is hard to rip out (“once you go Palantir, you do not go back”)
  • the security accreditation and trust needed to run classified and regulated workloads
  • a government franchise that is turning into infrastructure, with Maven open to all services, TITAN in production and the Army’s 10-year, up-to-$10B enterprise agreement signed in 2025

Structural tailwinds include enterprises moving AI from pilots to production, defense modernization and re-industrialization, and frontier labs expanding the overall market for AI deployment.

This quarter gave bulls plenty. Revenue accelerated to +96% at a ~$9B run-rate, and US government accelerated for a fourth straight quarter even though it is already a ~$3.8B annualized business. US commercial passed government, NDR jumped 8 pts, billings accelerated to +100% and the Q4 guide implies record sequential growth. Bulls read the bookings slowdown as an accounting artifact of a better sales motion. Deals under 12 months aren’t annualized in TCV or counted in RPO, and government isn’t in RPO at all. Meanwhile, pilots are converting to ACVs 4–15x larger within a quarter or two, so revenue should keep running ahead of bookings.

Bears don’t dispute the execution; they dispute the price and the visibility. At ~$165, PLTR trades at ~31x EV/CY27 sales and ~50x CY27 FCF on post-print estimates, well over 2x the multiple of scaled high-growth software peers. That leaves the stock needing relentless upward revisions while comps get much harder: the total revenue comp steps from 63% in 3Q to 70% in 4Q and 85% in 1Q27. Structurally, bears worry that the value PLTR captures as the diffusion layer gets competed away. Frontier labs are building their own forward-deployed and enterprise-agent offerings, and roughly half of Anthropic’s customers already buy direct. Hyperscalers and data platforms keep building semantic layers and agent frameworks that look more like the Ontology every year. If coding agents and AI FDEs make custom workflow building cheap, PLTR’s deployment know-how becomes less scarce. Mgmt’s own line that the fine-tuned model is “a commodity item” cuts both ways.

The other risks are familiar but still real. About half of revenue is tied to government budgets, with the Dec. 11 funding deadline ahead and some Maven-adjacent work lacking multiyear funding. Top-20 customers are ~40% of revenue, and international is growing only ~25% with Europe effectively off the table. Political and reputational controversy could spill into commercial buying, and mgmt now says its delivery model is bandwidth-constrained.

The quarter gave bears hard evidence. Every forward indicator slowed at once (TCV, RDV, RPO and duration-adjusted bookings), net new US commercial logos slowed for a second straight quarter, and $10M+ deals fell. Bears argue “short duration” is the risk, not the excuse. Sub-12-month Sovereign AI contracts mean less of 2027 revenue is contracted, and pilots have yet to prove they convert into multi-year commitments. Mgmt also chose not to repeat the duration-adjusted metric it highlighted last quarter. On top of that, margins stopped expanding (OM flat q/q, GM down 120bp), mgmt pre-emptively lowered the bar for the Q4 beat, the FY raise was smaller than Q2’s, and the new CAMT cash tax will dent FCF from 2027

AAPL: Top line better but services and GM overshadow. Memory Pushes the Margin Trough Past December

Key Takeaways:

  • Margins. The 45-46% Dec-Q GM guide is ~120bps below Street at the midpoint and ~140bps below Sept-Q’s 46.9% ex-refunds. That extends a steady slide from 49.3% in March and 48.1% in June (ex-refunds). Mgmt blamed memory for essentially all of the earlier declines and most of this one, plus Siri AI ramp costs. The offsets (price increases, cheaper non-memory parts, older low-cost inventory) are partial and shrinking. What’s new is that mgmt again sees memory prices rising beyond the next quarter (last call it was beyond Sept, now beyond Dec), so the Dec-Q is unlikely to be the bottom. Assuming ~75% Services GM, the guide implies Products GM of ~37.5-38% vs. 40.7% a year ago. Sept-Q itself was clean, landing at the high end of a guide that already included ~1pt of refunds.
  • Services. Revenue of $31.6B (+9.8% y/y vs. +12.1% in June) was in line with Street, but the +8-9% Dec-Q guide is ~2-3pts below. FX explains much of the slowdown (~4pts from March to Sept, another ~0.5pt in Dec) but not all of it. Sept-Q FX was lighter than the guide assumed, so the small beat came from FX while underlying growth slowed ~0.5pt. What’s new vs. last quarter: App Store went from a June-quarter record to flat y/y (weak mobile gaming, business-model changes in some countries, the US link-out ruling), and mgmt named third-party licensing (read: search TAC) as the main Dec-Q drag. Offsets are records in video, cloud, payments and ads, and 1.5B+ paid subs growing double digits. Services GM of 76.0% now has to absorb Siri AI inference costs.
  • Supply. The bottleneck has moved from chips to memory. Advanced-node limits that capped Sept-Q are easing as more products move to 2nm. But Mac and iPad will be memory-constrained in the Dec-Q because demand ran ahead of plan, and Duo remains constrained with no visibility on when supply catches up. Mgmt called memory pricing unprecedented (last quarter it compared it to a once-in-a-century event). It pointed to a $33B q/q jump in manufacturing purchase obligations and dodged whether it is signing long-term memory contracts, while inventory rose to $14.5B (vs. $5.7B a year ago). AAPL is using its balance sheet to lock in supply, which protects units and share but also locks in higher prices.
  • AI. Siri AI is live in beta in six languages, and Siri already handles 2.5B+ requests a day. Costs come first: cloud inference goes into Services COGS, and starting in the Dec-Q, Siri AI joins the bundled services for which AAPL defers part of device revenue. Revenue comes later, through usage limits on server-side features with paid upgrades via iCloud+. That’s a more concrete plan than last quarter, when mgmt said it had no complete answer on compute costs, though neither costs nor revenue were sized. Dec-Q opex growth slows to +11-13% (from +21% in Sept-Q) but is still led by AI R&D. Capex stays light ($2.2B, -31% y/y), so AI spending shows up in COGS and opex rather than capex.
  • Agents. Asked about consumer AI agents three times, mgmt described Siri AI as request-based today but built to expand, and leaned on the iPhone’s personal data and privacy as its edge. It didn’t address what happens to App Store and search revenue if third-party agents become the main way people use their phones, which remains the biggest long-term Services risk.
  • iPhone. Revenue of $57.6B (+17.5% y/y vs. +22% in June) beat Street’s $56.6B and the mid-teens guide, despite supply limits, FX and no standard iPhone 18. Sept-Q set upgrader records worldwide, in the US, China mainland and India, and FY26 was iPhone’s best year ever in every region. Duo demand is strong but supply-limited. It’s still too early to tell how demand responded to the Sept price increases, and supply constraints are hiding the effect of June’s Mac/iPad increases too. Mgmt wouldn’t explain the missing standard iPhone 18. It flagged financing programs like Apple Upgrade as more important at higher prices. The Dec-Q compares against +16% revenue growth a year ago.

The $0.05 tariff refund was already in the guide and Street numbers. About half of the revenue beat came from lighter-than-assumed FX. Bull vs. Bear Debate

The bull view is that AAPL owns the most valuable distribution channel in consumer AI. It has 2.5B+ active devices, access to users’ personal data, custom chips built for on-device AI, and premium customers who have absorbed two rounds of price increases without visible damage to demand so far. In that view, memory inflation is a temporary cost problem on top of a lasting upgrade cycle. Growth is coming from several directions at once. New form factors (Duo, the first touch Mac, the OLED iPad mini) and AI-ready hardware are pulling forward upgrades from an aging installed base. A staggered iPhone calendar, with the standard iPhone 18 expected in the spring, spreads the cycle across more quarters. MacBook Neo is taking share from Windows and Chromebooks, especially in education and emerging markets, and China’s recovery (+28% in FY26) is built on share gains. Services has a long runway from 1.5B+ paid subs growing double digits, ads in Maps and Apple Pay in India, plus a new AI revenue stream from usage limits and paid iCloud+ tiers. Bulls also see the shortage as an advantage. AAPL’s balance sheet lets it lock up memory and leading-edge chip capacity (purchase obligations +$33B q/q) when smaller Android and PC makers can’t. And its AI setup needs little capex, with inference costs that rise with usage and can now be metered and charged for.

This quarter delivered the demand evidence bulls wanted. Revenue beat the top of the 9-11% guide despite supply limits and no standard iPhone 18. iPhone beat its mid-teens guide, Mac grew 24% with share gains and China grew 21%. The Dec-Q revenue guide sits ~2.5% above Street even though it’s capped by supply, which means demand is running ahead of shipments. Chip constraints are easing with 2nm, opex growth is slowing to +11-13% as the FY26 R&D ramp laps, and mgmt explained for the first time how it plans to charge for AI. From here, estimates and the multiple can move higher on four things. First, DRAM prices peaking in 1H CY27 as new capacity arrives (roughly every ~5% move in DRAM prices is worth ~50bps of GM). Second, Duo supply catching up and the base iPhone 18 extending the cycle. Third, paid AI tiers and easier FX comparisons pushing Services back toward double digits. Fourth, ~$135-150B of annual FCF continuing to fund buybacks and dividends near 100% of FCF.

The bear view is that AAPL no longer controls the price of its most important input, just as its multiple assumes stable margins. Memory prices are now set by hyperscalers that sign multi-year deals and will pay almost any price. Mgmt itself expects memory costs to keep rising beyond the Dec-Q while the low-cost inventory cushion runs out. Ex-refund GM has fallen ~120-140bps in each of the last three quarters. The offsets either don’t last (cheap inventory) or carry demand risk AAPL says it can’t yet measure (price increases). Meanwhile Services, the business that justifies the premium multiple, is slowing for reasons beyond FX. App Store revenue was flat y/y with weak mobile gaming. Regulation (EU business-model changes, the US link-out ruling) is cutting into AAPL’s take of app sales. And third-party licensing, mostly the Google search payment, is now the main named drag as AI assistants take search share. Add AI costs that arrive before AI revenue (inference in Services COGS, larger hardware deferrals). Add the longer-term risk that AI agents and new devices (AI glasses, AI-first devices from OpenAI and others) bypass the app layer. Bears see FY27 as a year of roughly flat EPS on a ~35x multiple.

Bears think several bull assumptions are too generous. A FY28 GM recovery assumes DRAM prices fall, but AI demand has repeatedly outrun supply, and mgmt has pushed its rising-memory-cost warning out one more quarter on two straight calls. A Services rebound assumes AI tiers make real money, but mgmt sized neither the revenue nor the cost, and only the cost is in the guide. iPhone strength may also be driven more by price (increases plus Pro/Duo mix) and channel fill than by units. The March quarter is exposed until the base model arrives, and FY27 units could fall after a record FY26. This quarter gave bears plenty to work with. The Dec-Q GM guide is ~120bps below Street and implies EPS up only ~2% y/y on +8.5% revenue. Half of the Sept-Q EPS beat came from a lower tax rate and higher OI&E. FCF missed by ~$6B as inventory swelled to $14.5B. And post-print estimate changes raised FY27 revenue but trimmed EPS. The multiple shrinks if Street FY27 EPS drifts from ~$9.55 toward ~$9.00-9.20 and investors stop paying a premium for low-single-digit EPS growth.
