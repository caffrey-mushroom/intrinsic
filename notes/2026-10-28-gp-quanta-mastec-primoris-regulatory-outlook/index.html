SUMMARY

Overview

The discussion examined natural gas pipeline and electric infrastructure demand across PJM. Demand is being supported by gas- fired generation, data centers, manufacturing reshoring, and pharmaceutical investment. Abundant regional shale supply remains available at competitive prices, but transportation capacity is increasingly constrained.

Most current pipeline spending involves compression, looping, pressure increases, and other upgrades rather than new greenfield construction. Electric utilities are similarly optimizing existing networks while preparing substantial transmission and distribution investment. Permitting uncertainty, local opposition, litigation, elevated financing costs, and changing policy remain the main barriers.

Key Insights

The expert believes pipeline demand is clearly present, as newly offered capacity is often contracted quickly. Existing pipeline optimization is nearing its practical limit, increasing the eventual need for greenfield capacity. Federal conditions currently appear supportive, but state and local resistance can still prevent projects from advancing. Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Ohio were characterized as relatively favorable toward infrastructure. Virginia and Maryland were viewed as materially more difficult. Gas is expected to remain essential for dispatchable power because renewables and storage cannot independently satisfy peak reliability needs, while new nuclear deployment faces cost and scaling challenges. Permitting reform could materially increase construction by reducing litigation risk and improving long-term policy certainty. Affordability policies that cap utility returns and expanding environmental-justice requirements could undermine infrastructure investment.
